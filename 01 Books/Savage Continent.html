Savage Continent

Buy on Amazon — Savage Continent

Detailed overview

Keith Lowe’s Savage Continent is a history of Europe between roughly 1944 and 1949, before reconstruction, the Marshall Plan, and the familiar story of postwar stabilization could fully take hold. Lowe opens with a deliberately stripped-down picture of a continent without functioning banks, shops, railways, police, courts, schools, post offices, or reliable food supply. That opening is not a dystopian flourish: it prepares the reader to understand why violence, theft, rape, revenge, expulsions, and civil war continued after VE Day. Europe’s war did not end at one moment. Sicily, France, Yugoslavia, Greece, Poland, Ukraine, and the Baltic states each entered “postwar” time differently.

The book’s first major move is to dismantle the reassuring idea of Stunde null, the German “Zero Hour.” Lowe insists that there was no clean slate. Warsaw’s destruction, the ruined housing stock of Germany and Poland, the missing millions, the displaced persons in camps, the Greek famine, the black market, and the moral corrosion left by occupation all made ordinary administration fragile. Dean Acheson’s warning about combustible liberated peoples and the New York Times description of Europe as a “New Dark Continent” give the book its governing historical problem in concrete terms: Allied victory created neither order nor legitimacy by itself.

Lowe’s second move is to place vengeance at the centre of the immediate postwar years without treating it as simple barbarism. Red Army atrocities in East Prussia, the discovery of Majdanek, Auschwitz, Ohrdruf, Dachau, Bergen-Belsen, forced labourers looting German homes, German prisoners of war in Allied and Soviet hands, reprisals in Czechoslovakia and Poland, and the humiliation of women accused of sleeping with Germans all appear as different expressions of the same pressure. Lowe repeatedly asks who benefited from vengeance. Sometimes it gave victims a sense of agency; sometimes new regimes used it to remove enemies; sometimes later nationalists inflated or suppressed figures to win political arguments.

The book’s third large concern is ethnic cleansing. Lowe connects Nazi wartime racial classification to postwar expulsion and population transfer: identity cards, Volksdeutsche categories, Jews returning to Holland or Poland, property disputes in Budapest and Amsterdam, anti-Semitic violence, the Jewish flight to Palestine, Polish-Ukrainian massacres, the expulsion of Germans from Silesia and Pomerania, and the reshaping of Yugoslavia. His point is not that postwar expulsions were equivalent to the Holocaust, but that the war normalized ethnic sorting and made governments and neighbours think in collective categories that outlived Hitler.

The final part turns from ethnic violence to political war. Lowe treats France, Italy, Greece, Romania, Hungary, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, Ukraine, Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia as parts of one fractured continental story. Local civil wars began during Nazi occupation and continued after liberation; Communist parties used resistance credentials and Soviet backing to seize power in the east; western-backed anti-Communists in Greece used arrests, island camps, torture, and militias in ways that mirrored eastern repression. The “Cold War mirror” at the end refuses a comforting east-versus-west morality, while still acknowledging that Stalinist communism was more coercive and less democratic than western capitalism.

The book is especially useful because Lowe is obsessed with numbers and myths. He challenges exaggerated claims about massacres, collaborator killings, Dresden casualties, German expulsions, and revenge deaths, but he does not use correction to minimize suffering. The result is a history that moves between local testimony and continental pattern: John Vachon walking through Warsaw’s bricks and twisted beds; Roman Halter being forced by a Soviet soldier to prove he was Jewish; Norwegian “war children” marked as German; women shaved in France; Nemmersdorf turned into propaganda; and Juozas Lukša carrying Baltic pleas to Paris and the West. Lowe’s Europe is not reborn first; it survives, punishes, sorts, displaces, and only later begins to rebuild.

Section-by-section notes

Introduction

Summary: Lowe begins by asking the reader to imagine Europe without functioning institutions: no governments, schools, libraries, newspapers, railways, motor vehicles, telephones, banks, shops, police, courts, money, or dependable food. He anchors that image in 1944 and 1945, when American observers warned of possible Europe-wide civil war and the New York Times called the continent “The New Dark Continent.” He rejects celebratory narratives of reconstruction, Stunde nul, and a clean end to war by pointing to Sicily in 1943, France in 1944, Tito’s troops fighting Germans until 15 May 1945, Greece, Yugoslavia, Poland, Ukraine, the Baltic states, the Soviet tank leaving Poland only in 1989, and Estonia and Lithuania refusing Moscow’s 2005 VE Day commemoration. The introduction then lays out the book’s parts: destruction, vengeance, ethnic cleansing, civil war, and the link between the last spasms of the Second World War and the beginning of the Cold War. Source anchors: no police force; The New Dark Continent; Stunde nul; 15 May 1945; Soviet tank; 2005 VE Day; vengeance; Cold War.

Analysis: The Introduction makes “no police force,” The New Dark Continent, and Stunde nul into the book’s initial contrast: the actual postwar condition against the later myth of rebirth. The 15 May 1945 fighting in Yugoslavia and the Soviet tank in Poland show why Lowe refuses a single European endpoint for the war. The references to 2005 VE Day and the Cold War also make memory part of the evidence; Lowe is not only describing what happened after 1945, but why different European societies still contest when liberation arrived.

Part I: The Legacy of War

Summary: Part I explains what had to be rebuilt before order was possible by moving through “Physical Destruction,” “Absence,” “Displacement,” “Famine,” “Moral Destruction,” “Hope,” and “Landscape of Chaos.” Warsaw is the opening exhibit: Karl Baedeker’s 1943 guide praised the Royal Castle, cathedral, Jesuit Church, Piłsudski Square, Saxon Palace, and Saxon Gardens, but the Ghetto uprising, the Polish Home Army rising of August 1944, Jürgen Stroop, Hitler’s razing order, and the burning of archives left 93 per cent of dwellings destroyed or unusable. Lowe then broadens to bombed Britain, Coventry, Clydebank, Caen, Germany, Poland, Yugoslavia, Ukraine, dead and missing civilians, displaced persons, returning forced labourers, famine in Greece and Holland, the black market, children orphaned by war, mines and unexploded weapons, and the strange coexistence of hope, social mobility, rationing, land reform, resistance hero stories, Tito’s “brotherhood and unity,” and Allied administrative weakness. Dean Acheson’s December 1944 warning to Harry Hopkins closes the part by presenting liberated Europeans as combustible people whom the Allies had to feed, police, repatriate, and govern before civil war spread. Source anchors: Warsaw; Karl Baedeker; 93 per cent; displaced persons; famine in Greece; brotherhood and unity; Dean Acheson; combustible material.

Analysis: Warsaw, Karl Baedeker, and 93 per cent give Part I a concrete method, and the part is a case for Collapse and Resilience because Lowe measures loss by contrasting prewar cultural description with postwar ruins of a continent that had lost its working institutions. Displaced persons and famine in Greece show that destruction was not just architectural but administrative and bodily, because roads, rations, housing, and family networks were gone together, which is collapse measured as the disappearance of the channels that make a society operate. Brotherhood and unity and Dean Acheson’s “combustible material” make the part more than a catalogue of damage; Lowe shows that idealism and anarchy grew from the same destroyed landscape, forcing the Allies to improvise government amid hunger, resentment, and armed civilians, so resilience here begins as improvisation rather than as restoration.

Part II: Vengeance

Summary: Part II begins with Red Army entry into East Prussia and the contested atrocity story of Nemmersdorf, then moves to Powayen near Königsberg, Gross Heydekrug, Metgethen, Lev Kopelev’s objections to burning German villages, and Soviet soldiers such as Gofman, Salman Kiselev, and Second Lieutenant Kratsov who framed revenge through murdered families. Lowe then turns to the camps: Majdanek near Lublin, Babi Yar, Konstantin Simonov, Ilya Ehrenburg, Treblinka, Auschwitz, Natzweiler-Struthof, Ohrdruf, Eisenhower, Patton, Nordhausen, Buchenwald, Dachau, Bergen-Belsen, Josef Kramer, and Allied disbelief turning into rage. Subsequent chapters cover forced labourers taking revenge on German civilians, German prisoners of war in American, British, French, and Soviet hands, eastern European reprisals, collaborators as “the enemy within,” women publicly shaved or stripped for relations with Germans, Norwegian war children, and the difficult purpose of vengeance as punishment, social bonding, political theatre, myth-making, and statistical distortion. Source anchors: Nemmersdorf; Majdanek; Ohrdruf; Dachau; German prisoners of war; shaved women; Norwegian war children; bogus statistics.

Analysis: Nemmersdorf, Majdanek, Ohrdruf, and Dachau let Lowe trace vengeance from rumour and propaganda to direct Allied eyewitness experience. German prisoners of war and shaved women show how quickly punishment moved from battlefield enemies to symbolic bodies, especially defeated men and sexually accused women. Norwegian war children and bogus statistics extend the damage beyond 1945: Lowe is interested in how vengeance produced later institutional discrimination and how inflated or suppressed numbers became tools in national self-defence.

Part III: Ethnic Cleansing

Summary: Part III argues that the Second World War was also a racial and ethnic war whose categories survived Germany’s defeat. “Wartime Choices” describes Nazi identity cards, Polish racial hierarchy, Reich Germans, Volksdeutsche, Ukrainians, Poles, Gypsies, Jews, mixed communities, multilingual borderlands, and the dangerous calculations people made when choosing whether to claim German ethnicity or avoid it. “The Jewish Flight” begins with Roman Halter, an eighteen-year-old Polish Jew liberated near Dresden, being stopped by a Soviet motorcyclist who demands watches, smashes a jar of preserved meat, checks whether Halter is circumcised, and pulls the trigger of an unloaded or jammed revolver. Lowe then follows returning Jews in Holland, Amsterdam’s central station, Dutch volunteers marked with V, Resistance privileges, the American Jewish Joint Distribution Committee, UNRRA, anti-Semitic taunts in Thessaloniki, Eindhoven, Garmisch and Memmingen, property fights in Budapest, the Exodus 47, Poland and Ukraine’s retaliatory village burnings, the expulsion of Germans from Silesia and Pomerania, Yugoslavia as microcosm, and the contrast between western tolerance and eastern intolerance. Source anchors: Volksdeutsche; Roman Halter; Amsterdam central station; Joint Distribution Committee; Exodus 47; Silesia; Pomerania; Yugoslavia.

Analysis: Volksdeutsche and Roman Halter show the two sides of ethnic classification, and the part is a case for Legibility because postwar sorting ran through identity cards, collective categories, and bodily inspection rather than through individual judgment. Amsterdam central station and the Joint Distribution Committee reveal that even aid could erase Jewish specificity by lumping survivors into national categories, so the same classificatory machinery that made populations governable also made them expendable. Exodus 47 gives the Jewish flight its political destination, while Silesia, Pomerania, and Yugoslavia show why Lowe treats ethnic cleansing as a continental process; population transfer was not a footnote to reconstruction but one of the ways postwar states tried to make future conflict less likely by destroying mixed communities, which is legibility turned to removal once the war had normalized thinking in collective categories.

Part IV: Civil War

Summary: Part IV follows political violence that began under occupation and continued after Germany’s defeat. “Wars Within Wars” sets up France, Italy, Slovakia, Ukraine, Yugoslavia, and Greece as places where leftist partisans, rightist militias, nationalists, royalists, fascists, Communists, and anti-Communists sometimes fought each other as intensely as they fought Germans. Lowe then examines political violence in France and Italy, the Greek Civil War, British involvement in Greece, Athens in December 1944, EDES, ELAS, EAM, the return of the monarchy, arrests, island camps, and women sent to Trikeri, Ikaria, and Makronisos. In Romania, Petru Groza, King Michael, sham elections, Communist penetration of police and ministries, and land reform show the “cuckoo in the nest” method; the subjugation of eastern Europe then expands the pattern to Hungary, Bulgaria, Poland, and Czechoslovakia. The section closes with Ukraine and the Baltic “Forest Brothers,” Lithuanian deportations in May 1948, Latvian deportations in March 1949, collectivization, Juozas Lukša’s mission to Paris, NKVD pursuit in Punia and Kazl Ruda, Adolfas Ramanauskas, Stasys Guiga, Hugo and Aksel Mõttus, August Sabbe, and the memory of Kalniškes. Source anchors: Wars Within Wars; Greek Civil War; Makronisos; Petru Groza; Forest Brothers; Juozas Lukša; collectivization; Kalniškes.

Analysis: Wars Within Wars and the Greek Civil War show Lowe’s central claim that postwar violence was often a continuation of wartime local conflicts rather than a new outbreak, and the part is a case for Crisis Governance because authority had to be improvised amid armed civilians, broken police, and contested legitimacy. Makronisos and Petru Groza create the “Cold War mirror” before the named final chapter: western-backed repression and eastern Communist takeover used different languages but often similar tools of intimidation, police control, and purges, so both halves of the continent governed crisis by sorting populations into loyal and suspect. The Forest Brothers, Juozas Lukša, collectivization, and Kalniškes give the eastern resistance chapters their long ending, where armed defeat becomes later national memory in Lithuania, Latvia, and Estonia.

Partisans or “Bandits”?

Summary: The named interlude “Partisans or ‘Bandits’?” asks how Baltic and Ukrainian anti-Soviet fighters should be understood when their records include courage against Stalinist occupation, dependence on rural support networks, assassinations of Soviet officials, attacks on land-reform beneficiaries, requisitioning from communal stores, and episodes of ethnic or political terror. Lowe uses Lithuanian, Latvian, and Estonian cases to show why Soviet authorities called such men bandits while later national memory often calls them freedom fighters. The section pays attention to women restraining killings, pregnant civilians spared by local intervention, poor farmers killed after receiving land from kulaks, and the gradual public exhaustion with a struggle that looked unwinnable after deportations, collectivization, informers, and the failure of Western aid. Source anchors: Partisans or Bandits; Lithuanian cases; poor farmers; kulaks; informers; Western aid; freedom fighters; Soviet authorities.

Analysis: Partisans or Bandits is Lowe’s safeguard against simple heroic memory. Lithuanian cases, poor farmers, and kulaks show that the partisan war crossed class lines as well as national ones, and that anti-Soviet resistance could injure the same rural communities on which it depended. Informers, Western aid, freedom fighters, and Soviet authorities explain why the label remains unstable: betrayal and exhaustion helped defeat the partisans militarily, but Soviet brutality later made their cause central to Baltic independence memory.

Conclusion

Summary: The Conclusion returns to the book’s opening refusal of a clean postwar rebirth. Lowe gathers the ruins of Warsaw, displaced persons, famine, vengeance, liberated camps, German POWs, collaborators, shaved women, war children, returning Jews, ethnic cleansing, expulsions, Yugoslavia, Greece, Romania, eastern Europe, the Forest Brothers, and the Cold War into one chronology of delayed endings. He emphasizes that Europe’s eventual recovery was real but not immediate, and that reconstruction began amid punishment, hunger, population transfer, political manipulation, and local civil conflict. The final lesson is methodological as well as moral: myths of resistance, national victimhood, exaggerated massacre figures, suppressed atrocities, and Cold War simplifications must be tested against local evidence and responsible statistics. Source anchors: delayed endings; ruins of Warsaw; German POWs; returning Jews; population transfer; Forest Brothers; Cold War; responsible statistics.

Analysis: Delayed endings, ruins of Warsaw, and German POWs bring the book back to its basic chronology: 1945 was a transition into disorder before it was a transition into peace. Returning Jews and population transfer show why Lowe’s recovery story cannot be separated from exclusion and ethnic sorting. Forest Brothers, Cold War, and responsible statistics also state the book’s standard for historical judgment: postwar Europe must be remembered without sentimental unity myths, but also without inflated numbers that turn suffering into propaganda.
