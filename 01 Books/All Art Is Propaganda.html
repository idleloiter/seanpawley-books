All Art Is Propaganda

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer. Its strongest vault role is Orwell's demonstration that style, class perception, and political judgment form a single critical practice rather than separate literary and ideological domains.

Detailed overview

The extracted file presents All Art Is Propaganda as a 2008 Mariner Books anthology of George Orwell's criticism, edited by George Packer with an introduction by Keith Gessen and drawing on Peter Davison's edition of the complete works. The table of contents ranges from "Charles Dickens" and "Inside the Whale" to "Politics and the English Language," "Lear, Tolstoy and the Fool," and "Reflections on Gandhi," so the book is less a single argument than a map of Orwell's critical habits across literature, politics, journalism, and popular culture. Packer's foreword frames Orwell first and foremost as an essayist rather than merely the author of 1984 or Animal Farm, stressing the mix of journalism, polemic, review-writing, autobiography, and close reading that made the essay his natural form.

What the anthology wants to preserve is not just Orwell's opinions but the way his prose thinks. Packer places him in an English line running from Samuel Johnson and Charles Lamb to William Hazlitt, then emphasizes the stylistic traits that recur across the collection: flat declarative openings, a refusal of euphemism, a journalist's appetite for the memorable "lede," and a steady movement from a specific object to a larger moral question. The anthology title comes from Orwell's insistence that every writer has a "message" whether acknowledged or not, and that criticism becomes honest only when it admits the political and moral freight embedded in style, characterization, and genre. That premise is visible even in the contents page, where essays on Donald McGill postcards, boys' weeklies, T. S. Eliot, Salvador Dali, Gulliver, and demotic speech sit together without any embarrassment about crossing the boundary between high art and mass print.

The visible six-part structure in the extracted text belongs to Orwell's essay on Dickens, and it functions as a good miniature of the whole anthology. Orwell starts by asking why Dickens can be claimed by Chesterton, by the Marxist T. A. Jackson, by conservatives, and even negatively by Lenin's widow Nadezhda Krupskaya, who disliked the "middle-class sentimentality" of The Cricket on the Hearth. From there he works through Dickens's social location in the small urban bourgeoisie, his terror of poverty, his sentimental but not egalitarian relation to the labouring poor, his limited understanding of work and machinery, and his extraordinary gift for vivid, unnecessary detail. The Dickens essay therefore becomes a running demonstration of Orwell's core method: he refuses pious canonization, keeps the political question in view, and still lingers on sentences, comic objects, and the pressure of memory.

The book matters because Orwell never allows literary criticism to detach itself from institutions, class markers, or propaganda, but he also refuses to collapse literature into party line. In the Dickens essay he can say that Dickens has no workable economics, no clear revolutionary program, and no remedy beyond "Behave decently," while still insisting that this moral impatience made him a permanently subversive writer. Packer and Gessen both underscore that double movement as Orwell's signature. Spain, press deception, journalism, childhood reading, public-school mythology, imperial politics, and the fate of plain language all become sites where Orwell asks the same question: what kinds of sentences make it easier to see what is in front of us, and what kinds make lying feel natural? All Art Is Propaganda turns language and ideology into the organizing principle of the collection.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Language and Ideology: Orwell's critical method treats style, euphemism, class vocabulary, and literary form as places where politics becomes visible.
  • Moral Economy: the Dickens essay returns to decency, cruelty, poverty, and class shame as moral pressures that matter even when they do not become a systematic political program.
  • Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy: Orwell reads public style, popular print, and literary reputation as fields where cultural authority is made and contested.
  • National Identity: the anthology repeatedly asks how Englishness, anti-imperial judgment, public-school mythology, and class perception shape political imagination.
  • Technological Change: Orwell's account of Dickens's blind spots around work, machinery, and industrial process clarifies what older literary forms can fail to see.
  • Knowledge Preservation: the anthology preserves Orwell's essay form as a way of carrying moral judgment through criticism, journalism, review, and polemic.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

1

Summary: Orwell opens the Dickens essay by noting that Dickens has been repeatedly "stolen": G. K. Chesterton folds him into Catholic medievalism, the Marxist T. A. Jackson tries to make him "almost" revolutionary, and Nadezhda Krupskaya reports Lenin recoiling from The Cricket on the Hearth as intolerable middle-class sentimentality. Orwell argues that Dickens is neither a proletarian novelist nor a revolutionary programmatist, because his central world is the London commercial bourgeoisie of lawyers, clerks, tradesmen, innkeepers, servants, and small craftsmen rather than factory workers or farm labourers. He moves through Oliver Twist, Hard Times, Bleak House, Little Dorrit, Our Mutual Friend, and figures like Stephen Blackpool, the Plornishes, Bounderby, Gradgrind, and the Good Rich Man to establish the shape of Dickens's dissent. The result is a morally furious writer who attacks institutions with enormous energy but rarely names a structural remedy beyond personal decency and a hatred of cruelty. Source anchors: Chesterton; T. A. Jackson; Krupskaya; The Cricket on the Hearth; Stephen Blackpool; Good Rich Man.

Analysis: Chesterton, T. A. Jackson, and Krupskaya matter because Orwell uses their competing appropriations to clear a space for his own reading before he touches the novels themselves. Stephen Blackpool and the Good Rich Man then become proof that Dickens's social imagination is morally vivid but politically thin: he can feel industrial suffering and invent benevolent merchants, yet he cannot make the working class or socialism into the center of his fiction. By pairing The Cricket on the Hearth with Hard Times and Little Dorrit, Orwell shows ideological annexation at work: Dickens's rebellious moral temperature attracts rival camps precisely because it resists coherent doctrine.

2

Summary: Orwell next roots Dickens in the small urban bourgeoisie by recounting the clerkly government background of Dickens's father, the army and navy links on his mother's side, and the decisive fact that from the age of nine he grew up in London amid commercial life and struggling poverty. He compares that location to H. G. Wells and, more distantly, Arnold Bennett, then tracks what such a vantage point does to Dickens's likes and dislikes: hostility to kings, landowners, priests, bureaucrats, soldiers, and archaic authority, but limited understanding of peasants, industry, and the governing class as functional systems. The satiric targets here are Mayfair hangers-on, petty aristocratic offshoots, bureaucratic types in the Circumlocution Office, and military or official figures whom Dickens can make ridiculous but not anatomize with the social subtlety of Thackeray. Orwell also pauses over Dickens's unusual freedom from vulgar English nationalism, contrasting him with Thackeray's Waterloo obsession and noting Dickens's comparative lack of anti-French boasting, anti-Jewish joking, or imperial chest-thumping. Source anchors: H. G. Wells; Arnold Bennett; Circumlocution Office; Thackeray; Waterloo; anti-Jewish.

Analysis: H. G. Wells and Arnold Bennett give Orwell a way to specify Dickens's class angle without pretending that all bourgeois writers look the same. The Circumlocution Office and Thackeray show the limit of Dickens's satire: he can make bureaucracy grotesque, but because he lacks Thackeray's intimacy with that world he often stops at caricature. Waterloo and the absence of anti-Jewish sneering matter for a different reason, since Orwell uses them to show that Dickens's lower-middle-class puritanism restrains normal English nationalist identity even while it narrows his understanding of power.

3

Summary: Section three turns from class origin to Dickens's relation to poverty, labour, crime, and respectability. Orwell says Dickens grew up near enough to poverty to fear it, then shows that fear at work in disgusted descriptions of London slums, in the handling of convicts like Magwitch in Great Expectations, and in the autobiographical memory of the blacking factory where young Dickens insisted on a space between himself and the other boys who called him "the young gentleman." Orwell contrasts Dickens's sincere affection for families like the Peggottys and the Plornishes with his inability to imagine them as full social equals, and he lingers over speech as a class marker when Pip, Biddy, Sissy Jupe, and other sympathetic figures are protected from broad working-class accents. The section then widens into the class-sex problem through Steerforth and Emily, Eugene Wrayburn and Lizzie Hexam, Uriah Heep and Agnes Wickfield, Sam Weller, Mark Tapley, domestic servants, and the feudal dream of loyal retainers. Source anchors: Magwitch; blacking factory; young gentleman; Peggottys; Uriah Heep; Sam Weller.

Analysis: Magwitch and the blacking factory are the hinges of the whole section because Orwell reads both as evidence that Dickens never escaped the shame of social descent even when he sided with the poor against the rich. The phrase "young gentleman" captures the point exactly: Orwell listens for the class reflex inside Dickens's self-description, not merely summarizing the memoir. Uriah Heep and Sam Weller then let him show the two poles of Dickens's servant imagination, from erotic disgust at a socially "low" suitor to sentimental affection for feudal loyalty, and both poles reveal how Dickens's moral economy sides against cruelty without accepting full social equality.

4

Summary: Orwell then attacks Dickens's limitations as a novelist of work, process, and technology. Dickens is a Cockney, Orwell says, which means London appears as a city of consumers, inn signs, brass door-knockers, stage-coach interiors, coffee-rooms, painted jugs, faces, clothes, and food rather than a place understood from the side of labour or production. That is why Pip's business in Egypt remains vague, Clennam's commercial life in China barely exists, Gradgrind's factories are sketched impressionistically, and even legal machinery is less precisely handled than in Trollope. Orwell connects this weakness to Dickens's sprawling plots and to a larger indifference toward machinery, railways, sport, agriculture, and technical work, then drives the point home by contrasting stage-coach enthusiasm with the near absence of railway wonder, and by mocking the Victorian fantasy of genteel idleness at the end of Nicholas Nickleby, Martin Chuzzlewit, and other novels. Source anchors: Pip in Egypt; Clennam in China; Gradgrind's factories; Trollope; railway; Nicholas Nickleby.

Analysis: Pip in Egypt, Clennam in China, and Gradgrind's factories are not random examples; Orwell chooses them because they sit exactly where an engaged novelist of business or industry would have to say something concrete and Dickens repeatedly retreats into fog. Trollope and the railway sharpen the contrast by showing what Dickens does not care to know: procedures, systems, transport, finance, and work as a craft rather than a backdrop. Nicholas Nickleby matters because its domestic ending exposes a failed account of industrial and technical change, replacing vocation or technical interest with inherited money, family coziness, and permanent withdrawal from serious labour.

5

Summary: Section five supplies the title principle of the anthology by declaring that all art is propaganda, though not all propaganda is art. Orwell asks why Dickens remains unforgettable despite cast-iron plots, false pathos, bad architecture, and sentimental longueurs, and answers by appealing to his overabundant invention and his command of the unnecessary detail. He illustrates the point with Jack Hopkins's story at Bob Sawyer's party about the child who swallows a necklace, where the phrase "baked shoulder of mutton, and potatoes under it" lodges in the memory for no narrative reason at all. From there Orwell ranges across Sam Weller's long stories in Pickwick, Mrs. Harris, Mrs. Leo Hunter's "Expiring Frog," Boffin's miser books, Magwitch's terrifying speech in the churchyard, Mr. Murdstone's "double-Gloucester cheeses," and the static immortality of Squeers, Micawber, Mrs. Gamp, Mrs. Jellyby, Joe Gargery, and other Dickens creatures. He concludes that Dickens survives not because his novels are well-built wholes but because his gargoyles, comic language, and monomaniac figures keep breaking through the melodrama. Source anchors: all art is propaganda; baked shoulder of mutton; Sam Weller; Mrs. Harris; Magwitch; double-Gloucester cheeses.

Analysis: The phrase "all art is propaganda" governs the section because Orwell refuses the easy split between politics and style: the politics live in what Dickens notices, repeats, and makes unforgettable. "Baked shoulder of mutton," Sam Weller, and double-Gloucester cheeses are Orwell's best evidence, since each detail exceeds plot utility and thereby demonstrates that Dickens's deepest gift is verbal and imagistic surplus rather than formal design. Mrs. Harris and Magwitch show the risk that comes with that gift: Dickens can create beings and speeches that survive forever, but those inventions can distort tone, damage consistency, and overwhelm the novel that contains them. Orwell's point makes propaganda a property of attention and style, not merely overt doctrine.

6

Summary: In the final section Orwell explains Dickens's permanence by returning to his moral temperament. Dickens could not remain only the author of Pickwick because he always felt obliged to preach, to resist authority, and to side instinctively with the underdog even when he had no practical political program. Orwell distinguishes the moralist from the politician through examples drawn from David Copperfield, Barnaby Rudge, A Tale of Two Cities, and the treatment of figures like Micawber, Dora, Agnes, and the persecuted Catholics. He then widens the claim into cultural history, arguing that Dickens articulates a common Western decency visible in juries that favour poor victims over rich motorists, in popular suspicion of bullies, and in the enduring idea of human brotherhood despite class society. The essay closes with Orwell's own portrait of Dickens: a bearded man of about forty, laughing with anger but without malice, a nineteenth-century liberal whose open combativeness makes him hateful to every little orthodoxy contending for the soul. Source anchors: Pickwick; Barnaby Rudge; A Tale of Two Cities; Micawber; human brotherhood; nineteenth-century liberal.

Analysis: Pickwick, Micawber, and Barnaby Rudge allow Orwell to draw the line between comic invention and moral allegiance: Dickens may stumble when he turns respectable, but he recovers whenever persecution, pomposity, or bullying reappears. A Tale of Two Cities and the phrase human brotherhood show what Orwell thinks is still alive beneath Dickens's vagueness, namely a durable emotional code that sides against cruelty before it computes policy. The final image of the nineteenth-century liberal matters because Orwell is not merely praising Dickens's kindness; he identifies a specific public style that combines combativeness and generosity against the little orthodoxies contending for cultural authority.
