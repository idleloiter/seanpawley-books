Detailed overview

Mosquitoes is built as a satirical enclosure: Mrs. Maurier gathers artists, talkers, dependents, and accidental guests on the yacht Nausikaa, then Lake Pontchartrain and the insects strip away the party's pretensions. The novel begins in New Orleans with Mr. Talliaferro visiting Gordon's studio, talking about sex as if confession itself were sophistication, and failing even to carry a bottle of milk without humiliation. From that opening, Faulkner frames art talk, desire, class performance, and bodily discomfort as inseparable.

The yacht party gives the novel its pressure chamber. Mrs. Maurier wants a tasteful excursion populated by creative people, but Patricia brings Jenny Steinbauer and Pete without knowing them, Josh arrives with a carpenter's saw, Mark Frost performs poetic inertia, Dorothy Jameson measures everyone through artistic self-consciousness, Dawson Fairchild turns every exchange into anecdote or theory, and Julius Kauffman punctures nearly every theory with dry contempt. The Nausikaa carries not a plot of noble development but a set of people who cannot stop converting hunger, lust, boredom, class resentment, and embarrassment into talk about Art.

The grounding of the yacht is essential because it makes the conversation harder to escape while also exposing who can act. The captain, helmsman, and deckhand lose control of the boat after the storm; Mrs. Maurier is reduced to panic over food and propriety; the guests fall into breakfast improvisations, bathing mishaps, gossip, and arguments about poetry, geography, women, and genius. David West, the steward, and Patricia, Mrs. Maurier's niece, become the clearest counterforce to this enclosure because their impulses lead off the boat and into swamp, road, mosquitoes, hunger, and a failed attempt at freedom.

Faulkner repeatedly contrasts verbal sophistication with bodily fact. Fairchild, Eva Wiseman, Julius, Mark Frost, Major Ayers, and Dorothy Jameson argue about American literature, modern verse, Chopin, Debussy, biology, sex, and artistic consistency while Jenny's body draws attention without any theory at all and Pete remains resistant to being converted into the artists' material. Mosquitoes, nausea, heat, lake water, grapefruit, whisky, greasy galley work, and bad hats keep puncturing the language of art and romance.

The epilogue returns everyone to New Orleans without solving what the trip exposed. Jenny goes home by streetcar in her ruined green dress, Pete returns to the Ginotta family's Americanized restaurant and delivery business, Fairchild and Gordon fall back into night talk and writing, and Mr. Talliaferro turns his humiliation with Jenny into another absurd plan for masculine dominance. The novel's motion is therefore circular but not empty: the boat trip fails as adventure, but it reveals the habits each character will carry back into the city.

Section-by-section notes

Prologue

Summary: The prologue follows Mr. Talliaferro in Gordon's New Orleans studio, where he talks about the sex instinct, Conversation, frankness, and artistic intimacy while Gordon works with chisel and maul on a marble torso. The Vieux Carre, Jackson Square, Pontalba, the cathedral, Royal Street, a grocer, a milk bottle, Mrs. Maurier, and Patricia all become part of Talliaferro's small humiliations before the yacht trip begins. Mrs. Maurier tries to recruit Gordon for her party, Patricia exposes Talliaferro's hidden bottle of milk, and the prologue establishes Gordon's physical directness against Talliaferro's theatrical delicacy. Source anchors: Mr. Talliaferro, Gordon, marble torso, Vieux Carre, milk bottle, Mrs. Maurier, Patricia.

Analysis: Mr. Talliaferro treats confession as erotic capital, but Gordon and the marble torso expose how little contact his talk has with making, desire, or bodies. The Vieux Carre and milk bottle turn his cultivated persona into street comedy, because he cannot pass through ordinary errands without panic about appearance. Mrs. Maurier and Patricia then draw him toward the Nausikaa world where his theories of women will be tested by people who do not follow his scripts.

The First Day

Summary: The First Day assembles Mrs. Maurier's yacht party aboard the Nausikaa, with the yacht club basin, mahogany-and-brass fittings, almond cream, sunburn lotion, and nautical cries setting up a parody of cultivated leisure. Mrs. Maurier brings Fairchild, Gordon, Julius, Major Ayers, Mark Frost, Mrs. Wiseman, Dorothy Jameson, Mr. Talliaferro, Josh, and Patricia, while Patricia unexpectedly adds Jenny Steinbauer and Pete from downtown. Lunch, bridge plans, Fairchild's jokes about Major Ayers and Andrew Jackson, Julius's interruptions, Pete's discomfort, Jenny's placidity, and the men's retreat below deck immediately undermine Mrs. Maurier's idea of a managed social-artistic excursion. Source anchors: Nausikaa, Mrs. Maurier, Patricia, Jenny Steinbauer, Pete, Fairchild, Major Ayers.

Analysis: The Nausikaa gives Mrs. Maurier the illusion that she can curate people as aesthetic objects. The guest list becomes Elite Formation through invitations, artistic reputation, patronage, and performance rather than inherited office. Patricia's addition of Jenny Steinbauer and Pete breaks that boundary, while Fairchild and Major Ayers turn lunch into competitive anecdote. Mrs. Maurier's attempts to restore arrangement expose how fragile the salon hierarchy becomes once outsiders enter the boat.

The Second Day

Summary: The Second Day begins after a storm leaves the Nausikaa dragging anchors and sitting aground in shoaling water near trees and beach. Dorothy Jameson reflects on her Greenwich Village past, failed lovers, Mark Frost, Eva Wiseman, Patricia, Gordon, Pete, and Jenny, trying to understand why women uninterested in art attract men more easily than she does. Josh works on his handmade pipe, Patricia talks with Pete and then with David West, and Fairchild tells Josh about college poverty, the power house, the false fraternity initiation, and waiting behind the library in snow. The grounded yacht becomes a platform for long conversations about architecture, work, art, sex, American writing, Chopin, Debussy, women, modern poetry, and the difference between life and books. Source anchors: Nausikaa, Dorothy Jameson, Greenwich Village, Mark Frost, David West, Fairchild, power house.

Analysis: The grounded Nausikaa converts purchased leisure into delay. Dorothy Jameson's Greenwich Village memories and Mark Frost's talk expose Commercial Society inside bohemian identity, where reputation, patrons, publication, sex, and class all shape who can claim artistic freedom. David West gives Patricia a practical life outside the guests' talk, while Fairchild's power-house story reveals the labor and class wound beneath his comic abundance.

The Third Day

Summary: The Third Day opens in heavy mist as Patricia swims naked before dawn, makes David West watch her without quite knowing what to do with his longing, and then organizes their escape from the yacht. She steals bacon, bread, oranges, stockings, money, and a basket, leaves a note saying they are going to Mandeville, and rows ashore with David before the others wake. Their excursion through trees, swamp, road, mosquitoes, hunger, and filthy ditch water quickly turns romantic escape into bodily misery, while back aboard the remaining guests improvise lunch, argue over grapefruit, books, biology, art, Dorothy's tastes, Mark Frost's poetry, and Fairchild's theories of words. Source anchors: Patricia, David West, Mandeville, mosquitoes, grapefruit, Mark Frost, Fairchild.

Analysis: Patricia and David West leave the Nausikaa seeking action, but Mandeville and the mosquitoes impose Ecological Constraint through heat, bites, thirst, distance, and bad water. Grapefruit aboard is comic scarcity; ditch water and injured skin ashore turn Patricia's fantasy of movement into bodily dependence. Mark Frost and Fairchild continue discussing poetry while the escaped pair learns that leaving salon language does not remove environment, class difference, or vulnerability.

The Fourth Day

Summary: The Fourth Day starts with Fairchild reading David West's misspelled note saying he is leaving the boat for a better job and asking Mrs. Maurier to pay back five dollars. Breakfast proceeds without the steward as Mrs. Wiseman, Dorothy Jameson, Patricia, and others open cans, deal with grease, blood from Patricia's thumb, beans, coffee, and more grapefruit. The party tries to reconstruct whether someone has drowned after the confused boat episode involving Jenny, Ernest, Mark Frost, Talliaferro, Eva, and the rope, while Fairchild, Julius, Mark, Mrs. Wiseman, and others resume arguments about young girls, sex, American education, Emerson, Lowell, poetry, Satyricon in Starlight, and the gap between books and authors. Source anchors: David West, Fairchild, Mrs. Wiseman, Dorothy Jameson, grapefruit, Satyricon in Starlight, Jenny.

Analysis: David West's note removes the working-class figure serving the yacht, forcing Mrs. Wiseman and Dorothy Jameson to recognize food as labor. The grapefruit and breakfast confusion reveal Informal Institutions beneath Mrs. Maurier's salon: unspoken class expectations assign provisioning, cooking, and cleanup to people the guests barely see. Satyricon in Starlight and Jenny pull conversation toward theory and bodily presence while the failed meal exposes the labor that made aesthetic leisure possible.

Epilogue

Summary: The epilogue returns the characters to New Orleans and follows their separate reabsorptions into ordinary life. Jenny rides the Canal Street car in her rough-dried green dress, goes home to her night-force father, and defuses his anger by climbing into his lap and repeating Patricia's sailing phrase. Pete returns to the Ginotta family business, now transformed from an Italian restaurant into an Americanized dance-and-liquor establishment run by Joe with electric signage, booths, waiters, deliveries, a Studebaker, and a Chrysler. Fairchild and Gordon move through night streets, whisky, brothel doorways, the typewriter, and talk of genius, while Mr. Talliaferro tells Fairchild about Jenny abandoning him at a dance and ends the night spraying pennyroyal, soaking his feet, and telephoning a new plan to be brutal. Source anchors: Jenny, Canal Street, Pete, Ginotta, Fairchild, Mr. Talliaferro, pennyroyal.

Analysis: Jenny and Canal Street show how quickly the yacht adventure collapses back into working urban routines, with her green dress carrying lake damage into the streetcar. Pete and Ginotta place class mobility beside cultural loss, because the old food-centered restaurant has become a polished nightlife business without the old smells or community. Fairchild and Mr. Talliaferro end where the prologue began: talk, whisky, sexual failure, pennyroyal, and the inability to turn self-analysis into self-knowledge.
