Position in the vault

Travis J. I. Corcoran's Escape the City volume 1 is the first half of a practical, engineer's-eye homesteading manual (followed by Escape the City volume 2, which covers household, meat-processing, brewing, workshop, and blacksmithing). It sits in the practical-survival cluster alongside other how-the-machine-actually-works literature, and is conceptually the terrestrial twin of the vault's frontier-expansion books: the same "private actors build their own infrastructure under technical risk" logic that runs through The High Frontier - Human Colonies in Space and How to rapidly industrialize Mars is applied to an individual family carving a self-reliant footprint out of rural New England. It is the anchor reading for the Private Frontier Capital, Infrastructure, and Technical Risk reading path, and it feeds the concept notes on Frontier Systems, Legibility, Ecological Constraint, Logistics and Throughput, Capital Allocation, and Informal Institutions. No real Series hub covers it; it is a standalone two-volume manual with a direct sibling volume 2.

Detailed overview

Escape the City volume 1 is a decision manual for people who want to leave suburban or city life and run a modern homestead, written from the specific vantage of a suburban-born, forty-something, engineer-and-lawyer-typed author who bought a 56-acre farm in southern New Hampshire. The book is deliberately not a pastoral meditation. It is organized as an equipment-and-process handbook, moving from motivation (why move at all), to planning and finding land, to the hard infrastructure the rural household must own and run (heat, water, electricity, internet, garbage, septic, roads, freight, radio, driveway), to buildings, boundaries and layout, vehicles (dominated by the tractor), livestock, plants, pests, and finally the forest as a productive asset. Throughout, the author's persona is the curious amateur who treats homesteading as an engineering problem: he gives prices (as of 2021), catalogs failure modes, and repeatedly insists that enthusiasm is not competence.

Corcoran frames the book around Robert Heinlein's "competent man" quotation and explicitly rejects specialization. His guiding method is to break each system into components, research interfaces and tolerances, and test assumptions. The sustained argument is that the country is not a pre-modern wilderness but a place where the background services of the city are privatized downward onto the household: no trash pickup, no municipal water or sewer, no guarantee of grid power during ice storms, no curbside delivery, often no high-bandwidth internet, and no anonymous contractor culture. Every one of those services becomes a physical system the owner must design, buy, maintain, and repair — an "infrastructure pyramid" that is wider and taller than the suburban one.

The book is also, unusually for the genre, honest about economics and second-order effects. It attacks the circulating "one-acre self-sufficient homestead" fantasy by John Seymour for omitting parking, septic, wells, and pig behavior; it runs real cost-per-pound arithmetic for eggs, eggs-for-meat, sheep, pork, hay, and mushrooms; it works through two full hypothetical first-year budgets (a "small garden and chickens" homesteader who spends roughly $10,000, and a "homemade bacon" homesteader who spends roughly $40,000); it computes solar-panel NPV and firewood heating-degree-day math; and it repeatedly warns that labor, cold, snow, rats, weeds, and husbands-and-wives' competing priorities will consume more of every resource than the plan anticipated. It is aggressively skeptical of permaculture food-forest theory and of armchair formulas, favoring what it calls "actual dependencies" over fantasy.

Structurally, each major section follows a "tools first, processes second" schema, so the reader costs out fences, tractors, coops, orchards, and mills before deciding whether the activity is worth doing. The tone is chatty and self-deprecating (the author recounts his own mistakes at length: the log splitter sized for 30-inch rounds, the tree-guard that strangled a willow, the hay baler with a slipping knotter, the fence gate posts installed three inches too narrow, and the run-over brushhog), which does more than humanize: it treats failure as data. The result is less a how-to-homestead pamphlet than a manual for how to think about homesteading: enumerate the system, size the inputs, respect the constraints, buy the right tool once, and expect friction.

The manual closes inland and in the forest: forest management as a decades-long crop decision, firewood logging as a physical and safety skill, chainsaws down to their chain geometry, lumber milling and drying, mushroom culture, maple syrup as a spring ritual with evaporator sizing math, beekeeping as a rent-taking business, and deer hunting as a legal-and-tooling exercise in local knowledge. Volume 1 deliberately hands off the kitchen, butchery, brewing, workshop, and blacksmithing domains to Volume 2.

Major people, societies, and motivations

  • The reader / escaping urbanite: the intended audience — a suburban or city adult (often with a white-collar remote-friendly job) who dreams of land, livestock, and self-reliance but lacks farm experience. Corcoran constantly calibrates advice to this person's actual failure modes: overestimating first-year productivity, falling in love with a single property, buying animals before fences, and underestimating winter.
  • The author, Travis Corcoran (the narrator-homesteader): a suburbanite until age forty, self-described "curious amateur" with a "systematic and analytic" engineer/lawyer temperament (he half-jokes about being INTJ/"sperg"). He is the book's example-of-one: he does real homesteading work (1.5 days/week, plus hired farmhands at peaks), he welds, breeds sheep, taps maple trees, skids logs, and he documents his own purchases, prices, and mistakes. His wife is a shadow partner who manages the vineyard, berry patch, and front orchard and is credited with the pragmatic "learn gardening, food processing, and medical study" advice for young women.
  • The homesteaders, hobby farmers, and gentleman farmers the manual addresses: the taxonomy of rural dreamers (suburbanite-in-the-country, garden-with-chickens, market gardener, homesteader, commercial farmer) that the entire book is organized to serve differentially. The manual's core recommendation is that readers identify which scenario they actually intend before buying land, tractors, or chickens.
  • Rural contractors, loggers, excavators, and farmhands: the informal labor market the homestead depends on. Corcoran depicts rural scheduling norms ("I almost forgot," show-up-unannounced), branding absent (Don with a backhoe, email harleydavidsonfan1983@yahoo.com), logging and excavator supply chains oriented to big commercial jobs, and a farmhand labor pool that ranges from competent to catastrophic (the weeder who pulled all the pumpkins). He also documents the Free State Project's libertarian day-labor cohort and their "counterculture" work-boundary violations.
  • Neighbors and town officials: the social environment — the mill-ruining farmer who hayed the fields, the gunsmith neighbor defended by the zoning board, the building officer the board backed, and the toxic seller who stripped fixtures and returned to steal hardware. Corcoran is clear that informal local power, friendship networks, and right-to-farm law largely set the terms of rural coexistence.
  • The ancillary experts and correspondents: a friend Sean ("pigs are a-holes"), friend Marcus (cattle and logging stories), friend Dan (seed mix recommendations), the commercial orchard friend (cider blend plan), contractors, a forester, shearers, sawyers, and the book's proofreader (fastener expertise) — all woven in as named authorities reinforcing the "I report what I and trusted locals actually found" method.

Themes and concepts to track

  • The privatized utility: municipal background services (water, heat, power, sewage, roads, trash, phones) reappear on a homestead as owned systems requiring capital, maintenance, and repair. Track how each section converts a city service into a household task.
  • Competence as process: Heinlein's "competent man" ideal expressed as enumeration, sizing, and rehearsal — "tools first, processes second," budgets before pigs, and 100 hours of close-to-home chainsaw practice before felling.
  • Friction and second-order effects: Clausewitz's friction, the Hayek/Friedman bottom-up planning threads, and the recurring message that the simplest thing is difficult and accumulates.
  • Ecological and physical constraint as arithmetic: acreage per animal, bushels per tree, cords per acre per year, BTU budgets, frost days — the manual's standard move is to turn romantic goals into numbers.
  • Anti-fragility and resilience: wood heat, PTO generators, well buckets, wood gasification, stored seeds, and a deliberate preference for multi-use tools and redundancy ("backup of the backup").
  • The skepticism filter: Chesterton's fence and the efficient-market heuristic applied to food forests, walipini, permaculture pig "plowing," and heritage-breed obsessions — if it were truly better, someone would already be doing it.
  • Local knowledge and informal institutions: town meeting politics, right-to-farm, scheduling norms, fencing a hard-to-parcel land, and the sheer value of trustworthy neighbors and contractors.

Core concepts

  • Frontier Systems: rural households directly manage heat (oil/propane/wood), water (well + pump + treatment), backup power (generator, batteries, solar), septic, roads and driveways, freight delivery, garbage, and radio — every background service of suburban life becomes owned infrastructure with failure modes.
  • Informal Institutions: contractors, neighbors, zoning boards, right-to-farm statutes, Free State Project labor, and "rule of friendship" shape what can be built, hired, and defended far more than written law.
  • Legibility: surveys, monuments, deeds, stakes, 3-4-5 baselines, ground paint, and standardized 20-foot logs convert land and wood into coordinates others (excavator operators, sawyers, machines) can act on.
  • Ecological Constraint: pasture acres per sheep, deer fencing, pollination groups, growth factors and DBH, heating degree days, one cord per acre per year, first/last frost — biology and climate cap every plan regardless of budget.
  • Logistics and Throughput: storage parity for feed and fuel, winter chore access, pallet deliveries and liftgates, snow-drift economics, hay-elevator flows, and implement-swap time all determine whether a homestead's repeated chores stay workable.
  • Capital Allocation: the book is a sustained CAPEX/OPEX exercise — $10k vs $40k first-year scenarios, tractor-vs-rental versus excavator-vs-contractor math, solar NPV, per-pound egg/pork/hay/mushroom costs, and "buy cheap once" traps.
  • Economy, Firms, and Infrastructure: illiquid rural real estate, white-label implements, spot-price oil and pulp markets, loggers' two-sided business, and the commodity-business warning that hobbyists compete against scaled professionals.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Intro

Summary: Corcoran opens with three ways to read the book — straight through, by index, and by following cross-references in a "spanning tree" of topics (he likens it to Wikipedia, TVTropes, or the Encyclopædia Britannica). The worked example is a BLT: a "I, Bacon, Lettuce and Tomato Sandwich" recipe that walks the reader from a loaf of bread (vol 2 baking) through bacon (vol 2 butchering), tomato, lettuce, mayonnaise, a cast-iron skillet, and tomato soup — a culinary variant of Leonard Read's "I, Pencil." The welcome section establishes the author as a suburban-raised amateur ("not a third-generation farm guy"), a systematic/analytic INTJ type, and a manifesto for the Heinleinian "competent man" ethos, complete with the dedication "not to be bug men" and the full "change a diaper / plan an invasion" quotation. He then defines the taxonomy of gardening, homesteading, farming, horse farm, hobby farm, gentleman farmer, and his coinage "hobo farmer," and enumerates five farming scenarios — suburbanite in the country, a garden with chickens, market gardener, homesteading, and commercial farmer — with the running principle that no single rural dream fits all of them. He also explains the equipment-first, processes-second organization and notes that all prices are 2021 new-equipment prices, with used equipment as low as 10% of new.

Analysis: The Intro installs the book's central rhetorical move: turn a mundane consumable (a BLT, implicitly a pencil) into a branching chain of owned competencies, then sort readers by actual motive and scale before any money is spent. The taxonomy matters because it relentlessly separates "gardens grow food; farms grow dollars," and the hobo-farmer self-deprecation primes the reader to accept the author as a fellow amateur rather than a guru. This framing is itself a Capital Allocation discipline — decide which scenario you are buying before buying anything — and the "equipment first" rule means cost gating happens up front. The Heinlein dedication gives the book its Frontier Systems ethos: a household that can staff its own infrastructure.

Source anchors: BLT; Leonard Read, "I, Pencil"; Robert Heinlein, Time Enough for Love; spanning tree; gardening/homesteading/farming; horse farm; hobby farm; gentleman farmer; hobo farmer; market gardener; commercial farmer; 2021 prices.

Life in The Country

Summary: This section argues the country is still recognizably modern — schools, Amazon, Facebook, commuting, TV — but operationally different: no trash collection, legally fine AR-15 shooting in backyards, pig carcasses hung in a garage as the first step of bacon-making, and raccoons handled with a .22 rather than a police call. Distance from civilization is literally measured in 30-minute-to-two-hour drives for coffee, dinner, or groceries; the country store may combine gas station, convenience store, and hardware store; and the nearest hospital may be far. Corcoran compares taxes versus services (rural taxes lower, services thinner due to per-capita economics), reproduces a town right-to-farm clause backed by New Hampshire RSA 674:32 a-c and the federal right-to-farm consensus, and details what a farmer may legally do to a suburban neighbor: tractors at 4 a.m., hot-August manure spreading, pig slaughter and intestinal composting on the property line, and halogen lights at 10 p.m. He covers zoning, noise-ordinance hearings, yard-aesthetic norms (a 1,000-square-foot house on a lot with three working cars, four dead cars, snowmobiles, steel beams, a chicken coop, and an ice-fishing shack), internet reality (56-acre farm, ~50 Mbit/s fiber), cultural fit and scheduling norms, branding absence ("Don with a backhoe"), Free State Project day-labor boundary violations, rural jobs and remote work, neighbors, and buyer's remorse.

Analysis: The section converts "moving to the country" from a lifestyle fantasy into an institutional and social audit. The right-to-farm material is the clearest example: written law plus a town zoning board staffed by friends of the offending gunsmith make "the rule of friendship" dispositive, which is Informal Institutions operating at full strength. Buyer's remorse, cultural-fit mismatches, and the FSP labor norms all reinforce the same lesson that motivation must survive contact with local social texture. At the same time the "56-acre farm with fiber internet" detail and the free-market substitute services (contract trash pickup, tutors) sketch a fully modern life with private provision — the mildest possible version of Frontier Systems, where even public utilities can be bought as services.

Source anchors: right to farm; RSA 674:32 a-c; AR-15 backyard; pig carcass in garage; .22 raccoon; country store; 56-acre farm; fiber internet; cultural fit; "Don with a backhoe"; Free State Project; remote work; buyer's remorse; noise ordinance hearing; zoning board.

Planning Your Move

Summary: Planning spans everything from the final moving-truck weeks to a fifteen-year horizon. Corcoran rejects one-size acreage advice: a 10'x10' garden and chickens fit on a quarter acre, while sheep (10 on 2.5 pasture acres), a half-acre garden, a half-acre vineyard, a woodlot, cattle, hay, and grain push needs to fifteen, forty, or more acres. He devotes a section to why John Seymour's widely circulated "Start a 1-Acre, Self-Sufficient Homestead" article is harmful — no parking, septic, well, or yard; pigs imprisoned in a barn stall; the "pigs plow your grassland" myth; and an unrealistically small cow pasture — with the maxim "you can fix almost everything later, but not total size." He gives three countdown plans (ten-year for young adults building career/financial base/tools/skills/mate with a SpaceX-rocket compounding analogy; three-year research and practice plan; one-year house-hunting plan), a full house-hunting workflow (commute limits, town-tax spreadsheets showing fourfold variation, boot-on-the-ground town investigation, need/want lists with immutable-versus-mutable features, deed-registry spelunking including an F12 browser hack to dodge New Hampshire's $2-per-page deed fee, and a sober treatment of illiquid rural markets where a 3,500-unit town has only two houses meeting basic criteria), buying and haggling (BATNA, price and fixture haggling, junk and third-party equipment clauses, seller's tools negotiation — the Kubota B7800 package deal — and required inspections including septic pumping and well "shocking"), and arrival/goal-setting with two fully-budgeted first-year narratives.

Analysis: This is the book's heart as a planning document. The acreage discussion is a pure Ecological Constraint exercise — activity determines land need, and margins must absorb boulders, slopes, and poor soil. The illiquid-market analysis and the haggling play-by-play (including the \(20k-package-to-\)18k Kubota negotiation with comps and a manufactured walk-away) are Economy, Firms, and Infrastructure in action, while the two first-year narratives convert the whole planning stack into explicit Capital Allocation: $10,000 buys chickens, a garden, a used riding mower, and a winter of firewood; $40,000 buys a used tractor, fences, sheds, three pigs, and a full butchery kit. The "arrival goals" warning — everyone overestimates the first five years — is the book's recurring humility check, and the "work backwards from the winter cold window" scheduling logic is a nice example of sequencing under a physical constraint.

Source anchors: John Seymour, 1-acre homestead; space shuttle/SpaceX rockets; FOUR acreage arithmetic (10'x10' garden, 0.1 acre pigs, 2.5 acres sheep, 0.5 vineyard); realtor.com; New Hampshire deed registry; BATNA; Kubota B7800, \(18k; septic inspection; well shocking; Small Garden and Chickens (\)10k); Homemade Bacon ($40k); arrival goal priority lists.

Utilities

Summary: Utilities is the manual's largest infrastructure section. Heat covers oil furnaces and 250-gallon tanks and the economics of spot prices and fall lock-in deals, propane delivery and vaporization limits in cold weather, and a deep dive on wood heat: make-up air and chimney draft, flues and chimney types and defects (creosote, occlusion, cracked linings), cleaning, wood stoves versus fireplaces and fireplace inserts, wood boilers with their 300–500-gallon water jackets and feed/ash/airlock/maintenance routines, and the full firewood chain (cords, grapple loads, energy content, drying, stacking on mega-pallets, heating-degree-day estimation, manual/electric/hydraulic/PTO splitting, sourcing from loggers via the "I'm a farmer, several grapple loads, cash in hand" pitch, and a sustainable one-cord-per-acre rule). Water covers wells (groundwater vs aquifer, dug vs drilled, artesian), pumps, testing and shocking, modular treatment stages, emergency water, rainwater, and grey water. Electricity covers line power and its costs, generator sizing and classes, PTO generators, transfer switches and inverters, wood gasification, and a full solar sizing/NPV treatment. It then covers internet, garbage, septic, roads and road classes, freight, radio (ham/CB/FRS/GMRS/MURS, ending on the software-defined Baofeng UV-5R), and driveway snow removal and grading.

Analysis: The utility sections are the clearest statement of the Frontier Systems thesis: every background service of the city is enumerated, sized, and given a physical home on the property. The wood-burner subsections in particular are an exercise in Logistics and Throughput — firewood is a storage, drying, stacking, moving, and feeding problem measured in cords and heating-degree-days, and the author's "measure the splitter before you cut" advice is a small masterpiece of second-order thinking. Electricity is treated under Capital Allocation and ecological reality: solar doesn't make sense everywhere, but NH's high power prices offset its low insolation, and NPV math decides. The septic, well, road-class, and freight subsections all demonstrate that rural life privatizes risk downward — a cracked leach field or a class-6 road is a household problem with a dollar figure.

Source anchors: 250-gallon oil tank; creosote; chimney cap; wood boiler; cord; face cord; grapple load; mega-pallet; heating degree days; PTO generator; transfer switch; solar NPV; 100 gallons per household alcohol law aside; well pump; shock chlorination; septic leach field; class 5 vs class 6 roads; depot delivery; Baofeng UV-5R; spreader chains; "three-inch mix" driveway fill.

Buildings

Summary: Buildings enumerates the outbuilding stack beyond the house: workshop, bunkhouse, barn, coop, run-in shed, sugar shack, garden shed, tractor shed, firewood shed, and alternative structures (pole barns, cargo containers, fabric buildings). The house chapter argues hunting criteria — workshop space, pantry/storage, proximity to barns. The workshop chapter specifies 200 square feet as a bare minimum, an 800-square-foot shop described as cramped, ground-level access, a dedicated subpanel, lighting math (80–100 foot-candles; the author's "insane overkill" 180 fc × 800 sf = 144,000 lumens in 32 fixtures), and a workbench recipe. Barns get the central-aisle design, stall sizes and per-animal square footage (12'x12' stall holds 1 pony, 1 horse, 1.5 cows, 7 goats, 7 sheep, 40 chickens, 24 turkeys, 24 geese, 3 pigs), hayloft lighting and drop-hay options, underground conduit for barn utilities, yard hydrants and their packing/freezing maintenance, NEMA L5-15 locking outlets in stall ceilings, mucking with a pitchfork technique, and Joel Salatin's removable-partition idea. Coops get roosts (2x4 edge-up for chickens — the experimental forum lesson), automatic doors, fixed versus mobile chicken tractors, floor bedding, laying boxes, feeders/waterers (including nipple systems), dust, calcium, grit, food storage, light timers, and a \(0-to-\)6,500 cost range. Run-in sheds, sugar shacks, garden sheds, tractor sheds, cargo containers, fabric buildings (with the anti-wind anchoring and cover economics), and greenhouses/high tunnels/low tunnels are all given design and price detail.

Analysis: Buildings is where infrastructure becomes site design. The per-animal-stall math and the "you go to war with the barn you have" honesty are Ecological Constraint applied to floor plans; the author's clean-sheet wish list (five to six stalls, six-plus stalls of covered storage, removable half-height dividers so a skid-steer can muck the whole barn) is textbook Logistics and Throughput. The fabric-building anchoring saga, the cargo-container corner-loading cautions, and the greenhouse vent/shade/heating upgrade ladder all carry the book's persistent message that you should spend capital on structure first because it cannot be retrofitted, and defer lights, heaters, and irrigation until you know what you actually want. The high-tunnel economics (\(2.21–\)5.50 per square foot across DIY, economy, premium, and reinforced) and raised-bed price ladder ($0 to $1,050 per 4'x16' bed) are Capital Allocation miniatures.

Source anchors: 800 sq ft workshop; 144,000 lumens; 12'x12' stall; central aisle barn; hayloft; yard hydrant; Joel Salatin; chicken tractor; roll-away laying box; nipple waterer; rubber bowls; run-in shed; sugar shack; cargo container TEU; ShelterLogic fabric building; kayak-hoist-solved high tunnel; wiggle wire; high tunnel; walipini; 2x4 roost edge-up; egg-washing bucket.

Surveying and Layout

Summary: Corcoran separates surveying (licensed, legal weight, upstream of deeds) from layout (informal, no legal weight, for the owner's own work). Surveying delivers monuments (permanent; brass disks to rebar with crimped markers, survey nails, buried rebar — and removing one is a crime under New Hampshire RSA 472:6), stakes (temporary), a map, and optionally a report, all anchored in earlier surveys and office research with the National Society of Professional Surveyors 2 cm + 50 ppm precision standard. He recounts his own $600 boundary survey for a stone wall and screening trees along a 500-ft driveway, and reads old farm subdivision history off property-line geometry like a bullet-crack forensics photograph (straight long lines old, lines terminating against them newer). Layout covers the tool kit (stakes, mallet, string, string reel and level, flagging tape, ground paint, surveyor's tape, measuring wheel, compass, builder's level, transit), baselines, the 3-4-5 rule for right angles, diagonal-checks for squareness, grid-and-string staking for orchards, and vertical layout with string levels, builder's levels, water levels, and cut-and-fill.

Analysis: Surveying versus layout is a distinction about Legibility: a survey makes land legible to the law and to everyone downstream (deeds, excavators, zoning officers), while layout makes a garden, fence line, or orchard legible to the owner's own muscles and machines. The advice to do all layout before excavation, stake every point, and stay on site while the excavator works is a coordination rule for Logistics and Throughput — machines are expensive and the cost of "kinda north/south" is measured in moved earth. The 3-4-5 and diagonal-check material gives the reader a repeatable procedure, and the \(600-vs-\)150 survey story is a clean Ecapital decision about when precision is worth paying for. The Public Land Survey System aside frames the entire chapter as the tension between legible rectangular grids and inherited, organic New England metes-and-bounds.

Source anchors: survey; monument; stake; New Hampshire RSA 472:6; National Society of Professional Surveyors; $600 survey; 3-4-5 rule; diagonal check; builder's level; water level; string level; cut and fill; stone wall; metes-and-bounds; Public Land Survey System.

Vehicles

Summary: The vehicles section is dominated by the tractor, approached as a platform: three-point-hitch categories 0–4 (with cat. 1 as the overwhelming homestead choice), front-end loaders (bucket attachment systems, pin-on vs skid-steer quick-attach, forks, grapples, teeth, quick spades, chain grab hooks — including welding hooks onto the bucket and the "it's a barn, not a yuppie condo" permission structure), engines and cold-weather diesel gelling, cockpits/cabs/ROPS and rollover safety statistics, wheels/tires/tubes with full sizing and tread-pattern detail, tire ballast and chains, the three-point hitch (top link, lifter arms, telescoping stabilizers, quick hitches, rear remotes, top-and-tilt), the PTO (540-RPM stubs, shafts, guards, slip and overrun clutches, machine keys, shear pins and their nylock-nut/grade rules), and the implement catalog (unpowered drawbars, ballast, rakes, back blades, box blades, plows, middle busters, subsoilers, disc harrows, fence stretchers; powered log splitters, snowblowers, rotary cutters/bushhogs, rototillers, post-hole diggers, post drivers, and spreaders/seeders/overseeders). Buying guidance covers need/category/horsepower mapping to acres, new vs used, dealer inspection, odd brands and the gray market, and operation (starting interlocks, driving controls, PTO/3PH handling, FEL joystick, implement-attachment friction — Clausewitz's "friction" quoted approvingly). Riding mowers, ATVs/UTVs, skid-steers, backhoes, excavators (rent vs hire), bulldozers, trailers, and general maintenance (batteries, jacks, tires, oil changes) each get treatments.

Analysis: The tractor chapters are the book's most concentrated dose of Logistics and Throughput: the value of the machine is in its interfaces (3PH, PTO, quick-attach, remotes) and its inventory of implements, and the recurring pain is swapping, mounting, greasing, and shear-pin replacement. The cat. 1 focus and "'what category' before 'how much horsepower'" is a sizing discipline; the 56-acre→50-60 HP recommendation versus the author's own at-the-limit 30 HP B7800 is honest Ecological Constraint applied to machinery. Safety forms a through-line — ROPS + seatbelt as a 99% rollover-survival claim, PTO guards, tractor-towing flip hazard, and "never trust a hydraulic cylinder" — and the safety messaging is itself a bet on survivability as part of Frontier Systems. The gray-market/arbitrage story, the white-label implement business model, and the "buy cheap used, outgrow it, trade it" advice are Economy, Firms, and Infrastructure set-pieces.

Source anchors: three-point hitch categories; cat. 1; skid-steer quick attach; ROPS; PTO 540 rpm; shear pins; nylock nuts; slip clutch; post hole digger; backhoe subframe; gray market; Messick's; Kubota B7800; tire ballast; tire chains; riding mower; zero-turn; BCS two-wheel tractor; excavator rent vs hire; bulldozer PAT; trailer hitch classes; machine keys.

Raising Animals

Summary: Fencing comes first because it is common to nearly all livestock: perimeter versus cross fencing, corner designs from single posts up through the double-H corner, post types and driving methods, gates (stiles, cattle guards, swing gates and their installation/accessories, sliding gates, and electric-fence spring gates), chicken wire, wood fences, electric fencing (components, P vs PN modes, cross-pasture electro-mesh), woven wire, high-tensile wire, and feedlot panels, followed by the author's own five-phase fencing program (front 1.25 acres, chicken run, run subdivisions, road pasture, orchard) and "what I'd do differently." Chickens get sexes and roosters, breeds, egg and meat economics, predators, chicks (enclosures, heat, food/water/inoculation), keeping, and eggs. Goats and sheep cover resources, herd size, health care (drenches, FAMACHA, hoof trimming, hammocks), shearing and wool processing economics, breeding, milk, lambing, meat, the annual schedule, and per-sheep economics. Pigs cover behavior and ethics (the "pigs are a-holes" friend, and the dignity-vs-community argument), resources (shelter, straw, outdoor space, strong fence, water, food, mud wallow, feed types and growing your own), herd size, breeding economics, escapes and Maxwell's-demon herding, permaculture skepticism, and detailed conversion-ratio/marginal-growth/prise-per-pound arithmetic. Cattle are deferred to a friend's herd, and fish get ponds and IBC-tote aquaponics.

Analysis: The animals section makes Ecological Constraint concrete: fencing first because livestock is only a cycle of value if it stays contained and fed, and every species adds feed, water, shelter, health, breeding, and escape demands. The egg, sheep, and pig cost-per-pound chapters are sustained Capital Allocation — eggs at $1.20–1.50/dozen, sheep at about $5.70/lb of meat with fleece offsets, and pigs at anywhere from 74¢/lb (scraps + self-butchery) to $10/lb (heritage breed + custom feed + outsourced butchery). The fencing philosophy — "no such thing as a fence that is too good," ground-anchored corners, and the phase plan — is a physical constitution of the property, tying into Logistics and Throughput (gates large enough for machinery, runs arranged so animals and equipment can move). The pig-ethics digression, arguing that rights come from integration into the human community rather than intelligence, is the book's most explicit political-philosophy statement, and it sits oddly-and-refreshingly alongside all the conversion-ratio math. The IBC-tote fish chapter gestures at Frontier Systems extended to protein.

Raising Plants

Summary: The plant section opens with definitions (forest, woodlot, orchard, berry patch, vineyard, lawn, pasture, field, garden) and then a sustained attack on the "food forest" concept via Wikipedia's definition, the Big Rock Candy Mountain allusion, Chesterton's fence, the efficient-market heuristic, and an account of Paradise Lot where even the authors' mostly-documented failures resolve into "more research is needed." Silvopasture is treated through two worked examples (sheep in a fenced orchard; sheep asked to eat Japanese knotweed, which they stunted but did not kill). The orchard core covers tree biology (cultivars, pollination groups and pairs, grafting and rootstocks), planning math (bushels, sizes, spacing, pollination partner distance, climate zones, the grid layout method), multi-year schedules, ordering, planting, pruning, pest control (rust, Japanese beetles, guard ropes and the tree-guard strangulation story), harvesting, storage, and hard-cider orchards. Lawns get a full maintenance program (mowing, clippings, fertilizing, weeds, watering and why you mostly don't, overseeding, dethatching, aerating, top dressing, leaves, rock walls). The garden chapter is exhaustive: locating and bed/path sizing, raised-bed materials with a price ladder, the annual-planning spreadsheet (zones, frost dates, seed catalogs, complements/antagonists, rotation, multi-/cover-cropping, overwintering), seed starting indoors, soil preparation, planting depth, mulching options, thinning, weeding, irrigation, fertilizing, harvesting, cleanup, seed saving and genetics; then specific plants. Pasture covers creation (logging, stump removal, contouring, soil testing, amending, drag harrowing, seeding, cultipacking, TLC), maintenance (brushhogging, fertilizing, 2,4-D, overseeding, rotational grazing), hay vs silage, and the full haying chain and economics. Field crops, vineyards, and berry patches round out the catalog.

Analysis: The plant chapters are where Ecological Constraint and skepticism meet agronomy. The food-forest takedown is the flagship of the book's "Chesterton's fence" method: understand what already works and why before inventing layered utopias, and treat "low maintenance" as a red flag. The orchard chapters show genuine complexity accepted where biology demands it — pollen compatibility, rootstock/scion interaction, and pollination-group charts are exactly the kind of legible systems the author respects, and the iterative spreadsheet-based garden planning is Legibility applied to a seasonal system. The raised-bed and mulching price ladders, haying economics (naive $1,275/acre vs realistic $300 first-years), and the "plant trees first because they take fifteen years" advice are Capital Allocation throughout. The lawn-watering math (the tripod-sprinkler acre being essentially impractical) and rotational grazing's S-curve are Logistics and Throughput and constraint thinking made explicit.

Pests and Invasive Species

Summary: The pests chapter opens with Colorado potato beetles (identified by stripes and orange underside eggs): three defenses — late planting (the author's success, changing odds from maybe 90% to 50% plus luck), low tunnels (an abject failure for lack of attention, not a disproof of the method), and Sevin/carbaryl, which failed absolutely once because a bottle stored in the garden shed over winter had been destroyed by cold, then worked when fresh. Rats follow as an unavoidable barn companion wherever feed exists: poison (bromethalin vs warfarin, Tomcat bait stations) rejected in favor of traps and dogs once poison failed to work and the author soured on its slow deaths; spring traps and the gold-standard Goodnature A24 CO2 trap; and the author's two short Corgi mixes' nightly "barn time" rat-hunt over stall floors and under feed dishes. Flies are accepted barn furniture whose indoor overflow is dealt with by fly paper over the kitchen compost bin; wasps and hornets get Raid Wasp & Hornet spray (prallethrin and cypermethrin). The invasive-species half covers glyphosate in its forms and sprayer tiers (handheld, backpack, vehicle), Japanese knotweed's biology and carbohydrate rhizomes, the conventional useless/harmful methods (surface spray, tarps destroyed, brushhogging that spreads hydra-like fragments), the successful surface-spray-then-pull multi-year campaign on the "stonewall patch," and the far better JK injection-gun technique with its node placement, vent-hole modification, cane marking, and 98%+ claimed kill rates; then Oriental bittersweet (cut-and-spray, impractical to remove), and poison ivy (urushiol, war of attrition, never burn or compost).

Analysis: The pest and invasive-material is a finest-grained demonstration of the book's method: identify the biology, find the failure mode of the cheap/fast fix, then design a labor-and-chemistry program that respects the organism's actual limits. The Colorado potato beetle section is a lesson in timing, chemistry storage, and attention rather than product choice; the rat material is an explicit ethical and operational decision among poison, mechanical traps, and predator animals with vaccination-free pets; and knotweed is the template long-haul fight, ending with a tool (the injection gun) whose node-idiot-proofing converts an otherwise interminable surface-spraying grind into a 98% kill in one season. The Ecological Constraint lens is at its most literal here: an invasive's rhizome reserves are a carbohydrate endowment, and the winning move is to exhaust it faster than it refills. The "never compost knotweed or poison ivy" rules are precisely the kind of second-order caution the book is built on.

Forest Products

Summary: The final and longest section treats the forest as a renewable productive asset, answering The Lorax with paper arithmetic (a paper-mill tree makes ~80,000 sheets, so a million printed Loraxes cost over 1,250 trees) and defining silviculture. It grades wood into veneer, sawmill, and firewood/pulpwood tiers and gives rough prices; explains DBH and growth factors (the years-per-inch table, with ~81 years to 18" at a median 4.5 factor); covers rotational clearcutting, shelterwood/seed-tree, and selective harvesting; runs sustainable-yield math (a 37-ft-radius tenth-acre inventory circle, and the New England rule of one cord per acre per year in perpetuity); and explains forest improvement (selective pressure, the 24×diameter release rule, and a worked 36-year sapling-thinning plan) and coppicing/pollarding. Firewood logging gets full treatment: dealing with loggers, safety, choosing and marking trees, felling (escape routes, the 60–90° notch and why 90 is worth pursuing, the felling cut and hinge, wedges, sit-back and hang-up recovery), limbing, slash disposal (drag, chip, burn with NH permits and full extinguishing discipline), log lengths, winching, and skidding with ATV arch or tractor skidding-plate/FEL-hook. Chainsaws get a mini-manual: selection (Husqvarna vs Stihl; the author's Rancher and Dolmar), bars, skirted nuts, clutches and brakes, chains (parts, sizes, cutting-edge geometries, tooth sequences, brands, making your own), sharpening by hand and machine, raker depth, and the field/workshop checklists. Lumber covers milling economics, hiring a portable sawyer, bandsaw mills, chainsaw mills, cut types (plain, quarter, rift), riving, bowl blanks, and air vs kiln drying with checking and honeycombing. Mushrooms cover hunting/farming, substrates, spawn, biological efficiency economics, and the author's failed and accidental experiences. Maple syrup covers biology, collection, reduction (evaporator vs reverse osmosis), equipment (taps, buckets, homemade and commercial arches, pans flat/divided/drop-flue/raised-flue, sizing math, the kayak-hoist cleanup, accessories, indoor finishes with thermometer/hydrometer/optical), and process. Beekeeping is a gloss toward Storey's Guide plus hive technology and economics, and hunting covers licenses/tags/regions, seasons, scouting, tree stands, butchering logistics, and posting/trespass law.

Analysis: Forest Products is the manual's capstone: the same "turn a resource into numbers and a schedule" method applied to the slowest-maturing crop. The grading, growth-factor, and sustainable-yield material is the definitive statement of Ecological Constraint — firewood self-sufficiency only works at one cord per acre per year, and a tree is a crop that takes a human lifetime. The chainsaw chapter pushes Logistics and Throughput to the level of machine metaphysics (pitch, gauge, raker depth, carbide, and the "all my chains are the same H80, and every AR-15 is .223 so no incompatible magazines" standardization argument), while the felling safety material is the book's most serious survival text. Maple syrup and its evaporator-sizing math generalize the book's pattern of "determine your desired output, then size the tool." Beekeeping's Mancur Olson "stationary bandit" economics and hunting's tag and check-station explanations tie resource husbandry to Informal Institutions and state management of common-pool resources. Throughout, the section shows a private capital-owner building long-horizon, multi-output infrastructure — fuel, lumber, syrup, honey, and protein from one asset — the purest example of the vault's frontier-capital theme.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • BLT as a competence map: "I, Bacon, Lettuce and Tomato Sandwich" sends new readers through vol 2 (bread, bacon, mayonnaise, cast iron, tomato soup) — the book's spanning-tree organization in one recipe.
  • The competent-man ethos: the Heinlein quotation, and the dedication "not to be bug men," do all the ideological heavy lifting; reopened on the "specialization is for insects" line.
  • One-acre fantasy rebuke: John Seymour's "Start a 1-Acre, Self-Sufficient Homestead" — no parking, no septic, no well, no yard, a cow on half an acre, and the pigs-will-plow myth; "you can fix almost everything later, but not total size."
  • First-year budgets: the "Small Garden and Chickens" homesteader lands near $10,000; the "Homemade Bacon" homesteader near $40,000 — both before depreciation and both self-audited with line-item expense lists.
  • Right to farm: all 50 states; NH RSA 674:32 a-c; a town can authorize 4 a.m. tractors, hot-August manure, and backyard pig slaughter next door, and someone else can start a farm ten years after you buy.
  • Illiquid rural real estate: a town of 3,500 housing units hosts ~37 listings; the author's bare-minimum criteria (2,500 sf, 10+ acres) leave two; about one acceptable house per town per year; three-month search window ≈ 25% odds.
  • The $18k Kubota deal: seller's \(20k package → comp research (\)17k low / $19k high) → split to $18k → manufactured walk-away → seller caves a week later; "play the game the way it's meant to be played."
  • Firewood as numbers: face cord vs full cord (128 ft³); grapple load ≈ 10±2 cords; oak cord ~5,000 lb green / 3,600 dry; ~24M BTU/cord typical; NH rule = one cord/acre/year in perpetuity; 1,800-sf house in Anchorage ≈ 9.6 dry cords.
  • Wood boilers: 300–500-gallon water jackets make them all-or-nothing season-long systems; NH run is roughly first week of November to last week of April; feed twice daily; weekly ash; the Friday-night skip trick before cleaning.
  • Solar realism: NH insolation ≈ 3 kWh/m²/day → discount 25%; author's 1,500 kWh/month and $386/month bill imply 67 panels ≈ \(16,4k (system ~\)24.6k, and ~$20k with existing inverter/batteries); NPV verdict: solar beats NH grid power despite weak sun.
  • Panel price falloff: $3.50/W (2006) → $0.50/W (2020), ≈ 13%/year compounding — yet "ignore the wait-one-more-year bug."
  • Anti-fragility stack: wood heat + PTO generator + well bucket/manual pump + wood gasifier ambitions + stored seeds; the "backup of the backup" habit (two heat lamps, spare chains, spare motors on the shelf).
  • Chainsaw chain standardization: "every AR-15 in my house is chambered in .223" → all chains are one geometry/loop size so there are never incompatible spares.
  • Tree felling craft: 60–90° notch reaching ~25% depth; a full 90° makes the tree land exactly when the notch closes; wedges for sit-back; escape routes pre-chosen at 45°; hinge kept even or the tree twists.
  • Knotweed injection: glyphosate concentrate injected into the hollow cane between the 1st and 2nd nodes draws poison both up into the stalk and down into the rhizome; vent the summer water-filled internode first; ~200–250 ft²/hour on hands and knees; 98%+ kill claimed and matched.
  • Classic failure modes saved for reuse: Sevin stored too cold went inert; the log splitter's 24" limit vs 30" rounds; the spiral tree guard that strangulated a willow; gate posts set 3" too narrow; hay baler knotter stuck after two bales; the brushhog eating a bounced-out tractor-caddy tool.
  • Sheep herd math: 10 ewes × ~80% × 150% ≈ 12 lambs; ~6–7 in the freezer; 120 lb live → ~60 lb cut; two fleeces/year (spring poor, fall good); mature-herd "meat cost" ≈ $5.70/lb with fleece sales offset.
  • Pig economics ladder: scraps+self-butchery 74¢/lb → packaged feed+outsourced $5.55/lb → pumpkins+feed+self $2.60/lb (the author's method) → heritage+custom mix+butchering $10/lb; "buy piglets or buy feed and you can make money; buy both and you never will."
  • Hay economics: naive $1,275/acre vs $300/acre the first two years and \(600 thereafter; ~\)300/acre of out-of-pocket costs; ~100 two-string bales per acre per cutting.
  • The $60 stone-wall/expense cleanup: 12–24" quarry rocks at $20/yd³ + $150 trucking per 15-yd load, three loads + a rented excavator ≈ $2,000 total, ≈ $10 per linear foot of New England wall (and 1 yd³ ≈ 5–6 feet).
  • Solar concealment of intent in negotiation: "I'll be so house poor after closing I can't afford much" — expensive signaling is more trusted than cheap signaling.
  • Land-reading as class: long straight property lines are old; lines terminating against them are newer (the bullet-crack forensic trick); the west got Jefferson's legible Public Land Survey rectangles instead.
  • Retrieval cue for the whole book: "the actual dependencies hidden inside that phrase" — self-sufficiency is a phrase; this volume is the dependency list.
