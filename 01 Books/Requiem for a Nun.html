Detailed overview

Faulkner builds Requiem for a Nun as a hybrid of county chronicle and stage drama. Each act begins with a long prose history of a Yoknapatawpha institution, then narrows into a theatrical scene where Temple Drake Stevens, Gavin Stevens, Gowan Stevens, Nancy Mannigoe, the Governor, and the Jailor must speak inside the legal and moral rooms those histories have produced. The courthouse, the capitol's golden dome, and the jail are not background buildings. They are accumulated instruments: archives, locks, ledgers, statutes, speeches, windows, bars, and civic legends through which the county tries to turn guilt into record and suffering into sentence.

The first act makes the Yoknapatawpha courthouse out of accident, theft, frontier improvisation, and racial dispossession. A Chickasaw Agency trading post becomes Jefferson; land grants, slave bills of sale, militia rolls, tax rolls, mortgages, and execution records sit in an iron chest; Alexander Holston's fifteen-pound Carolina lock migrates from tavern object to mail pouch to jail door; Compson, Ratcliffe, Peabody, Louis Grenier, Sutpen's captive Paris architect, John Sartoris, Doctor Habersham, Issetibbeha, Ikkemotubbe, and old Mohataha all enter the founding story. The county seat begins as a compromise between violence and paperwork, and the courtroom scene makes Nancy Mannigoe's death sentence part of that archive.

The drama itself returns to Temple Drake after the events recalled from Sanctuary. Temple is now Mrs. Gowan Stevens, living respectably with Gowan, their son Bucky, and the murdered infant whom Nancy had nursed. Yet the old Memphis brothel, Red, Popeye's murder trial, Gowan's drunken failure, Temple's testimony, the Paris marriage, the Embassy reception, the Crillon, Cap Ferrat, and the blackmail letters all remain active debts. Faulkner's famous line, "The past is never dead. It's not even past," is not a general motto here; it names the exact pressure by which Temple's earlier corruption enters the nursery, Gowan's paternity doubts, Pete's blackmail, Nancy's intervention, and the child's death.

Nancy Mannigoe is written through the racist language and legal categories of Jefferson, but the play gives her a theological authority the white institutions do not possess. In the courtroom she answers the death sentence with "Yes, Lord"; in the jail she tells Temple that hope is harder to relinquish than salvation; and before execution she speaks of belief, sin, suffering, work, and a heaven where even murdered children and unborn children may be within forgiveness. Gavin Stevens can reason, quote, reconstruct, and defend, but Nancy's religious language forces him and Temple to confront a form of accountability that cannot be reduced to law.

The prose interludes keep enlarging private guilt into public history. Act Two's golden dome begins with prehistoric Mississippi, Pearl River, mound builders, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Natchez, Spaniards, Frenchmen, Anglo-Saxon pioneers, Jackson, secession, war, and state power before the Governor hears Temple's confession at two in the morning. Act Three's jail history moves from logs and whitewash to Mohataha's dispossession, Civil War burning, postwar return, progress, subdivisions, neon, federal money, old citizens, and the scratched windowpane bearing a girl's name. These chronicle sections make Temple and Nancy's case one episode in a county that continually preserves what it claims to have outgrown.

The ending refuses legal rescue. The Governor does not save Nancy; Temple cannot buy expiation by confessing; Gavin cannot convert explanation into release; Gowan's late arrival does not undo the damage in the nursery; and Nancy's execution remains scheduled for the next morning. What changes is Temple's understanding of what remains after sentence: "tomorrow, and tomorrow, and still tomorrow." Nancy has only to die, but Temple has to live in a world where forgiveness, if it exists, must be worked toward without proof. The jail bars, Coca-Cola, county coffee, hymn singing, and the clashing keys make that final theology local, bodily, and unromantic.

Core concepts and linkages

  • State Capacity: courthouse archives, capital authority, gubernatorial clemency, jail routines, execution procedure, and county recordkeeping show institutional power as paperwork, buildings, personnel, and ritual.
  • Legibility: land grants, slave bills of sale, tax rolls, militia records, court sentences, blackmail letters, and scratched windowpanes make people visible to institutions in unequal ways.
  • Knowledge Preservation: the courthouse, golden dome, jail, prose interludes, and Temple's confession preserve histories the county would rather convert into progress, procedure, or private shame.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: Nancy's "Yes, Lord," hymn singing, and theology of belief create an authority that law can punish but not fully judge.
  • Language and Ideology: verdicts, clemency language, racial categories, legal formulas, biblical phrases, and Faulkner's chronicle prose all decide what guilt can be called.
  • Moral Economy: Nancy's care for the child, Temple's confession, Gavin's defense, and the Governor's refusal expose claims of debt, sacrifice, punishment, and forgiveness that exceed legal accounting.
  • Empire and Periphery: Chickasaw dispossession, frontier settlement, state formation, and Jefferson's civic memory place Temple and Nancy's case inside a colonial county order.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

ACT ONE: The Courthouse (A Name for the City)

Summary: Act One begins by tracing Jefferson's courthouse back to a Chickasaw Agency trading post, a dog-eared archive of land grants, slave bills of sale, tax rolls, militia rolls, mortgages, rewards for escaped Negroes, and a jailbreak involving Natchez Trace bandits. Alexander Holston's Carolina lock, Pettigrew's mail pouch from Nashville, Compson's pistol, Ratcliffe's axle-grease complaint, Doctor Peabody's laudanum, Louis Grenier's carriage, Sutpen's Paris architect, John Sartoris's later columns, and the surviving courthouse make public justice look accidental before it looks solemn. The stage scene then places Nancy Mannigoe in the county courtroom on November thirteenth, with Gavin Stevens defending her after she smothered the Stevens infant, and her only spoken answer to the death sentence is "Yes, Lord." Source anchors: Chickasaw Agency; Holston lock; Pettigrew; Ratcliffe; Nancy Mannigoe; Yes, Lord.

Analysis: The Chickasaw Agency and Holston lock show that the courthouse grows from dispossession, property records, frontier fear, and improvised security rather than pure justice. The land grants, bills of sale, militia rolls, tax rolls, and jail lock make the courthouse a Legibility machine before it becomes a moral institution: people, land, enslaved property, fines, and sentences enter the county by being written down. Pettigrew and Ratcliffe expose the comedy and corruption inside public order: the mail pouch, the lock, and the axle-grease charge all make federal authority both ridiculous and binding. Nancy Mannigoe's "Yes, Lord" is a case for Religion and Legitimacy because it breaks courtroom procedure with a religious answer the judge, bailiff, spectators, and Gavin Stevens cannot assimilate into sentence, so the scene sets a sacred authority that the county's legal institution can neither refute nor acknowledge beside its own.

ACT TWO: The Golden Dome (Beginning Was the Word)

Summary: Act Two opens with the state capital on Pearl River, the golden dome imagined through geology, mound builders, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Natchez, Pascagoula, Frenchmen, Spaniards, Anglo-Saxon pioneers, Jackson, cotton soil, secession, Civil War, and the Capitol's claim to higher judgment. In the Governor's office at 2:00 A.M. on March twelfth, Temple Drake Stevens and Gavin Stevens plead for Nancy, but Temple's confession becomes the real proceeding: she recalls the University train, Gowan's drunken car, the moonshiner's house, Memphis, Red, the murder trial, Paris, the Embassy, the Crillon, Cap Ferrat, Gowan's paternity doubts, Nancy as confidante, Pete's blackmail letters, the hidden money and jewels, Bucky's absence, the six-month-old baby in the nursery, and Nancy's decision to kill the child rather than let Temple run away with Pete. Source anchors: Pearl River; Governor; Temple Drake; Red; Pete; hidden money.

Analysis: Pearl River and the Governor lift the case from county court to state sovereignty, but the late-night office still cannot separate law from Temple Drake's private history. The golden dome is State Capacity staged as architecture and clemency procedure: the Governor has the office that can halt the execution, yet Temple's confession shows how little that office can repair. Red and Pete make the past repeat through male violence, sexual coercion, and blackmail, while hidden money shows that the practical problem is not simply letters but Temple's desire to flee respectable life. Nancy's killing of the baby becomes intelligible only inside that pressure: she acts against the infant to stop Temple from carrying the child into Pete's orbit, which makes the Governor's legal choice morally insufficient even when it is final. The act's pressure is Knowledge Preservation without comfort, because confession preserves the past by making it inescapable rather than by resolving it.

ACT THREE: The Jail (Nor Even Yet Quite Relinquish)

Summary: Act Three begins with the jail as the oldest surviving witness in Jefferson, older in memory than the courthouse, marked by calcimine, creosote, whitewash, log walls, Mohataha's departure west, the jail's Civil War use, new subdivisions, neon from the Holston House, old citizens, Monday-morning fines, and the scratched windowpane that preserves a girl's name and date. The stage scene takes place in the second-floor bullpen on March twelfth at 10:30 A.M., where Temple, Gavin Stevens, Jailor Tubbs, and Nancy meet before the hanging. Tubbs offers Coca-Cola and county coffee, mentions hymn singing in the jail, and leaves Temple to ask Nancy how to live after the Governor refuses clemency; Nancy answers with belief, says salvation does not need hope, compares God to a man with too many mules, and insists that suffering and work remain even for a murderess. Source anchors: jail; Mohataha; scratched windowpane; Tubbs; Coca-Cola; believe.

Analysis: The jail, Mohataha, and scratched windowpane make confinement the county's deepest archive, and the interlude is a case for Knowledge Preservation because the building preserves dispossession, war, domestic legend, and punishment in one structure that the county claims to have outgrown. The scratched windowpane bearing a girl's name and date is preservation at its smallest, turning a private act into a record the institution cannot erase, which mirrors the way Faulkner's chronicles keep enlarging private guilt into public history. Tubbs and Coca-Cola keep the final interview brutally local, surrounded by small-town politeness, racial habit, hymns, keys, coffee, and impending execution. Nancy's "believe" matters because it gives Temple no legal escape and no intellectual proof; it leaves her with a command to live after Nancy dies, which is why the jail scene is harsher than the courtroom sentence. The final exchange is also Moral Economy in theological form: punishment pays the law, but Temple still owes work, suffering, and care to the living world Nancy is leaving.
