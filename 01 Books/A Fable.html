A Fable

Position in the vault

A Fable treats World War I less as a timeline of battles than as an administrative-mythic machine: rituals, schedules, paperwork, and public ceremonies that keep killing socially and procedurally possible. Faulkner makes that machine visible by staging a stoppage (a mutiny that produces silence) and then following the panic-response across crowd, staff room, provost procedure, economic coercion (payday debt), and quasi-fraternal authentication (lodge signs, committee names, passes).

Within this vault the book is especially useful for thinking about how institutions metabolize disruption: the Place de Ville as a clock-face, the factory-compound converted into a man-proof pen with electrified wire, the execution staged as a marketplace, and the later conversion of bodies into national ritual via the "unknown soldier" extraction at Verdun.

Detailed overview

Faulkner builds the novel around an impossible hinge: a French regiment refuses the ritual of going over the top, and the refusal propagates along the line as silence until the guns stop across sectors. The text insists the stoppage is not a private moral awakening but a logistical event: heliographs blink, dispatch riders roar, staff officers assemble, and the line itself becomes an audience watching the inspectors and provost marshals who arrive to make the pause legible as a "recess" rather than as a collapse. In the background, an aviator recognizes that even the anti-aircraft barrage can be theater: blank shells fired so the sky will still look "orthodox" while generals conduct their back-channel meeting.

The novel's public face is the crowd. Faulkner turns civilians into a collective body that moves like fluid down boulevards to the Place de Ville, toward an Hotel with a blank door guarded by three allied sentries under three flagstaffs. The crowd follows lorries carrying an arrested regiment, crashes against electrified wire at a compound that used to be a factory in peace, then turns back when it realizes it cannot yet be "late." The day becomes a machine of waiting: two-hourly reliefs, shadows, and the overlapping bugles of three armies (French, English, American) that close the day like a stamped document.

Under that mass movement sits a second binding medium: money and membership. A flashback to late spring 1916 introduces the runner and the battalion economy: a surly private with a thick leather money-belt and a dogeared notebook lends and collects at payday, and a committee with a grand title, Les Amis Myriades et Anonymes a la France de Tout le Monde, can send a French government motorcar to reach him. Names (Reverend Tobe Sutterfield; later "Tooleyman") matter here as mechanisms: legitimacy traveling by title, paperwork, and access rather than by rank.

Faulkner tightens the mutiny into a procedural trap. The runner is battered awake in the silence, finds the battalion p.c. emptied of its "orderly disorder," and uses a pass to move where rifles are suddenly irrelevant. Later he tries to recruit power by learning a lodge sign and by leveraging the money-belt man's hold over the battalion ("making them all Masons or anyway making them believe they are Masons"). The war-machine's answer is sealing: police perimeters, and then a perimeter manned by troops who cannot read the pass and do not share the language of the men bottled up behind them.

The punishment scenes are rendered with grotesque practicality: medals are torn off coats before the volley; a sergeant-major finishes a body with a pistol; fatigue parties arrive with a barrow and tarpaulin; and in another cell an American, Buchwald, tries to stage a clean symbolic death for a French general by literally plugging a wrong bullet-hole with candle wax so the corpse can be shot again "correctly." Procedure is sustained by people competent with tools.

Finally, the war collapses into domestic ruin and then resurfaces as national ritual. Marthe and her family return to a farm beyond Chalons near Vienne-la-pucelle and St Mihiel, bury a cheap coffin, and face land they are forbidden to work until unexploded shells are found. Then the state orders men to go to Verdun, to Fort Valaumont's catacombs, and "with expedition and despatch" extract "one complete cadaver" for the unknown-soldier rite. The fort's smell is described as fear and endurance made material, something men will get used to. If the book has a bleak claim about persistence, it is that trained tolerance of what should be intolerable is itself an institutional resource.

Within this vault, A Fable is particularly relevant to Crisis Governance (how panic and staff-control propagate), Language and Ideology (tokens like "Fatherland," court-martial language, committee names as legitimacy), Legitimacy and Succession (ritual authority: flags, medals, sentries, executions), and Collapse and Resilience (what "silence" does to the war-machine, and how the machine reasserts itself).

Primary characters and motivations

A Fable is built on a deliberately flattened cast in which most figures act less as individuals than as functions of the war-machine or of the attempt to refuse it. The characters below carry the book's load; several are unnamed in the text and are identified by role.

  • The runner: an anonymous English battalion runner, once a successful London architect who renounced his commission (he calls it "that sort of masturbation about the human race people call hoping") to live back in the mud as a private. He moves through the book as the official "listener": the man who learns about the thirteen French deserters, who explains the blank-shell "recess" to a disbelieving sentry, and who, in the end, organizes the battalion's climb over the parapet barehanded. His motivational arc runs from despair ("It's not that we didn't believe: it's that we couldn't, didn't know how anymore. That's the most terrible thing they have done to us") to a final shout of possibility ("They cant kill us! They cant! Not dare not: they cant!") before he is destroyed in the mutual barrage. In the epilogue a scarred veteran on crutches who "came back out in '16" and claims he only waited "until he would be ready to do the needing" preserves his function as witness.
  • Levine: a seventeen-year-old RAF replacement pilot who joins too late for the RFC's heroic register (Ball, McCudden, Mannock). His arc is disillusionment: he wants to repay the cost of his training, but the "recess" voids the only trade he has learned; he forces the squadron to fire blank tracer at him so he can test what is real, and his smoldering sidcott becomes the image of the war's quiet persistence ("you could depend on it"). He embodies the book's tragedy of the idealist youth — the one for whom stopping the war deletes the meaning of the letter to his mother.
  • The division commander (General Gragnon): a self-made soldier, an orphan of the Pyrenees who rose from private to general of division and was given a doomed attack as a sacrifice so that the group commander could earn his baton. His motivation is honor and record: "How it dies is the reason it died. That's my record." He demands the right to shoot his own mutinied regiment, is refused, is put under arrest himself, and is finally killed in a basement cell — with the state staging the death "from in front" as if he had died in the attack, to keep the record clean. Buchwald completes the irony: the "victim" refuses to turn, so the executioners must shoot "correctly" the second time.
  • Buchwald: an American private (a future bootlegging czar), brought from Blois with an Iowan and a Negro undertaker to kill the French general in the whitewashed cell. His function is grotesque practical competence: he treats a sanctioned killing as a problem of optics, proposing to plug the wrong bullet-hole with candle wax so the corpse can be shot again "in the right place." He is the book's proof that procedure is maintained by people good with tools.
  • The money-lender (the sentry): a surly English private, ex-horse groom of the great stolen horse, who owns his battalion economically — beneficiary of his mates' insurance and holder of their future pay in a thick leather money-belt. He is the battalion's true command-chain: rankless, but able to say "Follow me" because everyone owes him. The runner's plan banks on this: "You own the whole battalion... All you'll need is just to tell them to when you say Follow me." His personal history (Baptist, Mason, dice-player, made over in America) ties the battalion economy back to the book's American fable of the horse.
  • Reverend Tobe Sutterfield / "Tooleyman": the old Negro with the "Roman consul" face, head of the committee Les Amis Myriades et Anonymes a la France de Tout le Monde; his French name is a corruption of "Tout le Monde" — "Tooleyman." He traveled to France to find the horse's groom (the money-lender) and save him, "bears witness" to man rather than to God in the old sense, and at the end goes over the wire with the runner ("Maybe this is what I been hunting for too"). He is the book's moral center: the character who believes that man needs only to say "Enough of this" and that "cant no witness hurt him."
  • The corporal (unnamed): the leader of the thirteen — the ring of mountain foreigners at the heart of the mutiny, seen as the "savior" figure the book's fable keeps circling. He refuses every avenue of escape the generalissimo offers (the car, Paris, the world, recognition as son), repeating "There are still ten." His motivation is simplicity itself: to keep the ten with him and to die for the possibility the refusal represents. At the execution he is bound to the middle post; when the volley cuts his cords, the post pitches backward over the refuse ditch into the barbed wire, where the sergeant-major finishes him with a pistol.
  • The old generalissimo (supreme commander): the gray, fragile, sleepless old man in the Hôtel de Ville at Chaulnesmont, the corporal's father (revealed only through Magda's story and the locket). He is the machine incarnate — ruthless, courteous, "not afraid of man" but respectful of his capacities, certain that man "will prevail" and has already begun taking his house to the road. Against the corporal he plays a long, gentle, despairing game of persuasion, and loses.
  • Marthe / Magda: the corporal's half-sister, who raised him — "I wasn't Marthe until after I had a brother and had to cross half of Europe to face thirty years later the French general who would hold the refusal of his life." She carries the locket containing the generalissimo's portrait, confronts him across a desk, and demands only the permission to bury her half-brother's body. At the fence after the execution she states flatly: "The war is over." She is the book's measure of what the machine costs in kin and land.
  • The old generalissimo's staff circle: the group commander ("Mama Bidet"), who burns the division commander's resignation and pronounces the book's thesis — "Fatherland... Yes, let them believe they can stop it, so long as they dont suspect that they have" — and the Quartermaster General, the old general's St Cyr classmate who once loved him, who confronts his mentor after the catastrophe ("We did it then... We against all because we no longer belong to us") and is refused even the dignity of resigning.

Major linkages

  • Sibling fiction from Faulkner: A Fable sits inside Faulkner's own war-haunted fiction: Soldiers Pay treats the same returning-soldier materials with the same tension between civilian sympathy and institutional neglect; the Yoknapatawpha family novels (The Sound and the Fury, As I Lay Dying) echo its use of a single event and a single voice to organize an entire community.
  • War-fiction and history siblings: War and Peace matches its thesis that commanders do not make history but are made by it; If This Is A Man - The Truce resonates with its portrait of the truce as fragile and of endurance as institutional; Homage to Catalonia carries its mutiny-within-a-war theme into civil conflict; World War Z extends the same image of a global war-machine grinding to a halt and restarting; Worm presents organized refusal and coalition power at a similar superhuman scale.
  • Era and structural context: The novel is a WWI fable (May–November 1918) written against the background of the Korean-era reappraisal of total war; The War That Ended Peace supplies the diplomatic machinery, and Postwar its aftermath. There is no series hub — A Fable is a standalone.
  • Concept and MoC hubs: the book is a primary exhibit for War, Revolution, and Civilizational Breakdown, and it interlocks with Crisis Governance, Language and Ideology, Legitimacy and Succession, Collapse and Resilience, Informal Institutions, Information and Coordination, and Logistics and Throughput.

Themes and concepts to track

  • The mutiny as a "recess": the refusal of one regiment is immediately reframed by staff, provost, and news-rumor as a pause that both sides have agreed on, then as a "curious week's holiday," then as something to be forgotten. Track how a stoppage is converted back into a procedure. See Crisis Governance.
  • Legitimacy by ritual and token: white flags on empty staffs, three national bugles, two-hourly sentry relief, the "Medaille Militaire torn off" before the volley, the signed pass, the committee name, the lodge sign. The machine protects objects and meanings while killing bodies. See Legitimacy and Succession.
  • Language as a weapon and a shield: the word "Fatherland," the army-legal language of the court-martial, "anarchist" pinned on a foreigner so punishment can proceed, committee titles that "travel by title rather than rank," "Monsieur Tooleyman." See Language and Ideology.
  • Money as informal command-chain: the money-belt and the dogeared notebook, the sixpence-a-day debt economy, insurance policies made over to one man, the "Association." See Informal Institutions.
  • Information as coordination: heliographs blinking silence into a condition, dispatch riders, blank shells fired for appearances, the officer who "learns it looks like a recess but is a collapse." See Information and Coordination.
  • Collapse and reassertion: what silence does to the war-machine — how it seals, audits, and restarts; how men are made to "get used to" fort-smell, gas-masks, and the next ceremony. See Collapse and Resilience.
  • The conversion of bodies into state artifacts: the extraction of "one complete cadaver," the unknown soldier, the ossuary at Verdun, the empty coffin filled with a farmer's corpse. See Logistics and Throughput.
  • The fable's Christ-allegory without theological comfort: the thirteen, the "resurrection" witnessed by three officers, the priest's suicide with a borrowed bayonet, "Remember that bird" — a passion narrative that the machine reabsorbs into ritual rather than belief.

Core concepts

  • Crisis Governance: how a single regimental refusal becomes a coordinated "recess" across sectors, and how the staff controls panic through sealing, auditing, and restaging the pause as procedure.
  • Language and Ideology: the tokens — "Fatherland," court-martial phrasing, committee titles, the word "anarchist" — that let punishment proceed without admitting native desire to stop.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: flags, medals, sentries, executions, two-hourly reliefs, signatures — the rituals that manufacture authority and continuity inside a stoppage.
  • Collapse and Resilience: what the silence does to the machine and how the machine reasserts itself; trained tolerance of the intolerable as an institutional resource.
  • Informal Institutions: the money-belt economy, the "Association," the lodge, the committee that sends a government motorcar to a private's billet.
  • Information and Coordination: heliographs, dispatch riders, blank shells, passes — how the pause is made legible and how knowledge travels (or is blocked) across it.
  • Logistics and Throughput: the factory-compound converted into a pen, the execution staged as a marketplace, the "expedition and despatch" extraction of a cadaver at Fort Valaumont.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Wednesday

Summary: The opening chapter renders the city's dawn as a single collective body. Long before the barracks bugles, the warrened tenements disgorge their people, who flow through lanes and alleys like streams becoming rivers until the whole population pours into the Place de Ville, facing the blank door of the Hotel, "the three sentries of the three co-embattled nations" flanking three empty flagstaffs awaiting the three concordant flags. The sunrise gun crashes and the three flags break out and stream into sunlight; garrison cavalry tries to hold the boulevard and is underswept by the kneeling, martyr-like crowd; infantry with a light tank parts the mass like a snow-plow. At one point a thin, travel-stained young woman faints, and a tall man in a faded smock — whom the sergeant calls "not even a field marshal... an advocate" — offers her a heel of bread and lectures that the whole regiment, at zero, never left the trench except officers and N.C.O.'s, and that by last evening every front in France had quit except token guns. The three allied generals pass in a pennoned car, and then the lorries come: the whole arrested regiment packed "as though for the transportation of cattle," followed by one last lorry carrying only thirteen men — manacled, chained, alien — with the crowd perceiving that the thirteenth is alien even to the other twelve. As the last lorry crosses the Place, the corporal in it stares full at the old generalissimo on the Hotel steps. The girl who fainted tries to break toward the lorries, spits her half-chewed bread at the tall man, and is left alone in the emptying Place. Source anchors: Place de Ville; blank Hotel door; three sentries / three flagstaffs; sunrise gun; infantry + light tank; "not even a field marshal... an advocate"; "as though for the transportation of cattle"; the thirteen-man lorry; corporal / generalissimo stare; the girl's bread spat out.

Analysis: Wednesday establishes the book's core method: legitimacy manufactured through choreography that people cannot easily refuse. Monday: Monday Night

Summary: This division opens with the division commander's backstory: an orphan of a Pyrenean Catholic orphanage who rose from enlisted private to general of division, whose "destiny" has slowed — who is offered, by his corps commander and only friend Lallemont, an attack that the corps commander knows is intended to fail. The night section has the division commander read the dead aide's book (a story he coldly admires for its competence), drive alone through spring darkness to the chateau, try to resign — and have the group commander burn the resignation over a candle, telling him the only word that can destroy them is "Fatherland," and ordering him to Chaulnesmont that afternoon. Analysis: The chapter is Faulkner's portrait of the machine from inside: a general who believes he is choosing a sacrifice is himself selected, "not because you were Charles Gragnon, but because you were General of Division Gragnon at this time, this day, this hour." The burned resignation and the "Fatherland" speech preview the novel's thesis about language — authority survives as long as belief in the word survives — which connects directly to Language and Ideology. Gragnon is also the book's clearest instance of Legitimacy and Succession collapsing into procedure: the career that defines him is exactly what is being sacrificed to keep the succession to the baton intact.

Tuesday Night

Summary: The chapter cross-cuts to two British privates resting on a firestep below the Bethune slag-heap after the "recess" has begun. One is the sentry — a jockey-sized ex-horse-groom whom the authorities have investigated because eleven, then twenty, of his battalion-mates made him beneficiary of their life-assurance policies; the colonel, questioned at Whitehall, can only say the word is "love," while the staff suspect he "owns the battalion." The other is the runner: a former successful London architect who renounced his commission ("I just dont want this anymore") and returned to ranks, though he retains a component of the officer that keeps the men from fully trusting him. From an old A.S.C. lay-preacher at the St Omer ammunition dump he learns about the thirteen French soldiers — "the same thing all them two thousand years ago: that all we ever needed to do was just to say, Enough of this" — and stops believing he can cross the line to witness them directly. Analysis: This chapter introduces the book's alternative command-chain. The sentry's money-belt and the battalion's insurance-and-payday economy are the informal structure that actually holds the unit together, which makes the chapter a strong anchor for Informal Institutions: "the man who already owns the battalion" exercises authority that rank cannot touch. The runner's lecture on the blank shells converts the war-machine's theater into logistics disguised as combat — an unarmed corridor, a column of myriads of harmless shells, an archie barrage staged for appearances — a direct link to Information and Coordination, since the entire deception exists so that the pause can be hidden by coordinated lies about what the sky is doing. The sentry's violence is not stupidity but institution: he has invested four years of debt and profit in the war's continuation, and the runner's "We could all just say Enough" is, to him, a threat to the ledger. The chapter also sets up the book's central ambiguity: the man who "owns" the men is exactly the leverage the runner needs to move a whole battalion later.

Summary: This division belongs to Levine, the seventeen-year-old RAF replacement pilot. He is turned back from his last practice flight by a general in a Harry Tate who signals him down, then summoned to the mess where the major announces the French have asked for an armistice effective at noon: "It was a French truce; it wasnt ours." Levine's sidcott, smoldering all night, is the book's cleanest image of Collapse and Resilience: a thing that cannot be put out, that keeps on quietly and steadily, "you could depend on it," the war's persistence abstracted into a burning fabric. His letter to his mother and his listening for the "single voice of all the women" remind the reader that the people with the least invested in the machine's reputation — mothers, women — are precisely those who would gain most from its stoppage, and that the machine knows it.

Tuesday: Wednesday

Summary: The chapter reins in the crowd to measure it. The young woman who fainted on Wednesday now stands at the old eastern city gate, scanning each arriving face; the villagers and farmers, the parents and kin, have been streaming in since Tuesday, when the news of the mutiny reached the district, "hoicked by anguish and terror" toward the city. They learn the details: the regiment did not fail in an attack but refused to perform the ritual itself — "declined to perform that ritual act which, after four years, had become as much and as inescapable a part of the formal ritual of war as the Grand March"; that the whole three thousand were corrupted by a single squad of thirteen, four of them foreigners, only the corporal able to speak French; and that the thirteen, leave-billeting across the French, British, and American zones for two years, three times vanished from France for two weeks with papers unstamped, implying "only darkness and a pair of wire-cutters" — No-Man's-Land and over. Heliographs begin to blink; at noon the French and German fronts fall silent and at three o'clock the American and British sectors follow. The crowd has meanwhile surged to the compound — a converted factory, "converted again yesterday into a man-proof pen" with barricades of electrified wire, searchlight towers, and an elevated catwalk for guards — crashes against the wire, and then faces about, realizing it cannot be "late": the division commander will not arrive until three o'clock, and the only permitted action is to wait. The chapter ends with the division commander arriving in his car under arrest, the furled sabre under the chief-of-staff's arm, watched by the crowd as a victim "like them, of that same instant in geography and in time," then the provost-marshals' cars, and at sunset the three national bugles — Battre aux Champs, Last Post, Retreat — the flags descending and the crowd flowing home to eat and return. Analysis: The chapter makes the mutiny legible as an administrative scandal before it is a moral one. The crowd learns not a doctrine but a paper trail — unstamped passes, vanished two-week absences, wire-cutters — which is precisely Crisis Governance operating in public: the staff's horror is that the system was porous for years without knowing it. The compound's electrified wire and catwalks are the book's blunt institutional metaphor, the same industrial efficiency that shipped numbered matériel converted in a day into a pen for French bodies. The two-hourly sentry relief and the triple bugles closing the day like a stamped document continue the theme of Legitimacy and Succession: the machine keeps its calendar and its ceremonies running through the pause, so that waiting itself becomes an act of loyalty. The division commander's walk to the blank door under a furled sabre — victim and executioner in one — shows how the book understands the officer class: caught in the same procedure they serve, given "no rights in its fate."

Tuesday: Wednesday: Wednesday Night

Summary: The chapter opens in a 1916 flashback: the runner reports to a battalion in Picardy billets near Amiens, preparing for the First Battle of the Somme, and in the brigade office finds a surly private summoned before the brigadier — "someone named Reverend Tobe Sutterfield" from America has asked the French government to reach him, the private's hook being "Missouri." The private refuses, and two hours later the runner sees why he matters: he is a lender seated on his bunk with a thick leather money-belt and a dogeared notebook, counting out French notes, the line on payday extending into the street. An orderly summons him — "It's a bleeding fing motorcar from Paris with a bleeding fing prime minister in it" — and the runner sees a long black government car containing a rich American woman, a high Cabinet secretary, a thin Negro youth, and an old Negro in a worn tophat with the serene "Roman consul" face. The runner works the orderly-room and learns the committee name: Les Amis Myriades et Anonymes a la France de Tout le Monde. In Paris he visits the committee's house, where the old Negro explains he is now "Tooleyman" (Tout le Monde), and tells the runner the horse story: the champion three-year-old, "Mistairy," owned by nothing and no one, whose groom is the money-lender — the twenty-two months of flight across the Mississippi watershed on three legs, the Federal deputy marshal who is a failed poet and resigns rather than catch them, the Missouri jail and the New Orleans lawyer, the crowd that frees the old Negro, the baptism in the creek, the Masonry, and the rich American woman — a widow, whose money helped support the French air squadron in which her only son was a pilot until a German killed him, and whose money then brought them to France. The committee title Les Amis Myriades et Anonymes a la France de Tout le Monde does the same at civilian scale — legitimacy traveling by name and motorcar and access rather than by military hierarchy, which is Legitimacy and Succession operating through language, hence the "Tooleyman" corruption of "Tout le Monde" as a case study for Language and Ideology. The horse story frames the mutiny's deeper claim: the horse "belonged to nothing to be stolen from; it was the world's horse," and the flight was not theft but "immolation, apotheosis" — the same logic of unconditional value that later makes the corporal refuse the generalissimo's freedom. The 1918 section shows the pass working where rifles no longer do, and the blank-archie corridor making the pause readable from below, which returns the chapter to Information and Coordination.

Wednesday Night

Summary: The young woman remains at the eastern gate through the dispersal of the Place; she still has the crust of bread, which she eats in quick birdlike snatches, then runs to a family group coming up the road — an old man on a crutch leaning on a blind old woman, and a woman carrying a child whom she calls "Marthe." The identification scene is the book's most explicit staging of the fable: three officers independently "kill" and "bury" the corporal, and the only consistent fact is that he keeps reappearing, so the machine must "witness to his resurrection" — a passion-narrative rhythm that doubles as a logistics problem for Legitimacy and Succession. The howling of the evicted regiment toward the single lighted window, and the Senegalese guards lounging above the white working parties, give Collapse and Resilience its most visceral image: a mass body held together by the wire's electric field rather than by any conviction. Thursday: Thursday Night

Summary: In a Paris bedroom the runner lays out his knowledge to the old Negro: the blank shells, the tracer, the phosphorus hit "like a garden pea," the German general ferried under shield of an orthodox-looking sky to meet the Allied "pooh-bahs"; he admits he stole a policeman's suit to get out and back in. Tomorrow

Summary: The final division opens a year and a half later, after the true armistice of November. Twelve men and a dour office sergeant — leaving Paris at midnight in a special third-class carriage with a new empty military coffin and a stolen brandy ration — reach St Mihiel and then Verdun, where an old woman whose son Theodule was killed in 1916 begs to come and identify him ("I will know him"); the sergeant refuses. At Fort Valaumont they find a midden of bones and scraps beside the low entrance, and descend into the catacombs among tiered bunks, torchlight, and the great underground smell of fear and endurance — fear "attenuated to that point where it must choose between coma and madness and in the intermittent coma no longer feared but merely stank." Under the order to proceed "with expedition and despatch... and extricate therefrom one complete cadaver of one French soldier unidentified and unidentifiable," a stretcher is filled, signed, and carried up. Back in St Mihiel the squad, now led by the ex-picklock "Picklock," sells the corpse to the old woman — "This is Theodule. This is my son" — for the money in her reticule (a hundred francs, all that survives of her sold farm), then buys from a local farmer a replacement body dug out of his plowed field near Vienne-la-pucelle, on the land of the widow whose dead brother was the corporal, where "the whole war started again after that recess they took last May, right in that field yonder"; Morache trades his gold watch for the farmer's price, and the empty coffin returns to Paris carrying a different, anonymous dead. The epilogue shifts to the farm years later: Marya moves among the geese, Marthe sows with the hired man, and a scarred English veteran on crutches — one leg and one arm gone, his face divided by saffron scar — arrives "come for the medal," with the man who had once denied his name, Zsettlani; she tells them the girl went back to the brothel in Marseilles. The last scene is the great funeral of the old generalissimo six years after, the cortege up the Champs-Elysees to the Arc de Triomphe and the flame, when the same one-legged Englishman bursts out of the crowd, rips the French medal from his breast, and hurls it at the caisson — "Listen to me too, Marshal! This is yours: take it!" — crying the epitaphs "They shall not pass. My country right or wrong. Here is a spot which is forever England," until the crowd and police drag him down; lying in the gutter, bleeding, he laughs at the ring of faces: "Tremble. I'm not going to die. Never." A gaunt old man in a worn black overcoat — the Quartermaster General — bends over him: "I am not laughing... What you see are tears." Analysis: Tomorrow completes the book's argument that the state converts bodies into artifacts and calls it honor. The "expedition and despatch" extraction is Logistics and Throughput in pure form: a cadaver is quota, receipted, signed, and its anonymity is the entire point, because the unknown soldier must be interchangeable to be national. The old woman's counter-claim — "This is Theodule. The epilogue at the farm and the funeral race the machine's afterlife against the persistence of refusal: the scarred veteran flinging the medal at the caisson, and the old general's "I am not laughing... What you see are tears," give Collapse and Resilience its final form — the institutions win, the wars restart, but the refusal acquires an enduring witness who refuses to die. The whole division is thus a study in how a legend is made legible to the state (the unknown soldier) and kept illegible to it (the flung medal).

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • The money-economy of the battalion: leather money-belt, dogeared notebook, sixpence-a-day repayment, insurance made over to one man, the payday queue "like people waiting to enter a cinema." Retrieved in the 1916 Amiens flashback and again in the runner's Mason-sign scheme.
  • Les Amis Myriades et Anonymes a la France de Tout le Monde: the committee and its banner over the faubourg gate, the knitting woman in the white coif, the motorcar that reaches a private in a billet; "Tooleyman" = "Tout le Monde."
  • The horse, Mistairy: the champion running horse "owned by nothing," the three-legged flight across America, the Federal deputy who is a failed poet and resigns, the New Orleans lawyer, "It was a heap," "We dont like rich niggers here," the baptism in the creek, the Masons.
  • The thirteen: four foreigners, nine Frenchmen, corporal "who few knew by name and even they could not pronounce it"; unstamped two-week leaves; wire-cutters; "Only darkness and a pair of wire-cutters."
  • The "resurrection" testimony: Boggan at Mons, Brzewski buried at sea, "so all that remains for us is to witness to his resurrection."
  • Blank shells / the orthodox sky: three miles of AA shells, batteries unloading live rounds for dummies, the unarmed corridor, the S.E.5s and the German two-seater, "archie never hits anything anyway," the tracer "like a garden pea," seaweed-like phosphorus.
  • Tokens of legitimacy: "Endorsed as received"; the furled sabre; the torn-off Medaille Militaire; the signed paper and the medal passed through the wire; the Monday pass and the word "Latrine."
  • The internal clock: two-hourly sentry relief; three o'clock; noon / fifteen hours; "time... back in mufti"; the three national bugles at sunset.
  • The machine's vocabulary: "Fatherland"; "anarchist"; "the war is over" / "But not the army"; "expedition and despatch"; "one complete cadaver"; "They shall not pass. My country right or wrong. Here is a spot which is forever England."
  • Buchwald's candle wax: "plug it up" and the re-shooting "in the right place"; the Iowan's map and pencil; the Negro undertaker from Mississippi.
  • The farm near Vienne-la-pucelle beyond St Mihiel: the unmarked house "eviscerated by removal," the beech-tree bank burial, the medal on the mantel, "It will take years," unexploded shells still closing the land, the crater, the charge passing Marthe.
  • Endings: the flung medal at the caisson; "Tremble. I'm not going to die. Never."; "I am not laughing... What you see are tears"; the December 1944 / November 1953 composition note.
