Detailed overview

Tony Judt's Postwar treats Europe after 1945 as a continent rebuilt through ruins, silences, American protection, Soviet coercion, welfare promises, and belated memory. The book begins with the physical and moral wreckage of World War Two: Warsaw, Berlin, Rotterdam, Coventry, displaced persons, UNRRA relief, rape by occupying armies, famine, black markets, collaboration trials, and the removal of Germans, Jews, and other minorities from the social map of central and eastern Europe. Judt's first claim is that postwar Europe could not simply resume prewar life because war and occupation had destroyed property regimes, legal habits, class hierarchies, and the credibility of many older elites.

The first part explains why reconstruction required both punishment and forgetting. Retribution against collaborators in France, Italy, Belgium, Norway, the Netherlands, Poland, Hungary, and Czechoslovakia was legally messy and politically necessary. At the same time, the rehabilitation of Europe depended on welfare legislation, land reform, Christian Democracy, Social Democracy, the Marshall Plan, the Schuman Plan, and American-backed security arrangements that made former enemies administratively useful. Judt repeatedly shows the bargain beneath recovery: Europeans gained stability by narrowing public memory, accepting Cold War frontiers, and allowing Germany to return as West Germany rather than as the defeated center of a continent-wide reckoning.

The Cold War chapters do not treat Europe as a passive chessboard, even though Washington and Moscow dominate the strategic setting. Greece, Turkey, Berlin, Prague, Tito's Yugoslavia, the Cominform, NATO, the Warsaw Pact, Hungary in 1956, Suez, Algeria, and the Treaty of Rome show Europeans making choices inside external constraints. Judt's western Europe becomes stable in part because the United States absorbs the security problem and because domestic parties learn to govern within welfare capitalism. His eastern Europe becomes stable in a harsher way: Stalinization, show trials, collectivization, censorship, and Soviet tanks create obedience, but after 1956 that obedience rests increasingly on exhaustion and cynicism rather than revolutionary belief.

The middle of the book explains the postwar boom as a social transformation, not just an economic curve. West Germany, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Britain, Sweden, Austria, and Spain experience growth through trade, productivity, urbanization, state planning, migration out of agriculture, and demographic renewal. Refrigerators, cars, television, youth culture, New Wave cinema, Italian design, consumer credit, tower blocks, suburban campuses, and mass tourism alter daily life. Judt's point is double-edged: prosperity made democracy ordinary and protected welfare institutions, but the children of that prosperity turned against bureaucratic universities, brutalist housing, deference, colonial wars, gender hierarchies, and old party languages in the 1960s.

The recessionary third part follows the erosion of that settlement after 1971. Oil shocks, stagflation, terrorism, the Red Army Faction, the Red Brigades, the IRA, deindustrialization, feminism, environmental politics, Eurocommunism, Helsinki, southern European democratization, Thatcher, Mitterrand, Solidarity, Charter 77, Vaclav Havel, KOR, John Paul II, and Gorbachev all mark the weakening of institutions built after 1945. Judt ties economic disappointment to political invention: when growth no longer guaranteed social peace, old class parties lost authority, new movements spoke in the language of rights and identity, and eastern European dissidents learned to expose regimes by acting as though truth and law still mattered.

The final part is about Europe's liberation from one division and its exposure to another set of fractures. German unification, the Soviet collapse, Czechoslovakia's separation, the Baltic states, Yugoslavia's wars, Bosnia, Kosovo, privatization, corruption, NATO expansion, EU enlargement, Maastricht, migration, racism, welfare retrenchment, and Holocaust memory all return Europe to questions that the first postwar decades had suppressed. Judt ends by arguing that Europe became recognizable not through a single nation, army, or constitution, but through a way of life: social protection, capital punishment's absence, regulated capitalism, public services, historical guilt, and the memory of catastrophe as a civic warning.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Preface & Acknowledgements

Summary: Judt opens by making Europe itself difficult to define: the smallest continent, almost an annexe of Asia, divided by language, memory, war, and recent political borders. He frames the book as a history of contemporary Europe written from proximity, since he was born soon after World War Two and remembers many later events as a witness. The preface also names the book's practical limits: no one can write a fully comprehensive account of Europe from 1945 to 2005, so Judt organizes the story around political reconstruction, memory, Cold War division, welfare states, and the problem of writing near history. Source anchors: smallest continent; annexe to Asia; contemporary Europe; World War Two; proximity; comprehensive history; political reconstruction; memory.

Analysis: The smallest continent and annexe to Asia language matters because Judt refuses to treat Europe as a natural unit. Contemporary Europe, World War Two, and proximity explain why the book is partly a historical synthesis and partly a reckoning with the author's own lifetime. The stress on comprehensive history and memory prepares the reader for a narrative that keeps crossing national borders while returning to the problem of what Europeans chose to remember or suppress.

Introduction

Summary: The introduction begins at Vienna's Westbahnhof in December 1989, when Judt sees eastern Europeans crossing a newly opened Europe and decides that the postwar era needs a single account. Vienna, 1989, the Iron Curtain, Germany, wartime memory, and the Holocaust give the introduction its organizing pressure. Judt argues that Europe after 1945 was built under the long shadow of World War Two, but that full acknowledgement of collaboration, genocide, expulsion, and defeat was postponed because silence helped make reconstruction possible. Source anchors: Westbahnhof; Vienna; December 1989; Iron Curtain; Germany; Holocaust; silence; reconstruction.

Analysis: Westbahnhof, Vienna, and December 1989 give the book a vantage point after the Cold War rather than at the war's end. Germany, the Holocaust, and silence identify the moral cost of European recovery: public peace rested partly on not saying everything about wartime behaviour. Reconstruction is therefore not only factories, welfare laws, and alliances; it is also a managed postponement of memory until Europe could bear the weight of its own past.

I: The Legacy of War

Summary: Judt describes a continent of ruined cities, uprooted people, destroyed transport, hunger, rape, orphaned children, and collapsed states. Rotterdam, Coventry, Warsaw, Berlin, Minsk, Kiev, Greece, Yugoslavia, Poland, and the Soviet Union appear through figures for destroyed dwellings, dead civilians, wrecked railways, and missing men. He gives special attention to 36.5 million European war deaths, at least 19 million civilian dead, 5.7 million murdered Jews, displaced persons, UNRRA relief, Vienna rations, Berlin rapes, and the forced movement of Germans from the East. Source anchors: Warsaw; Berlin; 36.5 million; 19 million civilians; 5.7 million Jews; UNRRA; Vienna rations; displaced persons.

Analysis: Warsaw, Berlin, and displaced persons make the postwar settlement a response to social collapse rather than ordinary diplomacy. The numbers 36.5 million, 19 million civilians, and 5.7 million Jews prevent Judt from treating recovery as a quick material rebound. UNRRA and Vienna rations show that state authority had to be rebuilt first as food distribution, disease control, shelter, and population sorting before it could become party politics or European integration.

II: Retribution

Summary: Judt shows liberated Europe trying to punish collaboration without destroying the rule of law it claimed to restore. France, Italy, Belgium, Norway, the Netherlands, Poland, Bulgaria, Kielce, Vichy, Salo, Article 75, national degradation, the Milices Patriotiques, shaved women, Togliatti's 1946 amnesty, Nuremberg, and denazification all appear as uneven answers to the same problem. Retribution begins as street violence and revenge, then becomes trials, purges, amnesties, and selective forgetting as governments reassert control. Source anchors: Vichy; Salo; Article 75; national degradation; Milices Patriotiques; Kielce; Togliatti amnesty; Nuremberg.

Analysis: Vichy, Salo, and Article 75 show why collaboration was legally hard to define: many accused people had worked for regimes that claimed state authority. Milices Patriotiques and Kielce reveal that revenge could target personal enemies, women, minorities, and Jews as easily as real Nazi agents. Togliatti amnesty and Nuremberg mark the move from fury to state-managed closure, which lets Judt explain how Europe restored legality while leaving many wartime crimes only partly judged.

III: The Rehabilitation of Europe

Summary: This chapter follows the first postwar reconstruction through welfare, land reform, Christian Democracy, Social Democracy, economic planning, and American aid. Judt moves among Britain, France, Italy, West Germany, Scandinavia, Romania, Hungary, Czechoslovakia, the Marshall Plan, the OEEC, the Schuman Plan, Beveridge, the NHS, the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno, women voters, and Christian Democratic parties such as the CDU, DC, and MRP. Recovery works because reform promises security while anti-Communist and Catholic parties make welfare compatible with political moderation. Source anchors: Marshall Plan; OEEC; Schuman Plan; Beveridge; NHS; CDU; DC; MRP.

Analysis: Marshall Plan, OEEC, and Schuman Plan show that rehabilitation was international before it was sentimental: dollars, trade rules, and coal-and-steel coordination made recovery practical. Beveridge and the NHS show why welfare became a moral answer to interwar insecurity. CDU, DC, and MRP matter because Judt treats Christian Democracy as the political vehicle that made reform acceptable to conservative Catholics, women voters, and anti-Communists.

IV: The Impossible Settlement

Summary: Judt turns to the diplomatic settlement that could not produce a single peace but did create a divided order. Germany, Potsdam, reparations, the Bizone, Berlin, Austria, Trieste, Poland's western frontier, displaced Germans, the Soviet zone, the Western zones, and the emerging Federal Republic and German Democratic Republic all show how Europe was stabilized through provisional arrangements. The chapter's title points to a peace that was impossible in legal form but functional in practice because occupation zones, currency reform, and administrative division hardened into facts. Source anchors: Potsdam; reparations; Bizone; Berlin; Austria; Trieste; Federal Republic; German Democratic Republic.

Analysis: Potsdam, reparations, and Bizone identify the material disputes that made a normal settlement impossible. Berlin, Austria, and Trieste show how unresolved places became managed exceptions rather than triggers for immediate war. The Federal Republic and German Democratic Republic are the chapter's institutional outcome: Judt shows a failed peace conference being replaced by two German states embedded in rival systems.

V: The Coming of the Cold War

Summary: Judt describes the Cold War as a European settlement produced by Soviet control, American commitment, and local crises. Greece, Turkey, the Truman Doctrine, Marshall Aid, the Prague coup of 1948, the Cominform, Tito's break with Stalin, Berlin blockade, airlift, NATO, the Council of Europe, and the first moves toward European integration form the chapter's map. The conflict grows not from one speech or one doctrine but from accumulating disputes over recovery, Germany, eastern European elections, and the limits of Soviet tolerance. Source anchors: Greece; Turkey; Truman Doctrine; Prague coup; Cominform; Tito; Berlin blockade; NATO.

Analysis: Greece and Turkey show why Washington translated European instability into a global anti-Soviet commitment. The Prague coup and Cominform reveal that Moscow would not permit plural politics in its zone, while Tito shows that Communist independence was possible only at enormous risk. Berlin blockade and NATO make the division military and institutional, converting wartime alliance into a durable European border.

VI: Into the Whirlwind

Summary: This chapter enters Stalinized eastern Europe: forced party mergers, nationalization, collectivization, police terror, show trials, and the destruction of independent civic life. Judt discusses Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, Romania, Bulgaria, East Germany, Rajk, Slansky, Kostov, church trials, universities, the Doctors' Plot atmosphere, Soviet advisers, and the East German uprising of June 1953. The point is that Communist rule becomes not simply pro-Soviet government but a system of fear, confession, purges, and bureaucratic dependency. Source anchors: Poland; Czechoslovakia; Rajk; Slansky; Kostov; collectivization; Soviet advisers; June 1953.

Analysis: Poland and Czechoslovakia show how diverse postwar coalitions were narrowed into Communist monopolies. Rajk, Slansky, and Kostov demonstrate terror turned inward against loyal Communists, which is why Judt treats the show trial as an instrument of rule rather than only punishment. Collectivization, Soviet advisers, and June 1953 expose the fragility beneath the apparatus: workers and peasants were coerced into a system that claimed to speak in their name.

VII: Culture Wars

Summary: Judt follows the ideological fight for European intellectual life after 1945. Sartre, Camus, Aragon, the French Communist Party, Italian Communists, Koestler, The God That Failed, Radio Free Europe, the Congress for Cultural Freedom, CIA funding, Soviet peace campaigns, American films, Coca-Cola, jazz, and anti-Americanism all appear as weapons in a struggle over legitimacy. The chapter shows that culture mattered because parties, magazines, universities, cinema, and radio could make either Soviet Communism or American capitalism seem like Europe's future. Source anchors: Sartre; Camus; Aragon; The God That Failed; Radio Free Europe; Congress for Cultural Freedom; CIA funding; Coca-Cola.

Analysis: Sartre, Camus, and Aragon locate the Cold War inside Parisian arguments about responsibility, violence, and Communist loyalty. The God That Failed and Radio Free Europe show anti-Communism speaking through testimony and broadcasting, not only through armies. Congress for Cultural Freedom, CIA funding, and Coca-Cola let Judt connect high intellectual debate to American cultural power and to the European suspicion that liberation could become consumer dependence.

CODA: The End of Old Europe

Summary: The coda closes the first part by arguing that the war destroyed old Europe socially as well as militarily. Jews, Germans, Polish elites, Baltic minorities, Sudeten Germans, landowners, bourgeois professionals, and imperial social orders disappear or are pushed aside. Judt links ethnic cleansing, property seizure, land reform, wartime collaboration, population transfer, and the weakening of aristocratic and bourgeois authority to a newly homogeneous, state-administered Europe. Source anchors: Jews; Germans; Sudeten Germans; landowners; property seizure; land reform; population transfer; old Europe.

Analysis: Jews, Germans, and Sudeten Germans show that postwar homogeneity was produced through murder, expulsion, and fear rather than natural national consolidation. Property seizure and land reform explain how social power changed hands while governments claimed reconstruction. Old Europe names the world that Judt says could not return: estates, minorities, imperial habits, and prewar social rank had been broken before welfare democracy or Soviet rule consolidated the new order.

VIII: The Politics of Stability

Summary: Judt opens Part Two by explaining how western Europe unexpectedly became politically stable after 1953. Stalin's death, the end of the Korean War, NATO, the European Coal and Steel Community, the European Defence Community, Adenauer, the Pleven Plan, Mendès-France, the London Agreements, the Paris Treaties, West German rearmament, the Warsaw Pact, the Austrian State Treaty, Trieste, and the Geneva summit all appear as pieces of a settlement that reduced immediate danger. Source anchors: Stalin's death; NATO; European Defence Community; Adenauer; Paris Treaties; Warsaw Pact; Austrian State Treaty; Trieste.

Analysis: Stalin's death, NATO, and the European Defence Community explain why stability came through Cold War management rather than a return to old sovereignty. Adenauer and the Paris Treaties show West Germany becoming acceptable by being tied to institutions. The Warsaw Pact, Austrian State Treaty, and Trieste demonstrate Judt's larger point that unresolved conflicts were cooled by embedding them in negotiated blocs and provisional agreements.

IX: Lost Illusions

Summary: This chapter pairs decolonization with the collapse of Communist hope in 1956. Judt moves from the Dutch East Indies, Sukarno, Indonesia, French Indochina, Ho Chi Minh, Dien Bien Phu, Algeria, Suez, Macmillan's "wind of change," and Acheson's remark about Britain losing an empire to Poland, Imre Nagy, Budapest, Soviet tanks, Janos Kadar, Tito, Radio Free Europe, and the exodus of Hungarian refugees. The lost illusions are imperial, Communist, and Western: Europe cannot rule colonies, reform Stalinism freely, or rescue eastern rebels. Source anchors: Sukarno; Dien Bien Phu; Algeria; Suez; Imre Nagy; Budapest; Kadar; Radio Free Europe.

Analysis: Sukarno, Dien Bien Phu, Algeria, and Suez show western Europe discovering that imperial prestige no longer paid for weakness at home. Imre Nagy, Budapest, and Kadar show eastern Europe learning the limits of de-Stalinization. Radio Free Europe is crucial because Judt uses it to expose the gap between Western rhetoric and actual willingness to confront Soviet power in Hungary.

X: The Age of Affluence

Summary: Judt explains the western European boom through trade, productivity, urbanization, demographic growth, planning, and consumption. Jean Fourastie, West Germany, Italy, France, the Netherlands, Austria, Spain, the Treaty of Rome, EEC trade, migration from farms, the Cassa per il Mezzogiorno, French indicative planning, West German social contracts, baby boom births, refrigerators, cars, television, and mass tourism all appear as signs of the "thirty glorious years." Source anchors: Jean Fourastie; West Germany; Treaty of Rome; Cassa per il Mezzogiorno; baby boom; refrigerators; cars; television.

Analysis: Jean Fourastie and West Germany give Judt a language for extraordinary growth after decades of depression and war. Treaty of Rome and Cassa per il Mezzogiorno show that markets and states both mattered, one integrating trade and the other trying to pull backward regions into the boom. Baby boom, refrigerators, cars, and television make affluence a transformation of households, streets, leisure, and expectations.

POSTSCRIPT: A Tale of Two Economies

Summary: The postscript contrasts western growth with the command economies of eastern Europe. Czechoslovakia, Poland, the GDR, Hungary, Romania, Comecon, heavy industry, coal, steel, collectivized agriculture, shortages, plan targets, hidden inflation, and the neglect of services show why early Communist growth looked impressive but stored up failure. Judt argues that eastern systems could mobilize labour and raw materials for a while, but they could not match western productivity, consumer supply, or adaptive investment. Source anchors: Czechoslovakia; Poland; GDR; Comecon; heavy industry; coal; steel; shortages.

Analysis: Czechoslovakia, Poland, and the GDR let Judt compare relatively advanced eastern economies with their western neighbours. Comecon, heavy industry, coal, and steel identify the production priorities that produced visible output while starving consumer sectors. Shortages are the evidence that plan fulfilment did not equal prosperity, which is why the postscript prepares the later chapters on eastern stagnation and cynicism.

XI: The Social Democratic Moment

Summary: Judt describes the high point of the western European social state in the 1960s. Social Democrats, Christian Democrats, trade unions, employers, Sweden, Britain, Austria, West Germany, France, public housing, health care, education, pensions, progressive taxation, full employment, Harold Wilson, and the managed mixed economy all appear as parts of a settlement in which redistribution and growth reinforced each other. The chapter treats social democracy broadly, as a cross-party practice of welfare capitalism rather than only the program of socialist parties. Source anchors: Sweden; Britain; Austria; West Germany; public housing; pensions; progressive taxation; full employment.

Analysis: Sweden, Britain, Austria, and West Germany show the range of institutions that could produce a social democratic outcome, and the chapter is a case for State Capacity because Judt measures the postwar state by what it could actually deliver rather than by its constitutional language. Public housing, pensions, progressive taxation, and full employment explain why citizens came to experience the state as a provider of security, which is the conversion of fiscal and administrative reach into routine household protection that state capacity names. Judt's cross-party treatment matters because Christian Democrats and conservatives often administered the same welfare compact that Social Democrats claimed as their own, so the settlement survived by becoming an execution capability shared across parties rather than an ideological property of one.

XII: The Spectre of Revolution

Summary: Judt turns to the revolt of the prosperous young: students, workers, feminists, antiwar activists, and New Left intellectuals. Nanterre, the Sorbonne, Paris in May 1968, Daniel Cohn-Bendit, de Gaulle, the French Communist Party, Italian universities, German students, Vietnam, sexual liberation, consumer society, and the bureaucratic university system form the chapter's core. The revolt is dramatic but ambiguous: it attacks capitalism, hierarchy, boredom, and old parties, yet it is made possible by the education and affluence of the postwar order. Source anchors: Nanterre; Sorbonne; May 1968; Daniel Cohn-Bendit; de Gaulle; Vietnam; sexual liberation; bureaucratic university.

Analysis: Nanterre, Sorbonne, and May 1968 place the revolt inside a very specific French institutional setting. Daniel Cohn-Bendit, de Gaulle, and Vietnam show that anti-authoritarian politics mixed campus grievances with global spectacle. Sexual liberation and the bureaucratic university help Judt explain the paradox: the social democratic state educated a generation that then found its institutions cramped, ugly, paternal, and hypocritical.

XIII: The End of the Affair

Summary: This chapter follows the Prague Spring and the end of reformist Communist hopes. Alexander Dubcek, Czechoslovakia, "socialism with a human face," writers, workers' councils, censorship reform, Moscow, the Warsaw Pact invasion of August 1968, Brezhnev, normalization, Jan Palach, and western Communist reactions all appear in Judt's account. The chapter shows that 1968 in the East was not the same as 1968 in Paris: Czechs and Slovaks wanted legal socialism and national dignity, and Soviet tanks made that hope impossible. Source anchors: Dubcek; Czechoslovakia; socialism with a human face; August 1968; Brezhnev; normalization; Jan Palach; censorship reform.

Analysis: Dubcek, Czechoslovakia, and socialism with a human face show that reform Communism still had local credibility before the invasion. August 1968, Brezhnev, and normalization explain the mechanism by which Moscow replaced persuasion with doctrine and occupation. Jan Palach and censorship reform make the cost personal and cultural, not only geopolitical, because the repression targeted a society trying to speak publicly again.

XIV: Diminished Expectations

Summary: Judt begins Part Three with the end of the boom and the harsher politics of the 1970s. The oil shock, stagflation, unemployment, Britain's 1976 IMF crisis, Italy's anni di piombo, the Red Brigades, Aldo Moro, the Baader-Meinhof group, the Provisional IRA, inflation, industrial conflict, and the weakening of postwar confidence all mark a Europe no longer protected by automatic growth. Terrorism and economic disappointment expose the limits of the welfare settlement without yet replacing it. Source anchors: oil shock; stagflation; 1976 IMF crisis; Red Brigades; Aldo Moro; Baader-Meinhof; Provisional IRA; inflation.

Analysis: Oil shock, stagflation, and inflation show why the economic tools of the previous decades stopped working. The 1976 IMF crisis gives Britain as Judt's clearest case of a state forced to admit limits. Red Brigades, Aldo Moro, Baader-Meinhof, and the Provisional IRA show violence moving into democratic societies that had promised stability, making the 1970s feel like a souring rather than a simple downturn.

XV: Politics in a New Key

Summary: This chapter tracks new political languages after the decline of old class alignments. Feminism, environmentalism, regionalism, Eurocommunism, Ostpolitik, the Helsinki Final Act, human rights, nuclear protest, women's rights, gay rights, Scottish and Catalan claims, Italian Communists, French Communists, West German Greens, and detente all appear as signs that politics was no longer organized only by labour, church, and nation. Judt treats these movements as responses to both affluence and disappointment. Source anchors: feminism; environmentalism; Eurocommunism; Ostpolitik; Helsinki Final Act; human rights; West German Greens; detente.

Analysis: Feminism, environmentalism, and West German Greens show new constituencies entering politics outside the old party machines. Eurocommunism and Ostpolitik reveal attempts to escape rigid Cold War identities without abandoning left or national interests. Helsinki Final Act and human rights are especially important because their language later gives eastern dissidents tools that Communist governments had formally accepted but could not safely honour.

XVI: A Time of Transition

Summary: Judt turns to southern Europe's escape from dictatorship. Portugal's Carnation Revolution, Spain after Franco, King Juan Carlos, Adolfo Suarez, the Spanish constitution, Greece after the colonels, Karamanlis, NATO, the European Community, land reform, workers' control, and fears of military reversal show democratization as negotiated, anxious, and international. The chapter argues that Portugal, Spain, and Greece joined postwar Europe late by turning authoritarian exhaustion into parliamentary legitimacy. Source anchors: Carnation Revolution; Franco; Juan Carlos; Adolfo Suarez; Spanish constitution; Greek colonels; Karamanlis; European Community.

Analysis: Carnation Revolution, Franco, and Greek colonels identify three different authoritarian exits rather than one democratic script. Juan Carlos, Adolfo Suarez, and the Spanish constitution show elite bargaining used to dismantle Francoism without a civil war. Karamanlis and the European Community show that democracy in the south was stabilized by the promise of European belonging and by the delegitimation of military rule.

XVII: The New Realism

Summary: This chapter follows the turn from postwar social democracy toward market discipline and fiscal restraint. Margaret Thatcher, privatization, trade unions, the miners, monetarism, Mitterrand, French nationalizations, the 1983 tournant de la rigueur, West Germany, unemployment, public spending, and the decline of old socialist confidence all appear. Judt compares Britain and France to show that governments of right and left both confronted the same constraints: slow growth, global capital, inflation control, and voters who wanted welfare without permanent crisis. Source anchors: Margaret Thatcher; privatization; trade unions; miners; Mitterrand; nationalizations; 1983; unemployment.

Analysis: Margaret Thatcher, privatization, trade unions, and miners make Britain the sharpest case of confrontation with the postwar labour settlement. Mitterrand, nationalizations, and 1983 show a socialist government retreating from its own early program under market pressure. Unemployment ties the two cases together, because Judt presents the new realism as acceptance that the old promise of full employment could no longer organize policy.

XVIII: The Power of the Powerless

Summary: Judt examines eastern European dissent before 1989. Vaclav Havel, Charter 77, KOR, Solidarity, Adam Michnik, Jacek Kuron, John Paul II, the Stasi, Hungary's goulash Communism, East German surveillance, Polish workers, samizdat, human rights, and the language of "living in truth" show opposition moving away from party reform toward civic honesty. The chapter's dissidents are weak institutionally, but they expose regimes by refusing official lies. Source anchors: Vaclav Havel; Charter 77; KOR; Solidarity; John Paul II; Stasi; samizdat; living in truth.

Analysis: Vaclav Havel, Charter 77, and living in truth give Judt the ethical core of eastern dissent, and the chapter is a case for Language and Ideology because the dissidents' weapon is precisely the regime's official language. Public truth-telling becomes political action because the Communist state governs through phrases, slogans, and ceremonial lies that Havel's greengrocer scenario exposes as the real constitution. KOR, Solidarity, and John Paul II show why Poland is different, with workers, intellectuals, and Catholic authority reinforcing one another outside the official vocabulary. Stasi and samizdat show both sides of the mechanism: surveillance tries to atomize society, while unofficial writing and networks rebuild trust by restoring words their public meaning.

XIX: The End of the Old Order

Summary: This chapter narrates the collapse of Communist rule in eastern Europe. Gorbachev, perestroika, glasnost, Poland's Round Table, Solidarity elections, Hungary's border opening, East German refugees, Leipzig demonstrations, the Berlin Wall, Czechoslovakia's Velvet Revolution, Vaclav Havel, Romania, Ceausescu, Bulgaria, and the Soviet refusal to repeat 1956 or 1968 all appear. Judt emphasizes how quickly regimes fell once Moscow stopped guaranteeing them and local publics tested the absence of force. Source anchors: Gorbachev; perestroika; Round Table; Solidarity elections; Leipzig; Berlin Wall; Velvet Revolution; Ceausescu.

Analysis: Gorbachev and perestroika explain the external permission structure for collapse, but Round Table and Solidarity elections show local agency in Poland. Leipzig, Berlin Wall, and Velvet Revolution show mass action spreading once fear weakened. Ceausescu marks the violent exception, helping Judt show that 1989 was peaceful not because regimes were gentle, but because most no longer had Soviet backing or internal confidence.

XX: A Fissile Continent

Summary: Judt opens the post-1989 part with political fragmentation after liberation. German unification, Kohl, Gorbachev, NATO, the Two Plus Four talks, the Soviet collapse, Russia, Ukraine, the Baltic states, Czechoslovakia's split, Yugoslavia, Slovenia, Croatia, Bosnia, and the question of borders show that the end of the Cold War released national claims as well as freedoms. Europe becomes fissile because old federal structures, imperial borders, and suppressed identities now have to be renegotiated. Source anchors: German unification; Kohl; Two Plus Four; Soviet collapse; Ukraine; Baltic states; Czechoslovakia; Yugoslavia.

Analysis: German unification, Kohl, and Two Plus Four show statecraft succeeding under pressure because all major powers accepted a fast German settlement. Soviet collapse, Ukraine, and Baltic states show a much larger imperial dissolution with weaker institutions. Czechoslovakia and Yugoslavia form Judt's contrast: one separation was negotiated, while the other turned borders, armies, and historical grievance into war.

XXI: The Reckoning

Summary: This chapter follows the costs of post-Communist transition and the wars of Yugoslav succession. Bosnia, Sarajevo, Srebrenica, Kosovo, Milosevic, Tudjman, NATO bombing, Dayton, privatization, corruption, Russian oligarchs, shock therapy, unemployment, Roma, women, pensioners, and disappointed workers appear as Judt's evidence that liberation did not automatically produce justice. The reckoning is economic, legal, and moral: societies had to face crimes, property transfers, impoverishment, and the weakness of European intervention. Source anchors: Bosnia; Sarajevo; Srebrenica; Kosovo; Milosevic; Dayton; privatization; shock therapy.

Analysis: Bosnia, Sarajevo, and Srebrenica force Judt to confront Europe's failure to prevent mass killing after it had made "never again" part of postwar identity. Kosovo, Milosevic, and Dayton show intervention arriving late and under American pressure. Privatization and shock therapy widen the reckoning beyond the Balkans, because post-Communist freedom also meant asset stripping, insecurity, and new inequalities.

XXII: The Old Europe and the New

Summary: Judt examines the enlarged European project after the Cold War. Maastricht, the European Union, NATO expansion, Poland, Hungary, the Czech Republic, the Baltic states, Romania, Bulgaria, Turkey, the euro, Brussels, subsidies, migration, agricultural policy, and anxiety over sovereignty show old western Europe trying to absorb new eastern members. The chapter treats enlargement as both a moral obligation after 1989 and a source of resentment among citizens who feared bureaucracy, competition, and diluted identity. Source anchors: Maastricht; European Union; NATO expansion; Poland; Hungary; Baltic states; euro; Brussels.

Analysis: Maastricht, European Union, and euro show integration becoming more ambitious just as voters became less deferential. NATO expansion, Poland, Hungary, and Baltic states show security and memory driving eastern accession: these states wanted guarantees against the Russian past. Brussels becomes the shorthand for a real institutional problem in Judt's account, because decisions that stabilized Europe also seemed remote from national electorates.

XXIII: The Varieties of Europe

Summary: This chapter turns from institutions to Europe's internal diversity. Britain, France, Germany, Spain, Belgium, Italy, Scandinavia, language politics, immigration, Muslims, regional identities, Catalonia, Scotland, Flanders, global cities, Berlin, London, Paris, and memory disputes show that Europe was not converging into a single culture. Judt treats variety as Europe's normal condition: integration had to coexist with local languages, welfare models, colonial legacies, and different memories of fascism, Communism, and empire. Source anchors: Britain; France; Germany; Catalonia; Scotland; Flanders; immigration; Muslims.

Analysis: Britain, France, and Germany let Judt compare national habits that persist inside common European institutions. Catalonia, Scotland, and Flanders show subnational identities becoming more assertive in the same decades that Brussels gained authority. Immigration and Muslims force the question of whether postwar Europe can extend its civic protections to populations not covered by older national myths.

XXIV: Europe as a Way of Life

Summary: Judt's final chapter defines Europe's achievement less as a superstate than as a public settlement. Welfare states, public transport, regulated markets, social insurance, human rights, abolition of capital punishment, secular politics, consumer comfort, environmental regulation, museums, schools, and European Union law mark a distinct way of life. He contrasts this Europe with the United States and with Europe's own violent past, arguing that postwar Europeans built an unusually humane order precisely because they remembered what unrestrained nationalism and ideological violence had done. Source anchors: welfare states; public transport; regulated markets; human rights; capital punishment; secular politics; European Union law; violent past.

Analysis: Welfare states, public transport, and regulated markets make Judt's Europe tangible in services and habits rather than slogans. Human rights, capital punishment, and European Union law identify the legal-moral boundary that separates the postwar order from older sovereignties. The violent past gives this way of life its reason for being: Europe is valuable because it was built against the memory of what Europe had done to itself.

Epilogue: From the House of the Dead

Summary: The epilogue returns to memory, especially the delayed centrality of the Holocaust. Judt discusses Auschwitz, Jedwabne, Vichy, German restitution, West German trials, Polish memory, French responsibility, anti-Semitism, Israel, Holocaust education, museums, memorials, and the way World War Two entered public history after decades of silence. He argues that Europe's postwar identity finally came to rest on recognition of Jewish destruction and collaboration, but that this recognition was late, uneven, and politically fragile. Source anchors: Auschwitz; Jedwabne; Vichy; restitution; West German trials; Polish memory; Holocaust education; memorials.

Analysis: Auschwitz, Jedwabne, and Vichy return the book to crimes that early reconstruction often muted, and the epilogue is a case for Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation because Judt treats the delayed admission of genocide and collaboration as the work that finally made postwar Europe legible to itself. Restitution and West German trials show institutional ways of acknowledging guilt, while Polish memory and Holocaust education show how hard that acknowledgement remained outside Germany, where the founding silences had done different political work. Memorials close the book because Judt sees public memory not as decoration but as a civic structure: postwar Europe ends when its founding silences can finally be named, which is exactly the conversion of buried experience into a shared, teachable record that knowledge preservation requires.
