Lakota America

Buy on Amazon — Lakota America

Place in the vault

This note belongs with Identity, Culture, and Social Formation, but its strongest use is as a case study in mobile Indigenous power, not as a generic "frontier" summary.

Detailed overview

Pekka Hamalainen writes Lakota America against the habit of treating Lakotas as a people who appear fully formed at the Little Bighorn and then disappear into defeat. He starts with 1776 as a double birth year: one nation in Philadelphia, another in the Black Hills. That opening lets him keep Custer, Crazy Horse, Sitting Bull, and the Seventh Cavalry in view while refusing to let the Battle of the Little Bighorn dictate the whole chronology. The book asks how a people who numbered no more than about fifteen thousand could command the North American interior, bend French, British, Spanish, and American ambitions around them, and keep remaking themselves without ceasing to be Lakota.

The book's answer is built from ecology, mobility, kinship, and selective incorporation. Lakota power grows first from the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ, the Seven Council Fires, and from a flexible kinship order in which thióšpayes, oyátes, wólakȟota, takúye, and the sacred hoop make belonging behavioral rather than strictly biological. Hamalainen keeps returning to river valleys because they are the real infrastructure of the story. The Mississippi, Minnesota, Missouri, White, Bad, Cheyenne, Grand, Yellowstone, Bighorn, and Little Bighorn valleys provide water, wood, winter shelter, corn, trade routes, bison access, and political meeting grounds. The open plains matter, but the book keeps correcting the romance of empty grassland by showing that Lakota expansion depended on controlling narrow riparian corridors.

The early chapters reconstruct a world in which European empires were small islands in a vast Indigenous ocean. Tiyoskate's 1695 journey to Montreal, Frontenac's role as Onontio, Pierre-Charles Le Sueur's trading posts, the Great Peace of 1701, Sauteur and Cree pressure, Mesquakie violence, and the French need for beaver all make Lakotas and their Dakota kin central before the United States is relevant. Hamalainen shows Lakotas learning to use imperial outsiders without surrendering to them. Guns, iron, French traders, Spanish merchants, American Fur Company posts, Fort Pierre, vaccines, annuities, and later agencies become resources that Lakotas try to pull into their own order.

The middle of the book traces the rise of a kinetic Lakota empire. Horses from the Pueblo Revolt's aftermath, the gun frontier from French and British trade, the Missouri Valley corn villages, Arikara and Mandan vulnerability, the Black Hills, the Sun Dance, the White Buffalo Calf Woman, and the formation of seven Lakota oyátes all become parts of one political ecology. Hamalainen is careful not to describe Lakota rule as a sedentary state. It is a system of movement: raiding, hunting, trading, adopting, gathering, dispersing, extorting, and making peace. It depends on horses, bison robes, captives, women processing hides and preserving kinship, men’s societies, shirt wearers, akíčhitas, councils, winter counts, and the ability to concentrate force quickly in a world where outsiders often could not even see the structure they were confronting.

The United States enters as another expansionist power whose plans repeatedly misread Lakota power. The Indian Office, Indian Removal, the Mexican-American War, the Oregon and California trails, Fort Laramie, cholera, the Horse Creek Treaty, Harney, Fort Snelling, the Dakota War, Sibley and Sully, Red Cloud's War, the Fetterman Fight, Fort Phil Kearny, Fort C. F. Smith, and the 1868 Treaty of Fort Laramie all show the same pattern: Americans assume treaties, forts, railroads, surveys, and reservations will make Lakotas legible and governable, while Lakotas treat those same institutions as instruments to be resisted, exploited, or folded into Lakota sovereignty. The book's strongest interpretive move is to show that coexistence lasted as long as Lakotas and Americans wanted different things from the same spaces; it broke when bison, railroads, gold, and reservation policy made those differences impossible to contain.

The final chapters turn from ascendancy to dispossession without making dispossession the whole story. Custer's Black Hills expedition, the Yellowstone surveys, the Great Sioux War, Little Bighorn, the illegal taking of Pahá Sápa, Crazy Horse's killing, Sitting Bull's surrender, allotment, the Ghost Dance, Wounded Knee, the Indian Reorganization Act, Pick-Sloan dams, Vine Deloria Jr., AIM, Wounded Knee 1973, the Black Hills case, and the Dakota Access Pipeline all belong to one long struggle over sovereignty. Hamalainen closes by insisting that Lakota history did not end with defeat. The same capacity for shapeshifting that carried Lakotas from woodlands to river valleys to horse pastoralism also sustains modern fights over land, water, ceremony, language, and treaty rights.

Core concepts

Frontier Systems, Empire and Periphery, National Identity, and Ecological Constraint are relevant here only when kept attached to concrete Lakota institutions such as Očhéthi Šakówiŋ, winter counts, Pahá Sápa, treaty rights, river valleys, and the bison economy.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

A Note on Terminology

Summary: Hamalainen explains why he uses "Sioux" as a cover term for the Lakotas, Yanktons, Yanktonais, Mdewakantons, Sissetons, Wahpetons, and Wahpekutes, the seven allied oyátes of the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ or Seven Council Fires. He notes that "Sioux" comes through French from the Ojibwe "Nadouessioux," a hostile term meaning snake or enemy, but that it remains common in English and is used by many modern Lakota oyátes. He distinguishes Dakota as a cover term for the four eastern peoples and names Hunkpapas, Minneconjous, Oglalas, Sans Arcs, Sicangus or Brulés, Sihasapas, and Two Kettles while also explaining his preference for names such as Odawas, Mesquakies, Wyandots, and Ho-Chunks. Source anchors: Sioux; Očhéthi Šakówiŋ; Seven Council Fires; Nadouessioux; Jan Ullrich; Hunkpapas.

Analysis: Sioux and Nadouessioux matter because the book begins by refusing innocent terminology: the common name carries French transmission and Ojibwe hostility inside it. Očhéthi Šakówiŋ and Seven Council Fires make Language and Ideology politically consequential by naming a confederated body that U.S. records often fragment into separate bands. Jan Ullrich and Hunkpapas show that exact names track kinship, place, and collective scale rather than decorating a history already defined in English.

Introduction: Dark Matter of History

Summary: The introduction begins with 1776 as the birth year of both the United States and a Lakota nation centered on the Black Hills, then jumps a century to the Little Bighorn, where Lakotas and Cheyenne allies kill more than two hundred soldiers of the Seventh Cavalry. Hamalainen argues that Custer, Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, Dee Brown, Wounded Knee, and the Little Bighorn have made Lakotas famous while also flattening their earlier history into a prelude to U.S. expansion. He proposes instead to recover Lakota America through winter counts or waníyetu iyáwapi, Iktómi, river valleys, the Missouri, French, Spanish, British, and American empires, and a people who moved from foragers to farmers to equestrian hunters to nomadic pastoralists. Source anchors: 1776; Little Bighorn; Sitting Bull; waníyetu iyáwapi; Iktómi; Missouri.

Analysis: 1776 and Little Bighorn let Hamalainen replace the usual U.S. national clock with a paired Lakota-U.S. chronology. Sitting Bull marks the danger of reading Lakota history backward from one famous battle. Waníyetu iyáwapi make Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation part of the book's method, because winter counts preserve a Lakota sequence of events that can reorganize the archive, while Iktómi and the Missouri supply adaptability and riverine space as the history's governing logic.

Chapter 1: A Place in the World

Summary: The first chapter follows Tiyoskate, a Mantanton Mdewakanton war leader, and Zhingobiins, a Sauteur chief, on their 1695 journey to Montreal to seek peace, trade, and French guns from Frontenac. Tiyoskate places beaver robes and twenty-two arrows before Onontio, asks the "master of iron" to become father to the Sioux, and dies in Montreal after contracting an alien disease. Hamalainen then reconstructs the iron frontier around Crees, Sauteurs, Wyandots, Jesuits, Mde Wakan, Mille Lacs, thióšpayes, wild rice, bison, Pipestone Quarry, Mesquakie and Mascouten violence, Perrot's Saint-Antoine and Bonsecours posts, Le Sueur, Fort L'Huillier, and the emergence of Sioux country as a central place in the western fur trade. Source anchors: Tiyoskate; Frontenac; twenty-two arrows; Mde Wakan; Le Sueur; Fort L'Huillier.

Analysis: Tiyoskate and Frontenac give the book its first model of Lakota diplomacy: emotional submission is used to create obligation, not surrender. The twenty-two arrows turn villages into claims on French goods and protection, while Mde Wakan anchors Sioux power in a homeland of lakes, rice, fish, maple, and bison rather than in an abstract tribal label. Le Sueur and Fort L'Huillier show how trade posts become contested instruments that Sioux use to pull European power toward themselves.

Chapter 2: Facing West

Summary: Chapter 2 starts far south with Po'pay's 1680 Pueblo Revolt, the destruction of Spanish New Mexico settlements, the flight to El Paso, and the release of horses into Native trade circuits that move north through Comanches and other nomads. At the same time, the Great Peace of Montreal in 1701 excludes the Sioux, Le Sueur leaves Fort L'Huillier, French goods dry up, Vaudreuil permits Lakes Indians to make war on Sioux, and the seven fires look westward for security. Sicangu winter counts record drowned bison under lake ice, stored meat, horses from Omahas in 1708, the Des Moines, Little Sioux, Floyd, Big Sioux, Red River, Sheyenne, James, and Missouri valleys, clashes with Otoes, Omahas, Poncas, Iowas, Arikaras, Cheyennes, and the convergence of guns and horses. Source anchors: Po'pay; Pueblo Revolt; Great Peace of 1701; šúŋka wakȟáŋ; eight valleys; Arikaras.

Analysis: Po'pay and the Pueblo Revolt matter because Lakota equestrian power begins with an anti-Spanish Indigenous revolution far from Lakota country. The Great Peace of 1701 sends Lakotas west as excluded peoples seeking new routes to survival, not as inevitable plains nomads. Šúŋka wakȟáŋ changes mobility through Technological Change, but the eight valleys and Arikaras impose Ecological Constraint: horses widen range while water, wood, winter shelter, corn, and resident peoples determine what land can sustain occupation.

Chapter 3: The Imperial Cauldron

Summary: At midcentury the Očhéthi Šakówiŋ dominates tall-grass prairies between the upper Mississippi and the Missouri while French, Spanish, British, and Native projects collide around it. Hamalainen describes refugee Omahas, Poncas, Otoes, and Iowas along the Missouri, Bourgmont's lower Missouri ventures, Pawnees and Otoes destroying a Spanish expedition, Osage and Missouria envoys going to France, and then the collapse of New France after Amherst takes Montreal in 1760. Oglalas seek Arikara corn, internal Oglala strife follows, Pontiac's War shuts trade, Lakota winter counts record fear and hunger, but 1771 attacks on Mandans, renewed British trade, Peter Pond, St. Louis, Spanish Louisiana, the Black Hills in 1776, North West guns, Cheyenne trade, and Spanish-Lakota diplomacy rebuild Lakota power. Source anchors: Očhéthi Šakówiŋ; Amherst; Arikara corn; 1771; Black Hills; North West gun.

Analysis: Očhéthi Šakówiŋ and Amherst show that Lakota fortunes are tied to imperial collapse as much as imperial support. Arikara corn explains why military power alone is insufficient: Lakotas need carbohydrates and riverine villagers even when they dominate them. 1771, Black Hills, and North West gun mark the recovery of momentum, combining raids, sacred geography, and improved weapons into a new Lakota position between Native rivals and European traders.

Chapter 4: The Lakota Meridian

Summary: Chapter 4 turns the Louisiana Purchase into an imperial fiction by showing that Napoleon, Jefferson, Monroe, Talleyrand, and the $15 million transfer of 530 million acres mattered little on the upper Missouri until Americans encountered Lakota control on the ground. Smallpox in 1801 and 1802, Loisel's Fort Aux Cèdres, Tabeau's reports, Black Buffalo, Mato-kokipabi, Arikara dependence, the phrase "kind of serf," Lakota control of corn and meat exchange, and the intimidation of Omahas and Poncas define the Missouri order. Lewis and Clark learn this at the Bad River in 1804, where Black Buffalo and the Partisan block their movement, while the War of 1812, Portage des Sioux, O'Fallon, Fort Snelling, Fort Pierre, the Yellow Stone steamboat, American Fur Company competition, Arikara removal, rats, vaccines, and buffalo robes make the Lakota Meridian a center of trade and power. Source anchors: Louisiana Purchase; Fort Aux Cèdres; Black Buffalo; Bad River; Fort Pierre; Yellow Stone.

Analysis: The Louisiana Purchase exposes the difference between paper sovereignty and actual power, since Lakotas still control the corridors Jefferson's country claims to own. Fort Aux Cèdres and Black Buffalo make the Missouri a chain of Chokepoints and Gateways where Lakota decisions determine who may move goods and where. Bad River, Fort Pierre, and Yellow Stone show U.S. posts and steamboats entering a Lakota-dominated commercial geography rather than automatically extending American command.

Chapter 5: The Call of the White Buffalo Calf Woman

Summary: Hamalainen opens with Ptesáŋwiŋ, the White Buffalo Calf Woman, bringing the sacred Buffalo Calf Pipe, Ptehíŋčala Čhaŋnúŋpa, to starving Lakotas and binding them to Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka, the buffalo, the sacred hoop, and seven ceremonies. The chapter then follows Lakotas west from the Missouri toward Pahá Sápa through the White, Bad, Cheyenne, Moreau, Grand, Cannonball, Heart, and Knife rivers, displacing Kiowas and Crows while allying with Cheyennes and Arapahos. It explains horse pastoralism, bison ecology, drought, blue grama and buffalo grass, cottonwood bottomlands, pemmican, winter counts, Sun Dance camps, huŋká adoption, Bull Bear, Smoke, shirt wearers, akíčhitas, wakíčuŋzAs, Red Cloud's White Owners society, and the emergence of a kinetic empire in the 1840s. Source anchors: White Buffalo Calf Woman; Pahá Sápa; Buffalo Calf Pipe; Sun Dance; shirt wearers; kinetic empire.

Analysis: White Buffalo Calf Woman and the Buffalo Calf Pipe supply Religion and Legitimacy for a people whose expansion could otherwise look only predatory. Pahá Sápa and the Sun Dance make sacred geography a political gathering system as well as drought refuge. Shirt wearers and the kinetic empire produce Polycentric Order without a centralized state, because councils, societies, camp police, war leaders, and ceremonial gatherings concentrate or disperse authority as circumstances require.

Chapter 6: Empires

Summary: Chapter 6 places Lakota expansion beside U.S. empire-building after John C. Calhoun creates the Bureau of Indian Affairs in 1824, Eli Whitney's cotton gin helps produce the Cotton Kingdom, Andrew Jackson turns Indian Removal into federal policy, and the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo transfers half of Mexico. The chapter then follows Oregon and California overlanders, They Fear Even His Horses petitioning Fort Laramie in 1846, the 1849 cholera disaster on the Platte, the 1851 Horse Creek Treaty, Thomas Fitzpatrick, David Mitchell, Cheyennes, Arapahos, Pawnees, Fort Laramie, Harney's 1855 massacre, the Grattan affair, Red Cloud, Crow wars, Pryor Creek, Dog Soldiers, Pikes Peak gold, Fort Union, Bear Ribs, Agent Twiss, and Captain Raynolds's Black Hills survey. Source anchors: Bureau of Indian Affairs; Indian Removal; Horse Creek Treaty; Fort Laramie; Harney; Raynolds.

Analysis: The Bureau of Indian Affairs and Indian Removal show the United States developing State Capacity through agents, removal machinery, forts, surveys, and treaty administration before it can impose those tools on Lakotas. The Horse Creek Treaty and Fort Laramie reveal the limit of that capacity because Lakota power still blocks routes to California and Oregon. Harney and Raynolds mark the coercive turn as massacre, reconnaissance, and fort-building begin replacing trade-and-gift accommodation.

Chapter 7: War

Summary: Chapter 7 begins with Lakota words, wólakȟota, sacred speech, and Twiss's 1859 warning that declining bison and white settlement could bring "all the horrors of Indian war." It then traces U.S. military science, Fort Randall, Hunkpapa attacks on Fort Union, Bear Ribs's rejection of annuities, the killing of Bear Ribs, Hunkpapa letters rejecting land travel, steamboat attacks, the Dakota War of 1862, Andrew Myrick's "eat grass" insult, Little Crow, Mankato executions, Sibley and Sully's campaigns, Killdeer Mountain, Sand Creek, Powder River fighting, the Fetterman Fight, Red Cloud, High Backbone, Sitting Bull, Fort Phil Kearny, Fort C. F. Smith, the Wagon Box Fight, Sherman, the Indian Peace Commission, Father De Smet, Fort Laramie in 1868, Article 16 unceded territory, Article 11 hunting rights, and the making of "much medicine." Source anchors: wólakȟota; Bear Ribs; Dakota War; Fetterman Fight; Red Cloud; Article 16.

Analysis: Wólakȟota shows War, Crisis, and Collapse emerging from the breakdown of a long diplomatic order rather than a Lakota preference for violence. Bear Ribs and the Dakota War trace different responses to U.S. pressure: severing upper-Missouri ties and revolt under starvation in Minnesota. The Fetterman Fight, Red Cloud, and Article 16 make the 1868 treaty a Lakota victory as well as compromise, because mobile force compels the United States to abandon forts and recognize territory and hunting rights.

Chapter 8: Shapeshifters

Summary: After the 1868 treaty, the United States tries to turn the Great Sioux Reservation into a managerial frontier of Quaker agents, Peace Policy assimilation, cattle annuities, Whetstone Creek, Cheyenne River, and Grand River agencies. Lakotas accept beef, guns, tools, and rations while rejecting plows, counting, fixed residence, and the reduction of their world to reservation categories; Crazy Horse and Sitting Bull articulate iwáštegla, going slowly with white men while preserving Lakota autonomy. Hamalainen follows Red Cloud's Powder River world, Winchester rifles, buffalo robe commerce, agency manipulation, friendly and hostile categories, Milk River and Fort Peck, Spotted Eagle, Sitting Bull, Gall, Northern Pacific surveyors, Fort Peck rations, Crow and Pawnee dispossession, the Republican Valley massacre, Mission Creek, Utes, Shoshones, and Red Cloud's demand for guns and ammunition. Source anchors: Peace Policy; iwáštegla; Crazy Horse; Fort Peck; Sitting Bull; Republican Valley.

Analysis: Peace Policy names the U.S. attempt to replace conquest with managed transformation, but Lakotas treat its supplies as treaty resources rather than civilizing tools. Iwáštegla, Crazy Horse, and Sitting Bull reveal a sophisticated middle position: coexist slowly, accept useful goods, and block the sale of land or abandonment of the hunt. Fort Peck and Republican Valley show that agency resources can support continued Lakota expansion against both the United States and Native rivals.

Chapter 9: Upside-Down Soldiers

Summary: Chapter 9 moves from early-1870s winter counts to the Yellowstone Expedition of 1873, where Custer, Sheridan, Sherman, Stanley, Northern Pacific surveyors, 1,451 soldiers, 353 engineers, 275 wagons, and more than 2,000 animals try to force a railroad survey through Lakota country. Sitting Bull, Gall, Crazy Horse, Winchester and Henry rifles, Pryor Creek, the Panic of 1873, Black Elk's vision, Saville, Camp Robinson, Custer's 1874 Black Hills expedition, Newton Winchell, Hidden Wood Creek, Sheridan's wish to make it hot for Lakotas, gold fever, Red Cloud Agency confrontations, corrupt Indian rings, Republican Valley hunting rights, Little Bighorn, Manypenny's 1876 commission, illegal Black Hills cession, Crazy Horse's death, Sitting Bull's surrender, McLaughlin, Ghost Dance, Wovoka, D. F. Royer, Spotted Elk, Hotchkiss guns, and Wounded Knee carry the book from military victory to catastrophic dispossession. Source anchors: Yellowstone Expedition; Black Hills expedition; Little Bighorn; Manypenny; Ghost Dance; Wounded Knee.

Analysis: The Yellowstone and Black Hills expeditions expose railroad-and-gold pressure that makes the 1868 settlement impossible to preserve. Little Bighorn is a tactical and symbolic Lakota victory, but Manypenny shows Crisis Governance converting military shock into coercive legislation and illegal land seizure rather than renewed treaty negotiation. The Ghost Dance and Wounded Knee complete the upside-down reservation order as soldiers, police, agents, and reformers treat ceremonies and kinship practices as security threats.

Epilogue: The Lakota Struggle for Indigenous Sovereignty

Summary: The epilogue follows Lakotas after Wounded Knee through winter-count silences, suicide, domestic violence, allotment, Bennett County, Red Cloud's 1909 death, Mount Rushmore on the Six Grandfathers, hidden Sun Dances, Ghost Dance persistence, Frank Fools Crow, John Collier, the Indian Reorganization Act, George White Bull, Rosebud and Pine Ridge politics, Pick-Sloan dams, Lake Oahe, World War II service, Vine Deloria Jr.'s Custer Died for Your Sins, AIM, Alcatraz, Mount Rushmore protests, Raymond Yellow Thunder, the Trail of Broken Treaties, Dick Wilson, Wounded Knee 1973, Russell Means, Leonard Crow Dog, Buddy Lamont, the 1975 Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act, the Black Hills case, Jane Spotted Tail, Whiteclay, Obama at Standing Rock, Dakota Access Pipeline, LaDonna Brave Bull Allard, Oceti Sakowin, Trump, and the 2018 Fort Laramie commemoration. Source anchors: Red Cloud; Indian Reorganization Act; Pick-Sloan; Vine Deloria Jr.; Wounded Knee 1973; Dakota Access Pipeline.

Analysis: Red Cloud and Pick-Sloan show dispossession continuing after open war through land policy, dams, and administrative control. The Indian Reorganization Act and Vine Deloria Jr. contrast federal redesign from above with National Identity rebuilt through Indigenous intellectual and political sovereignty. Wounded Knee 1973 and the Dakota Access Pipeline mobilize media, law, prayer, protest, and coalition-building to keep Pahá Sápa, Mníšoše, treaty rights, and Fort Laramie active as political facts rather than memorial claims.
