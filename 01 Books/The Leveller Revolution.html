Position in the vault

Detailed overview

John Rees writes the history of the Levellers as an account of organised political work inside the English Revolution, not simply as a genealogy of democratic ideas. The book opens with the public trial and execution of Charles I at the Banqueting House in Whitehall, then works backward through the London crowd politics, illegal print networks, gathered churches, Trained Bands, parliamentary factions, and New Model Army Agitators that made such an outcome possible. John Lilburne, Richard Overton, William Walwyn, William Larner, Thomas Lambe, Katherine Chidley, Henry Marten, Thomas Rainsborough, Edward Sexby, William Bray, William Eyre, and John Rede appear not as ornamental radicals but as organisers who moved petitions, presses, regiments, churches, taverns, and prison campaigns into contact with one another.

The book's central claim is that the Levellers became a political movement through practice. Rees follows them from the anti-episcopal crowds of Christmas 1641, where Lilburne and London apprentices confronted Thomas Lunsford's Tower appointment, into the collapse of Star Chamber censorship, the spread of secret printing in Bishopsgate, Southwark, Coleman Street, and Wapping, and the mobilisation of apprentices, women, gathered churches, and soldiers. The "Leveller" label becomes meaningful only after years of activity: illegal pamphlets for Prynne, Burton, and Bastwick; Katherine Chidley's defence of independent congregations; William Larner's printing shop at the Blackimore; Richard Overton's Martin Marpriest satires; and the petitions that tied liberty of conscience, legal reform, anti-monopoly claims, prison reform, and the House of Lords' veto into one programme.

Rees gives London a material role in the story. The City, suburbs, and liberties are not just background scenery; they are the infrastructure of the movement. St Stephen's Coleman Street, Bell Alley, the Windmill and Whalebone in Lothbury, the Saracen's Head in Friday Street, St Paul's Churchyard, the Tower Hamlets, Southwark, Moorfields, and the Honourable Artillery Company form a dense geography in which a pamphlet, sermon, petition, muster, tavern meeting, and ward crowd could reinforce one another. The book's strongest pages show how a short walking distance between a separatist church, a bookseller, a printer, a tavern, and a Trained Band company shortened the time between grievance and collective action.

The New Model Army becomes the second great arena of Leveller politics. Rees does not reduce the Agitators to pay and arrears, though he gives those issues real weight. The soldiers' demand for arrears, indemnity, freedom from pressing, and security against transportation to Ireland became political because it was fought through elected agents, petitions, printed manifestos, and debates about sovereignty. At Saffron Walden, Putney, Ware, Corkbush Field, Bishopsgate, Burford, Oxford, and the Guildhall trial of Lilburne, the same questions recur in different institutional settings: who may speak for the people, whether the Commons can bind the nation without new elections, whether the army must obey its officers when the settlement betrays its own declarations, and whether juries can judge law as well as fact.

The book also explains why victory became defeat. The Levellers helped force the crisis that ended in Pride's Purge, the High Court of Justice, the execution of Charles I, the abolition of the Lords, and the Commonwealth. Yet the republic that emerged was controlled by the Rump, Council of State, Cromwell, Ireton, Fairfax, and the "Silken Independents" whom Rees shows separating religious toleration from popular sovereignty. The arrests of Lilburne, Overton, Walwyn, and Thomas Prince in March 1649, the Particular Baptist petition against the Levellers, the women's sea-green petitions, the Bishopsgate and Burford mutinies, and Lilburne's treason trial at Guildhall show a regime using the same revolutionary language of public safety against the organisers who had helped bring it to power.

Rees closes by arguing against historians who dissolve Leveller organisation into a loose radical mood. He accepts that the Levellers were not a modern party with membership cards, but he insists that regular meetings, treasurers, local agents, weekly dues, named printers, coordinated petitions, shared colours, army contacts, repeated joint declarations, and recognisable enemies made them an organised political formation in seventeenth-century terms. The result is a book about revolutionary agency: how a minority rooted in London's lower middling sort, separatist churches, print shops, apprentices, and army ranks could exert pressure far beyond its numbers, and how its defeat still left channels into Baptist dissent, jury rights, republican memory, and later democratic language.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

A Note on the Text

Summary: Rees explains how he handles seventeenth-century documents before the narrative begins. He keeps original spelling and punctuation because the language of John Lilburne, Richard Overton, Katherine Chidley, William Walwyn, parliamentary orders, petitions, newsbooks, and court records is part of the evidence, but he makes limited changes where unmodernised text would seriously block a reader's understanding. The note asks the reader to meet the idiom of people writing more than 300 years earlier while still having enough editorial help to follow the political claims, legal formulas, and religious polemic. Source anchors: seventeenth-century documents; original spelling; punctuation; seriously impair; 300 years ago

Analysis: The note matters because Rees later relies on exact verbal textures from seventeenth-century documents, original spelling, and punctuation to show how activists made claims in their own idiom. Lilburne's legal defiance, Overton's satire, Chidley's scriptural examples, and Walwyn's tolerationist prose all depend on words that could be flattened by modern paraphrase. By warning that he intervenes only when readability would seriously impair comprehension, Rees makes Knowledge Preservation methodological: pamphlets, petitions, and trial speeches remain primary evidence rather than decorative quotation.

Introduction

Summary: The introduction begins at the Banqueting House in Whitehall in 1649, where Charles I was tried for treason against the English people, then moves through the abolition of the House of Lords, the declaration of the Commonwealth, the collapse of the national church, the execution of Archbishop Laud, and the explosion of pamphlets, newspapers, broadsheets, and ballads. Rees names John Harris and Richard Rumbold on later scaffolds, John Lilburne in Star Chamber prisons, Richard Overton at a secret press, Henry Marten in Parliament, and William Larner, Thomas Lambe, William Eyre, William Bray, William Browne, and John Rede in the second rank. He frames the Levellers as organised activists whose petitions, demonstrations, print runs, prison campaigns, and regimental contacts made popular politics a practical force. Source anchors: Banqueting House; Charles I; House of Lords; Commonwealth; John Lilburne; Richard Overton; Henry Marten; William Larner

Analysis: Banqueting House, Charles I, the House of Lords, and the Commonwealth give the book its destination, while John Lilburne, Richard Overton, Henry Marten, and William Larner identify the organisers whose earlier work has to be reconstructed. Rees uses the introduction to reject a purely doctrinal history of "Leveller ideas" and instead asks how people without state office made enough coordinated pressure to affect Parliament, the army, and London. The introduction also explains why the narrative is chronological: the Levellers' own capacities appeared through repeated confrontations, not from a finished programme announced at the start.

1. 'The Maddest Christmas That Ever I Saw'

Summary: The first chapter reconstructs the Christmas 1641 crisis in London after Charles I appointed Colonel Thomas Lunsford lieutenant of the Tower of London, giving a violent cavalier influence over the Mint, merchant wealth, and a symbolically explosive fortress. Panic over the Irish rebellion, Strafford's execution, Laud's imprisonment, the dismantling of Star Chamber and High Commission, and the Grand Remonstrance made Lunsford's appointment intolerable to City petitioners, apprentices, sailors, and future Leveller Richard Overton. John Lilburne appears in the Westminster crowd, Captain David Hide and Sir Richard Wiseman clash with demonstrators, Trained Bands gather near Whitehall and Westminster Abbey, and Charles's attempt to seize Pym, Hampden, Holles, Strode, and Haselrig drives the five members into Coleman Street and Guildhall protection. Source anchors: Thomas Lunsford; Tower of London; Irish rebellion; John Lilburne; Captain David Hide; Sir Richard Wiseman; five members

Analysis: Thomas Lunsford and the Tower of London make the chapter concrete because control of a fortress, the Mint, and City wealth turns court policy into an immediate London threat. John Lilburne, Captain David Hide, Sir Richard Wiseman, and the five members show how street action, parliamentary crisis, and armed authority converged before a named Leveller movement existed. Rees uses the Christmas events to show that later Leveller politics grew out of specific anti-court mobilisation in London, where apprentices, sailors, petitioners, printers, and Trained Bands learned that crowds could alter royal and parliamentary decisions.

2. The First Leveller, John Lilburne

Summary: Rees makes John Lilburne's early life and punishment the first personal bridge between illegal Puritan print and Leveller politics. Lilburne is imprisoned by Star Chamber for distributing the works of Prynne, Burton, and Bastwick, whipped, pilloried, and then turned into a public cause through William Larner's printing, Katherine Hadley's prison smuggling, apprentice petitions from Moorfields, and Oliver Cromwell's first Long Parliament speech. The chapter also gives Katherine Chidley a parallel path from Shrewsbury and St Chad's parish disputes to London separatist circles, Thomas Edwards's attacks, The Justification of the Independent Churches, William Kiffin, Thomas Lambe, tailors, feltmakers, buttonmakers, tentmakers, and women petitioners. Source anchors: John Lilburne; Star Chamber; William Larner; Katherine Hadley; Oliver Cromwell; Katherine Chidley; Thomas Edwards; William Kiffin

Analysis: John Lilburne's Star Chamber punishment turns a legal injury into a political identity because the whipping, pillory, and prison letters make his body a public text. William Larner and Katherine Hadley show the practical chain by which prison grievance became printed agitation, while Oliver Cromwell's first speech connects Lilburne's case to Parliament's anti-Laudian opening. Katherine Chidley, Thomas Edwards, and William Kiffin widen the chapter from one heroic prisoner to gathered-church networks where lay conscience, women's petitioning, and anti-clerical polemic became Leveller resources.

3. London, the Great Leveller

Summary: This chapter maps London as the Levellers' organising machine. Rees moves from the City gates, Guildhall, Exchange, St Paul's book trade, Coleman Street, Lothbury, Friday Street, Bishopsgate, Southwark, Wapping, Limehouse, Whitechapel, the Tower Hamlets, Moorfields, Finsbury Fields, Spitalfields, and St George's Fields to the institutions that made those places politically dense. He describes a metropolis of about 135,000 inside the City and roughly 350,000 with suburbs, where manufacturing, retail, wardmotes, Common Hall, the Court of Aldermen, gathered churches led by Thomas Lambe, John Goodwin, Henry Jessey, John Spilsbury, and Praise-God Barebone, secret presses run by Henry Overton, Rice Boye, Edmund Chillenden, Nicholas Tue, and Richard Overton, and the Honourable Artillery Company created overlapping networks. Source anchors: Coleman Street; St Paul's; Southwark; Wapping; Thomas Lambe; Honourable Artillery Company; Richard Overton; Common Hall

Analysis: Coleman Street, St Paul's, Southwark, and Wapping are not scenery in Rees's account; they explain how a pamphlet, petition, church meeting, and muster could move across the city quickly. Thomas Lambe's church, Richard Overton's print circles, the Honourable Artillery Company, and Common Hall make Informal Institutions operational because radicals gained access to speech, arms, elections, and crowds through overlapping habits of church, craft, ward, tavern, and militia life. The chapter's mechanism is spatial: London compressed religious dissent, craft labour, book production, municipal conflict, and military training into a walkable political environment.

4. Levelling by Print

Summary: Rees follows the Levellers through the breakdown of censorship and the building of print relationships. Star Chamber's 1637 press restrictions, episcopal control of imported books, the Stationers' Company seizure of Lilburne, the punishment of Burton, Bastwick, and Prynne, and Sir John Lambe's notes on separatist booksellers show the old machinery of control. The Long Parliament then opens a flood: titles rise from hundreds a year to thousands by 1642, newspapers multiply, presses expand, and Stationers hunt Nicholas Tue, William Larner, Richard Overton, and Henry Hills. Rees places Peter Cole, the Cloppenburg Press, Bell Alley, the Martin Marprelate inheritance, Samuel Chidley's memories, Giles Calvert, Henry Cripps, John Harris, Gregory Dexter, women street sellers, and penny pamphlets inside one distribution world. Source anchors: Star Chamber; Stationers' Company; Nicholas Tue; William Larner; Richard Overton; Henry Hills; Giles Calvert; Samuel Chidley

Analysis: Star Chamber and the Stationers' Company supply the coercive background for the Levellers' later insistence on print freedom. Nicholas Tue, William Larner, Richard Overton, and Henry Hills show that illegal printing was an organised craft risk, not an abstract belief in liberty. Giles Calvert, Samuel Chidley, women sellers, and cheap pamphlets turn Information and Coordination into movement infrastructure, carrying imprisonment, heresy fights, and parliamentary betrayal from authors into apprentices, soldiers, gathered churches, and street readers.

5. Civil War

Summary: The fifth chapter follows the radical war party as civil war makes London mobilisation decisive. Rees places the Committee for General Rising, Salters' Hall, Common Hall, the earl of Manchester, John Pym, Henry Marten, William Waller, Peter Cole, Thomas Paine, and the City Trained Bands in the fight against royalist pressure. After Waller's defeat at Roundway Down on 13 July, London crowds oppose peace negotiations, 5,000 people gather in Old Palace Yard crying "No Peace," Lady Brouncker's white-ribbon women demonstrate for peace, Waller's troops fire on protesters, and Pym creates a Council of War while excluding Marten. The chapter connects the Lines of Communication, Turnham Green, the relief of Gloucester, first Newbury, Thomas Prince's wound, and the City radicals Pennington, Mainwaring, Atkins, Warner, and Towse to the parliamentary war effort. Source anchors: Committee for General Rising; John Pym; Henry Marten; William Waller; Old Palace Yard; Lines of Communication; Gloucester; Thomas Prince

Analysis: John Pym, Henry Marten, William Waller, and Old Palace Yard show how the war party depended on popular force while fearing its independent direction. The Committee for General Rising and Lines of Communication give institutional form to London mobilisation, while Gloucester and Thomas Prince's wound show that radicals did not merely petition from safety but fought and bled for Parliament. Rees uses Marten's exclusion to mark a recurring pattern: parliamentary leaders drew energy from crowds and Trained Bands, then tried to keep the most republican organisers away from command.

6. The War, the Church and the State

Summary: Rees brings John Lilburne and Oliver Cromwell back together through the Eastern Association, where military reform, religious independence, and political distrust of Presbyterian settlement meet. Cromwell's troop, Hampden's warning about needing men of spirit, the earl of Manchester, Ironsides, James Berry at Gainsborough, William Packer's Anabaptism, Edward Sexby in the Grocers' Company, William Eyre from New England, and Lilburne's service under Colonel Edward King connect army recruitment to religious and social networks. The chapter also follows Westminster Assembly politics, the Dissenting Brethren, the Solemn League and Covenant of September 1643, the Committee of Both Kingdoms, Manchester's reluctance to fight the king, Tickhill Castle, Cromwell's accusations, and Lilburne's Just Mans Justification. Source anchors: Oliver Cromwell; Eastern Association; Ironsides; William Packer; Edward Sexby; William Eyre; Solemn League and Covenant; Tickhill Castle

Analysis: Oliver Cromwell's Eastern Association gives Rees a setting where military effectiveness and gathered-church confidence reinforce one another. William Packer, Edward Sexby, and William Eyre show that the army contained men whose religious independence and colonial or craft backgrounds made them receptive to later Leveller claims. The Solemn League and Covenant, Westminster Assembly, and Tickhill Castle make Religion and Legitimacy central because war leadership and church settlement could not be separated: officers who wanted to beat the king resisted a Presbyterian settlement that would discipline the sects who supplied their best soldiers.

7. The Coming of the Levellers

Summary: The seventh chapter shows the Levellers becoming recognisable in 1645 and 1646 through conflict with Presbyterians, censorship, and the House of Lords. Lilburne leaves the New Model after refusing the Covenant, William Larner and Thomas Prince return from war, Prynne and Bastwick reappear as Presbyterian opponents, Thomas Edwards hunts heresy, Katherine Chidley writes New-Yeares-Gift, Walwyn publishes The Compassionate Samaritane from Overton's secret press, and Nicholas Tue is arrested in Coleman Street with printing equipment linked to Thomas Lambe's General Baptist church. Richard Overton's Martin Marpriest series attacks Sir Symon Synod, Sir John Presbyter, Mr Persecution, tithes, censorship, and the Assembly of Divines, while A Remonstrance of Many Thousand Citizens brings Lilburne's prison image, Walwyn's toleration, Marten's republicanism, Norman yoke language, and annual elections into one text. Source anchors: John Lilburne; Solemn League and Covenant; Thomas Edwards; Katherine Chidley; William Walwyn; Nicholas Tue; Martin Marpriest; Remonstrance of Many Thousand

Analysis: Lilburne's refusal of the Solemn League and Covenant makes the break with Presbyterian discipline personal and public. Thomas Edwards, Katherine Chidley, William Walwyn, Nicholas Tue, and Martin Marpriest reveal the practical alliance between religious separatists, illegal printers, and anti-clerical writers. The Remonstrance of Many Thousand turns Language and Ideology into political construction because it fuses Lilburne's legal resistance, Walwyn's liberty of conscience, Marten's anti-monarchical pressure, and a demand that representation and sovereignty be remade.

8. Petitions and Prison

Summary: Rees turns 1646 and early 1647 into a study of petitioning under repression. City Presbyterians, Sion College, the Common Council, reformadoes, seamen, watermen, Langham, Warner, Gayre, Holles, Stapleton, and Essex form the conservative pressure around London, while Overton's An Alarum to the House of Lords attacks aristocratic privilege. Robert Eeles and Abraham Eveling raid Richard and Mary Overton's Southwark home, Newgate receives Overton, Magna Carta is confiscated, Mary Overton is later seized with Thomas Johnson in Maiden Lane, and her baby dies after Bridewell imprisonment. The chapter then follows Lilburne's City franchise fight at Guildhall, Henry Wansey, Thomas Adams, the Buckinghamshire and Hertfordshire petition of 11 February 1647, the Large Petition's thirteen demands, Thomas Lambe's house, John Glyn, Nicholas Tue, William Browne, and Henry Marten's reply to Rash Oaths Unwarrantable. Source anchors: Richard Overton; Mary Overton; Newgate; Magna Carta; Guildhall; Large Petition; Thomas Lambe; William Browne

Analysis: Richard Overton, Mary Overton, Newgate, and Magna Carta turn legal theory into household violence: Rees shows the Lords and Stationers reaching into a Southwark family to crush print organisation. Guildhall and the Large Petition widen the prison story into a constitutional campaign over voting, monopolies, tithes, trials, debtors, and poor relief. Thomas Lambe and William Browne connect this campaign to church meetings and prison solidarity, while Henry Marten's response shows that some MPs still served as parliamentary relays for demands generated outside Parliament.

9. Agitators

Summary: The ninth chapter explains how army grievance became a political revolt in 1647. The New Model resists disbandment, Fairfax and Cromwell share command, Holles, Stapleton, Thomas Adams, Skinner, Waller, Massey, reformadoes, Trained Bands, and the Militia Committee prepare a Presbyterian counterweight, and Parliament proposes to reduce the army while the king sits at Holmby after the Scots are paid. Walwyn's Poore Wise-mans Admonition urges soldiers, seamen, mariners, and citizens to distrust corrupt elections and City oligarchs. At Saffron Walden, Clotworthy and Waller confront the soldiers; Thomas Pride, Ireton, Holles, and the Declaration of Dislike enter the dispute; the cavalry and foot elect Agitators; Edward Sexby, William Allen, and Thomas Shepherd carry the Apologie to the Commons; and Joyce's seizure of the king follows the soldiers' decision to act through representatives. Source anchors: New Model; Holles; Saffron Walden; Thomas Pride; Edward Sexby; William Allen; Agitators; Holmby

Analysis: The New Model, Saffron Walden, Edward Sexby, William Allen, and the Agitators show why Rees treats the army revolt as more than unpaid soldiers demanding arrears. Holles and the Presbyterian plan to disband or redirect the army made material grievance inseparable from settlement politics. Thomas Pride and the elected agents made Polycentric Order visible inside the revolution, because rank-and-file representatives, officers, Parliament, City factions, and the king all became competing centers of political action after Holmby and Joyce's seizure.

10. Putney Church and Corkbush Field

Summary: Rees places Putney and Ware at the centre of the clash between Leveller constitutionalism and Grandees' control. After the army enters London, Fairfax holds the Tower, the Null and Void Ordinance reverses the Presbyterian coup, William Batten resigns, Thomas Rainsborough becomes vice admiral, John Warner becomes lord mayor, and Ireton's Heads of Proposals seeks a deal with Charles at Hampton Court. Rainsborough quarrels with Cromwell, Lilburne refuses Cromwell's offers in the Tower, new Agitators meet with London radicals, The Case of the Army Truly Stated circulates, John Wildman appears, William Clarke records the debates at St Mary's Church, and the Agreement of the People collides with Ireton's property-based franchise. The chapter then follows Ware and Corkbush Field, where Tobias Box, Thomas Lambe's hat papers, Samuel Chidley, William Bray, William Eyre, Robert Lilburne's regiment, Harrison's regiment, Fairfax, Cromwell, and Richard Arnold's execution define the defeat. Source anchors: Putney; Thomas Rainsborough; John Lilburne; Case of the Army; John Wildman; Agreement of the People; Corkbush Field; Richard Arnold

Analysis: Putney, John Wildman, and the Agreement of the People let Rees stage the Levellers' constitutional claims in direct speech against Ireton and Cromwell. Thomas Rainsborough and John Lilburne show two pressure points on the Grandees: one inside senior command and Parliament, the other from prison-linked civilian organisation. Corkbush Field and Richard Arnold's execution make State Capacity coercive at the decisive moment, because Fairfax and Cromwell restore discipline by force once debate threatens command, while William Bray, William Eyre, Tobias Box, and Thomas Lambe show how civilian and army Levellers had briefly made the Agreement an active regimental demand.

11. Counterrevolution

Summary: The eleventh chapter follows the reaction after Ware and the renewed danger of royalist, Scottish, and Presbyterian settlement. In February 1648 the Commons offers £100 for the author or printer of the blasphemous "New Testament" of the House of Commons and New Model council; Charles's agreement with the Scots, the Vote of No Addresses, Henry Marten's pamphlets, and the Personal Treaty question define the national crisis. At Well-yard in Wapping on 17 January, George Masterson, John Lilburne, John Wildman, and the Earnest Petition connect London organisation to treason charges, while George Masterson's testimony describes Whalebone meetings, Southwark and Wapping meetings, 30,000 petitions, weekly dues, treasurers Thomas Prince and Samuel Chidley, agents in Hertfordshire, Buckinghamshire, Kent, Oxford, Cambridge, and Rutland, and distribution through Barkstead's regiment. The chapter also traces The Moderate, Mercurius Militaris, John Harris, risings in Wales, Kent, Essex, and the North, the navy mutiny, Colchester, Marten's Berkshire regiment, William Eyre, and Major Fincher. Source anchors: New Testament; Vote of No Addresses; Well-yard; George Masterson; Whalebone; Thomas Prince; Samuel Chidley; The Moderate

Analysis: The New Testament pamphlet, Vote of No Addresses, and Well-yard meeting show how irreverent print, parliamentary votes, and underground organisation became one field of contest in 1648. George Masterson's Whalebone evidence is crucial because it exposes meetings, dues, treasurers, agents, and regimental distribution in unusually concrete detail. Thomas Prince, Samuel Chidley, The Moderate, and Mercurius Militaris show the movement adapting under counterrevolutionary pressure, keeping a public voice while royalist war, Presbyterian negotiation, and army politics all moved at once.

12. Revolution

Summary: Rees shows the Levellers and army radicals helping to force the final break with Charles in 1648. The Second Civil War brings Scots, Kentish rebels, Colchester, mutinous ships under Prince Charles, Royal Exchange recruitment by Lunsford, Maidstone, Kingston, Pontefract, supply-train attacks in Walbrook and Cheapside, Skippon's mobilisation, and petitions from Aldgate, Bishopsgate, Whitechapel, and Shoreditch. Lilburne is freed after a 10,000-person petition on 1 August, sends Sexby to Cromwell after Preston on 16-18 August, and joins Walwyn in the Large Petition of 11 September with 40,000 signatures demanding no treaty, safety above law, trial of the king, and economic relief against monopolies, excise, enclosures, debtors' prisons, and tithes. Ireton writes from Colchester, Agitators reappear, John Harris's Mercurius Militaris pushes anti-monarchical claims, Thomas Rainsborough is murdered at Doncaster, petitions flood Parliament and the army, and the Nags Head and Garter Inn meetings precede Pride's Purge on 6 December. Source anchors: Second Civil War; Preston; Large Petition; John Harris; Thomas Rainsborough; Nags Head; Garter Inn; Pride's Purge

Analysis: The Second Civil War and Preston make anti-treaty politics urgent because Charles and the Scots prove that compromise can reopen war. Lilburne's release, Sexby's mission, the Large Petition, and John Harris's newsbook show Leveller organisers pushing the army to treat popular safety, not royal negotiation, as the source of authority. Thomas Rainsborough's murder, the Nags Head meeting, Garter Inn talks, and Pride's Purge make Crisis Governance revolutionary: Leveller pressure helps drive the army and MPs toward regicide and purge, but the resulting power remains in the hands of officers and the Rump rather than the petitioners.

13. Defeat in Victory

Summary: The thirteenth chapter describes how the republic was born while Leveller demands were blocked. Lilburne wants an Agreement before the trial or execution of Charles, Nags Head and Windsor negotiations send Lilburne, Wildman, Maximilian Petty, Colonel Wetton, Ireton, Harrison, Marten, and Independents into a committee of sixteen, and the Whitehall debates begin on 14 December before Fairfax, Ireton, Harrison, Peter, Goodwin, Collier, Nye, Erbury, Lilburne, Overton, Wildman, and Walwyn. The Levellers object to the officers' Agreement at the Mews, the king is executed on 30 January 1649, the Lords are abolished, the Council of State and Rump take power, Sexby is promoted, Walwyn withdraws, and Lilburne goes to County Durham. Englands New Chaines Discovered, Captain William Bray, John Reynolds, John Nayler, Christopher Cheeseman, The Hunting of the Foxes, Daniel Axtell's arrests, Cromwell's threat to break the Levellers, the Tower prisoners, Particular Baptist opposition, sea-green women's petitions, Bishopsgate, Burford, Oxford, and Lilburne's Guildhall acquittal complete the defeat. Source anchors: Whitehall debates; committee of sixteen; Charles I; Englands New Chaines; William Bray; Daniel Axtell; sea-green; Guildhall

Analysis: The Whitehall debates and committee of sixteen show that Leveller language entered the settlement process at the highest level but could not control procedure, timing, or final adoption. Charles I's execution and Englands New Chaines mark the reversal: the enemy king is gone, yet Lilburne and his allies identify a new coercive power in the army and Rump. William Bray, Daniel Axtell, the sea-green petitions, and Guildhall show the scale of the 1649 confrontation, moving from mutiny and arrests to women's mobilisation and a jury victory that saved Lilburne's life but not the movement as an effective national force.

14. Lieutenant Colonel John Rede's Last Stand

Summary: Rees follows John Rede after the main Leveller defeat to show how revolutionary networks survived locally and religiously. Rede appears as governor of Poole amid conflict with Skutts, Bingham, Denis Bond, the Poole council, Presbyterians, Silken Independents, James Heane, George Joyce, Edward Sexby, John Gardner, Southsea Castle, Portland, and south-coast army connections. After removal, he helps build the Porton Particular Baptist church from Birdlymes Farm, where 85 brethren and sisters gather on 3 April 1653 and a circuit of 111 people from about twenty Wiltshire and Hampshire villages develops. Under the Restoration he faces the Tower in 1661, the Act of Indemnity, Act of Uniformity, Conventicle Act, Five Mile Act, Arlington's examination in 1665, Brockers Wood and North Bradley conventicles, a 1672 license fight, later Baptist assemblies, Monmouth-era disarming, and family memorials through Sarah Bernard, Sarah Rede, and Devizes trustees. Source anchors: John Rede; Poole; George Joyce; Edward Sexby; Porton; Act of Uniformity; Conventicle Act; Brockers Wood

Analysis: John Rede, Poole, George Joyce, and Edward Sexby let Rees trace Leveller influence beyond London pamphlets and the New Model's central councils. Porton turns Rede's postwar career into Collapse and Resilience evidence: defeat did not erase the movement's personnel, because some shifted into Baptist congregation-building, local magistracy, and dissenting survival. The Act of Uniformity, Conventicle Act, and Brockers Wood show the Restoration state treating those religious networks as political threats, while Rede's long life links Leveller-era army radicalism to later Nonconformist endurance.

15. The Levellers and the English Revolution

Summary: The final chapter turns to historical interpretation and defends the Levellers as an organised movement. Rees engages sceptical historians such as Mark Kishlansky, John Walter, Jonathan Scott, Diane Purkiss, and Michael Braddick, then draws on Austin Woolrych, Ian Gentles, Barbara Taft, Clive Holmes, Rachel Foxley, Blair Worden, Brian Manning, Christopher Hill, and Jason Peacey to renew the case for organisation. He recaps illegal printing by Lilburne, Larner, Hills, Overton, the Chidleys, Paine, and Walwyn; petitions with 30,000, 98,064, 20,000, and 10,000 claimed names or copies; the development from parliamentary alliance to win-the-war politics, Independency, Putney, Ware, and 1648 revolution; and contemporary references to a Leveller party by Denzil Holles, Charles II's correspondent, Kingdomes Scout, Henry Denne, Thomas Edwards, Sea-Green and Blue, The Moderate, and Francis White. Source anchors: Mark Kishlansky; Austin Woolrych; Rachel Foxley; William Larner; Putney; Denzil Holles; Sea-Green and Blue; The Moderate

Analysis: Mark Kishlansky, Austin Woolrych, and Rachel Foxley identify the historiographical dispute Rees has been building toward: whether Leveller action was only a loose radical current or a recognisable organisation. William Larner, Putney, Denzil Holles, Sea-Green and Blue, and The Moderate supply the evidence he thinks scepticism cannot explain away: printers, programmes, named enemies, repeated public labels, and coordinated interventions in Parliament, London, and the army. The conclusion matters because it makes the book's earlier close work cumulative, showing how churches, print, apprentices, petitions, and regiments became a political force without needing a modern party form.
