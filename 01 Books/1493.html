1493

Buy on Amazon — 1493

Position in the vault

1493 is the direct sequel to 1491 and one of the vault's most important world-history synthesis books. Where 1491 reconstructs the complexity of the Americas before Columbus, 1493 explains how the post-Columbian collision of continents created a new planetary system linking ecology, commerce, labor, disease, and empire.

Detailed overview

Charles C. Mann's central claim in 1493 is that Columbus did not merely connect two previously separate worlds. The encounter initiated the construction of a single, globally interlocked world-system whose ecological and economic effects still define modern life. Mann does not present globalization as a recent novelty with deep historical antecedents. He presents the Columbian Exchange and the linked trade systems around it as the beginning of the world we inhabit now: a world of biological remixing, coerced labor, commodity chains, state finance, migration, environmental upheaval, and the repeated tension between economic gain and social dislocation.

The book's organizing strength is that it never treats ecology and political economy as separate explanatory lanes. Tobacco, silver, malaria, sugar, potatoes, sweet potatoes, rubber, slavery, maroon societies, and Chinese demand all appear not as colorful side stories but as system-forming forces. Mann argues that after 1492 the world's separated biotas, labor pools, and trade circuits began to fuse. Plants and diseases moved with ships; labor regimes expanded to exploit new commodities; states were reshaped by revenue streams and strategic vulnerabilities opened by those movements. The "new world" Columbus created is therefore not simply the Americas under European rule. It is a newly integrated globe.

One of the book's most valuable features is its attack on narrow national or civilizational storytelling. Jamestown is not just an English colonial beginning; it is part of a wider Atlantic biological and commercial system driven by tobacco, indigenous dispossession, and the importation of labor and disease. The Pacific silver trade is not just Spanish imperial history; it is part of the formation of a Sino-American economic connection that redirects global demand. The Agricultural and Industrial Revolutions do not emerge in an insulated Europe; they are materially shaped by American crops and colonial extractions. Mann's world is one in which distant places repeatedly determine one another without the participants fully understanding what they are building.

The ecological side of the argument is just as forceful. Mann draws on Alfred Crosby's notion of ecological imperialism but extends it in a more explicitly global frame. American crops alter Chinese demography and land use. Mosquitoes and pathogens help shape labor systems and geopolitics. Sugar transforms landscapes, appetites, and slave demand across empires. Rubber eventually changes industrial possibility. The point is not that ecology explains everything; rather, it is that politics, commerce, and ecology continually co-produce one another. Empires do not just conquer territory. They rearrange species, disease environments, labor regimes, and consumption patterns.

The slave trade chapters are especially important because they prevent globalization from appearing as a neutral or merely innovative process. For Mann, the integration of the world was inseparable from vast coercion. Plantation systems, transatlantic slavery, demographic replacement, and maroon resistance are not regrettable side effects. They are constitutive features of the early global economy. This is why the book works so well in a vault concerned with empire and periphery: it repeatedly shows that commercial expansion is built on ecological disruption and human subjugation as often as on entrepreneurial energy.

At a deeper level, 1493 is a book about unintended consequences. Very few of the people moving tobacco, silver, crops, or slaves around the world understood the full system they were helping to construct. Yet that system cohered anyway. Mann is especially strong on feedback loops: silver to China, crops to demographic pressure, demographic pressure to ecological strain, ecological strain to political instability, and so on. This is one of the reasons the book links so well to state-capacity, collapse, and maritime-power works in the vault. It shows how tightly coupled systems create both productivity and fragility.

By the end, the book's argument has become civilizational rather than event-focused. Globalization is not a late twentieth-century aberration. It is the long consequence of post-1492 integration. Our supposedly familiar local landscapes, cuisines, labor expectations, and political economies are all artifacts of centuries of exchange, violence, adaptation, and environmental change. 1493 is thus one of the vault's best books for understanding modernity as a world-ecological construction.

Major people, societies, and motivations

  • Charles C. Mann: acts as a synthetic historian tying environmental history, Atlantic studies, Pacific trade, agrarian change, and slavery into one planetary frame.
  • Alfred Crosby and the ecological-imperialism line of thought: provide the crucial interpretive backdrop for how species transfer and environmental adaptation aided empire.
  • Colonial planters, merchants, and imperial states: seek profit, durable commodities, and strategic leverage, often without understanding the system-scale consequences of their actions.
  • Chinese silver demand and East Asian market structures: show that the making of the modern world was not Atlantic alone but deeply Pacific and Afro-Eurasian.
  • Enslaved Africans, maroons, and coerced labor systems: reveal globalization as an engine of forced mobility and resistance, not just exchange.

Major linkages

  • 1491: reconstructs the pre-contact worlds whose collision and dismemberment 1493 tracks afterward.
  • A History of Portugal and the Portuguese Empire: relevant for maritime empire-building, commodity routes, and imperial adaptation.
  • The Dutch Republic: links through commerce, finance, maritime scale, and the political consequences of long-distance trade.
  • Imperial Twilight: useful as a later-stage example of global commerce and imperial pressure interacting across long-distance systems.
  • Going the Distance: relevant where maritime movement, trade, and political systems interlock.
  • Seeing Like a State: useful for comparing state ambition with system complexity and unintended consequences.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Commodity Systems: tobacco, silver, potatoes, sugar, rubber, and maize convert biological transfers into durable commercial systems.
  • Ecological Constraint: disease environments, invasive species, soil exhaustion, monoculture, and landscape transformation shape political and economic outcomes.
  • Coercive Labor: plantation slavery, silver mining, sugar production, conquest labor, and maroon resistance make coercion central to global integration.
  • Financial Infrastructure: American silver and Asian demand turn extraction, mercury refining, and galleon routes into monetary world-formation.
  • Logistics and Throughput: ships, ports, ballast, galleons, mining flows, and biological hitchhikers make movement the book's practical mechanism.
  • Legibility: monuments, racial labels, colonial categories, and ecological baselines show how empires simplify the mixed world they create.
  • Collapse and Resilience: monoculture, disease, demographic shock, and ecological homogenization generate productivity while exposing systems to cascading failure.

Core concepts

  • Commodity Systems: tobacco, sugar, silver, and rubber are the book's exemplary chains — each converts a transferred biological or mineral resource into a durable commercial system that reorders landscapes, labor, and imperial competition, from the Virginia tobacco coast to Asian rubber plantations.
  • Ecological Constraint: disease environments (malaria and yellow fever), invasive species, soil exhaustion, monoculture, and landscape transformation decide who can settle, work, and dominate, making ecology a causal limit on political and economic history rather than a backdrop.
  • Coercive Labor: Potosí's forced Andean labor, Atlantic plantation slavery, sugar extraction, and conquest labor make coercion constitutive of global integration, while maroon and fugitive communities show that coercion also generates resistance and alternative order.
  • Financial Infrastructure: Spanish American silver refined with Huancavelica mercury and shipped via Manila turns distant extraction, transport, and taxation into a global monetary system that binds American mines to Chinese demand and state finance.
  • Logistics and Throughput: ships, ports, ballast soil, galleons, and mining flows are the book's practical mechanism — movement of goods, people, and organisms simultaneously connects economies, carries disease, and redistributes species.
  • Legibility: monuments, racial and caste labels, and colonial categories simplify the mixed world empires create, obscuring the ecological and demographic systems underneath heroic or tidy narratives.
  • Collapse and Resilience: Irish potato monoculture under blight, standardized plantations, and ecological homogenization raise yields and population while trading diversity for cascading fragility.
  • Technological Change: potatoes and Peruvian guano fuel the Agricultural Revolution and demographic capacity, while rubber enables industrial possibility — European technological advance is shown to rest on distant biological and mineral inputs.
  • Frontier Systems: maroon communities and fugitive landscapes (quilombos, forests, wetlands, mountains) show that terrain and autonomy can transform coercive peripheries into counter-geographies and negotiating zones.
  • Language and Ideology: the appendixes' treatment of labels for Europeans, Africans, Asians, and indigenous Americans (including chino, sangley, negro, and preto) shows that naming is a political act that classifies populations and shapes how global movement is narrated; the casta system proper appears in Chapter 8, not the appendixes.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Prologue

Summary: Mann begins with the tomato-garden anecdote so globalization feels tactile and ordinary before he scales outward. The tomato that seems local and natural carries a transcontinental history: wild relatives cluster in South America, domestication and selection move through Mesoamerica, and global circulation after Columbus makes the familiar garden into a record of planetary exchange. Mann then uses Alfred Crosby's ecological-imperialism frame and Hispaniola's demographic catastrophe to show that plants, insects, pathogens, and people moved together. Source anchors: tomato-garden anecdote; wild tomatoes; Mesoamerica; Alfred Crosby; Hispaniola; pathogens.

Analysis: The tomato-garden anecdote, wild tomatoes, Mesoamerica, Alfred Crosby, Hispaniola, and pathogens make Ecological Constraint the prologue's core mechanism. Mann turns the ordinary tomato into evidence that modern life rests on biological movement, not just trade or conquest. Crosby gives the movement explanatory force, while Hispaniola shows that ecological transfer carried lethal asymmetries. The prologue also prepares Legibility because the familiar garden hides the violent history that made it familiar.

Introduction: In the Homogenocene

Summary: The introduction defines the stakes of the whole book by describing a new era of ecological and economic mixing after 1492. Mann uses the Homogenocene frame to argue that formerly separate biotas, crops, animals, pathogens, and commercial circuits began to converge. He also stresses that homogenization did not produce equality: ecological mixing generated new inequalities, fiscal dependencies, demographic shocks, and regional vulnerabilities. Source anchors: Homogenocene; ecological mixing; economic mixing; biotas; fiscal dependencies; regional vulnerabilities.

Analysis: Homogenocene, ecological mixing, economic mixing, biotas, fiscal dependencies, and regional vulnerabilities turn globalization into ecological history rather than trade history alone. Mann shows that connection can homogenize species and commodities while intensifying inequality and instability. That mechanism links Ecological Constraint to Commodity Systems: the post-Columbian world grows more integrated because organisms, money, and goods move through the same channels.

Chapter 1: Two Monuments

Summary: Mann begins with La Isabela and the Columbus monument to show how empire and ecology jointly remade landscapes. The failed colony exposes the practical weakness of Spanish plans: hunger, water problems, labor demands, conflict with Taino communities, and the pursuit of gold all strain the settlement. Yet the colony still works as an ecological beachhead because livestock, crops, weeds, insects, rats, mosquitoes, and pathogens keep moving even when the human project falters. Source anchors: La Isabela; Columbus monument; Taino communities; gold; livestock; mosquitoes; pathogens.

Analysis: La Isabela, the Columbus monument, Taino communities, gold, livestock, mosquitoes, and pathogens make the chapter a strong anchor for Logistics and Throughput and Legibility. Mann treats colonial beginnings as fragile operating systems, not just legal or military events. The Spanish settlement struggles to feed and govern itself, but its biological cargo still remakes the Caribbean. The monument then simplifies that process into heroic discovery, obscuring the ecological and demographic system the colony actually launched.

Part One: Atlantic Journeys

Summary: The first part maps the Atlantic side of the new world-system through plantation logic, disease environments, and labor demand. Mann uses tobacco, malaria, yellow fever, sugar, Jamestown, and Caribbean settlement to show that Atlantic integration did not operate as commerce alone. Ships moved crops and people, but they also moved insects, pathogens, animals, soil, and expectations about land use. Source anchors: Atlantic; plantation logic; disease environments; tobacco; malaria; sugar; Jamestown.

Analysis: Atlantic, plantation logic, disease environments, tobacco, malaria, sugar, and Jamestown define the section's analytical machinery. Mann shows that Atlantic integration fused Commodity Systems, Ecological Constraint, and Coercive Labor rather than adding them as separate consequences. Commercial demand pulled land, disease, and labor into one system, which makes the Atlantic chapters crucial for comparing empire, ecology, and forced movement elsewhere in the vault.

Chapter 2: The Tobacco Coast

Summary: Mann uses Jamestown and tobacco to show how a single crop can reshape land use, labor, settlement patterns, and imperial ambition. Tobacco demand keeps settlers in Virginia, pulls ships across the Atlantic, and encourages continuous cultivation. Those ships and fields also move ballast soil, earthworms, livestock, honeybees, and weeds, while English expansion displaces Powhatan's polity and changes forest, field, and fallow cycles. Source anchors: Jamestown; tobacco; Virginia; ballast soil; earthworms; honeybees; Powhatan.

Analysis: Jamestown, tobacco, Virginia, ballast soil, earthworms, honeybees, and Powhatan show how Commodity Systems reshape landscapes before colonizers understand the system they build. Tobacco does not merely generate revenue; it sustains settlement, increases ship traffic, and changes land-use rhythms. The chapter also anchors Ecological Constraint because earthworms, bees, livestock, and weeds alter what the landscape can support. Powhatan's presence prevents the note from treating Virginia as empty space.

Chapter 3: Evil Air

Summary: Mann turns to malaria, yellow fever, and the disease environment that shaped labor systems, political outcomes, and imperial vulnerability in the Atlantic world. He treats mosquito-borne illness as an institutional force: European mortality affects settlement patterns, differential immunity shapes labor choices, and plantation zones emerge under biological constraints that rulers and planters do not fully control. Disease becomes a driver of extraction, slavery, and colonial state formation rather than background misfortune. Source anchors: malaria; yellow fever; disease environment; European mortality; differential immunity; plantation zones; slavery.

Analysis: Malaria, yellow fever, disease environment, European mortality, differential immunity, plantation zones, and slavery make Ecological Constraint central to the chapter. Mann destroys the habit of explaining early modern history only through ships, laws, and armies. Microscopic life helps decide who can work, settle, survive, and dominate. That disease logic also intensifies Coercive Labor because plantation systems respond to mortality and immunity by sourcing labor through racialized violence and forced movement.

Part Two: Pacific Journeys

Summary: The second part widens the story beyond the Atlantic and makes the Pacific trade in silver and goods indispensable to world formation. Mann links American extraction, Manila galleons, Asian demand, Chinese fiscal systems, and crop movement into one Pacific circuit. The point is not to add a side route to an Atlantic story, but to show that modern globalization depended early on American resources and Asian markets. Source anchors: Pacific trade; silver; Manila galleons; Asian demand; Chinese fiscal systems; crop movement.

Analysis: Pacific trade, silver, Manila galleons, Asian demand, Chinese fiscal systems, and crop movement make Financial Infrastructure and Logistics and Throughput central to this section. Mann refuses an Atlantic-only account of globalization. American extraction and Asian demand joined early, and the galleon route turned oceans into monetary and biological conduits. The section therefore links finance, ports, crops, and state vulnerability rather than treating silver as inert treasure.

Chapter 4: Shiploads of Money (Silk for Silver, Part One)

Summary: This chapter centers on silver, especially Spanish American silver flowing into China, to show how deeply the Americas were entangled with Asian monetary and commercial structures. Mann uses Zheng He and Ming maritime capacity to break European teleology, then turns to Potosi, Huancavelica mercury, Manila, and forced Andean labor to show how silver became the medium that bound distant economies together. Extraction, refining, transport, and taxation all make money physically and politically costly. Source anchors: Zheng He; Ming maritime capacity; Potosi; Huancavelica mercury; Manila; forced Andean labor; taxation.

Analysis: Zheng He, Ming maritime capacity, Potosi, Huancavelica mercury, Manila, forced Andean labor, and taxation make the chapter one of the book's strongest Financial Infrastructure anchors. Silver does not function as abstract treasure; it binds distant economies through mines, mercury, ports, ships, taxes, and coercion. The Potosi-Huancavelica system also anchors Coercive Labor because Andean bodies become the throughput of global money. Mann uses the Pacific crossing to show how rulers finance empire through geographies they only partly comprehend.

Chapter 5: Lovesick Grass, Foreign Tubers, and Jade Rice (Silk for Silver, Part Two)

Summary: This chapter deepens the Pacific story by turning from money to crops, especially tobacco, maize, sweet potatoes, and other American plants moving into China. Mann shows how imported crops altered taste, settlement, demography, land use, and political vulnerability. Tobacco demand pushed farmers toward profitable but soil-draining cultivation; maize and sweet potatoes made marginal land more usable; population growth then increased pressure on hillsides, forests, and fragile soils. Source anchors: tobacco; maize; sweet potatoes; China; land use; population growth; marginal land.

Analysis: Tobacco, maize, sweet potatoes, China, land use, population growth, and marginal land make this chapter a tight link between Commodity Systems and Ecological Constraint. Mann shows how crop movement becomes state history: new plants support people and profit, but they also intensify settlement pressure, soil exhaustion, deforestation, and vulnerability. The feedback loop matters more than the crop list. American plants alter Chinese political possibility because they change where people can live, what they can grow, and how much stress the landscape can absorb.

Part Three: Europe in the World

Summary: The third section examines how Europe's rise depended on exchange rather than internal genius alone. Mann uses potatoes, guano, rubber, colonial extraction, and industrial materials to show that European agricultural and industrial transformations relied on biological and mineral inputs from elsewhere. The section turns domestic European development into a global ecological and commercial story. Source anchors: Europe; potatoes; guano; rubber; colonial extraction; industrial materials.

Analysis: Europe, potatoes, guano, rubber, colonial extraction, and industrial materials make Technological Change inseparable from Commodity Systems. Mann shows that European development did not emerge from a sealed civilizational core. It drew calories, fertility, raw materials, and industrial inputs from colonial-biological circuits. The section therefore helps compare this book with vault notes on industrialization, finance, and empire because it exposes the distant inputs inside domestic growth.

Chapter 6: The Agro-Industrial Complex

Summary: This chapter links the Columbian Exchange to the Agricultural Revolution and industrial growth through transformed food systems and agrarian productivity. Mann uses potatoes, Peruvian guano, intensified cultivation, Phytophthora infestans, Irish monoculture, and scientific blight evidence to show that abundance and fragility grew together. American crops altered caloric efficiency and demographic capacity, while imported fertilizers and standardized plantings increased yields but exposed food systems to catastrophic disease. Source anchors: potatoes; Peruvian guano; intensified cultivation; Phytophthora infestans; Irish monoculture; blight evidence; demographic capacity.

Analysis: Potatoes, Peruvian guano, intensified cultivation, Phytophthora infestans, Irish monoculture, blight evidence, and demographic capacity connect Technological Change to Collapse and Resilience. Mann pushes against the idea that European agrarian progress was self-contained. Distant ecological incorporation raises yields and supports population growth, but standardization and monoculture reduce resilience. The chapter explains why productivity can become a vulnerability when a system trades diversity for throughput.

Chapter 7: Black Gold

Summary: This chapter uses rubber to show how a transplanted biological resource could transform industrial possibility on a planetary scale. Mann follows rubber from Amazon extraction and imperial competition to Asian plantation regimes, using Hevea brasiliensis, smuggling, violent extraction, monoculture rows, and development schemes to show how a species becomes an industrial system. Rubber's biological origin and industrial destination pull different regions into one commodity chain. Source anchors: rubber; Amazon extraction; Asian plantations; Hevea brasiliensis; smuggling; monoculture rows; development schemes.

Analysis: Rubber, Amazon extraction, Asian plantations, Hevea brasiliensis, smuggling, monoculture rows, and development schemes make this chapter a mature Commodity Systems anchor. Mann shows that globalization is not one moment but a recurring pattern: a species moves, enters commercial and imperial networks, and then transforms both economy and landscape. The chapter also links to Ecological Constraint because plantation order simplifies living systems into financeable rows while pushing agronomic risk onto local communities.

Part Four: Africa in the World

Summary: The fourth section turns most directly to human coercion, the slave trade, maroon societies, and the remaking of American and Atlantic demography through forced movement. Mann follows Africans, Native Americans, Europeans, plantation labor, conquest, sugar, and fugitive settlements to show that global integration depended on violence as well as exchange. The section refuses any triumphalist story in which enslaved Africans appear only as an afterthought. Source anchors: slave trade; maroon societies; forced movement; Africans; Native Americans; plantation labor; sugar.

Analysis: Slave trade, maroon societies, forced movement, Africans, Native Americans, plantation labor, and sugar make Coercive Labor central to the section. Mann shows that global integration did not merely enrich or connect people; it also seized, transported, and reorganized them. The section matters because it links coerced mobility to new social forms and resistance, preventing commodity history from hiding the human violence that made many Atlantic systems work.

Chapter 8: Crazy Soup

Summary: This chapter explores the slave trade and the demographic mixing of Africans, Native Americans, and Europeans in the Americas. Mann uses figures such as Juan Garrido and Estebanico, along with conquest, smallpox, sugar, and African transport, to show that Africans were present in conquest and colonial formation from the beginning. The Atlantic becomes a site of violence, adaptation, translation, and new social formations, not merely a route that later supplied plantation labor. Source anchors: Juan Garrido; Estebanico; conquest; smallpox; sugar; African transport; new social formations.

Analysis: Juan Garrido, Estebanico, conquest, smallpox, sugar, African transport, and new social formations make this chapter a strong Coercive Labor anchor. Mann widens exchange beyond voluntary movement: forced and constrained movement still generates new identities and political forms. The chapter refuses to separate global integration from systematic brutality because conquest, disease, and sugar demand interact. Africans appear not as a later add-on but as participants, victims, intermediaries, and agents inside the first generations of the post-Columbian world.

Chapter 9: Forest of Fugitives

Summary: Mann turns to maroon communities and fugitive landscapes to show that coerced systems generate counter-geographies. Quilombos, forests, wetlands, mountains, trade with colonial towns, and negotiated settlements allow escaped slaves and allied indigenous people to turn difficult terrain into political leverage. The chapter also shows that peripheries were not only exploited zones; they could become spaces of alternative order, bargaining, and durable autonomy. Source anchors: maroon communities; fugitive landscapes; quilombos; forests; trade with colonial towns; negotiated settlements; autonomy.

Analysis: Maroon communities, fugitive landscapes, quilombos, forests, trade with colonial towns, negotiated settlements, and autonomy connect Coercive Labor to Frontier Systems. Mann shows that coerced systems generate counter-geographies because terrain can raise pursuit costs, hide settlements, and create bargaining power. The chapter complicates empire/periphery analysis by showing that margins do not only receive domination; they can also host alternative order and stubborn autonomy.

Chapter 10: In Bulalacao

Summary: This final chapter brings the narrative into the present through Bulalacao, the Philippines, and the problem of invasive species versus belonging. Mann uses the "Bahay Kubo" garden song, introduced crops, Conservation International, water hyacinth, tilapia, land erosion, and household gardens to ask when an import becomes part of home and when it remains an ecological threat. The chapter connects modern globalization to the sixteenth-century template with faster movement and denser externalities. Source anchors: Bulalacao; Philippines; Bahay Kubo; introduced crops; Conservation International; water hyacinth; household gardens.

Analysis: Bulalacao, the Philippines, Bahay Kubo, introduced crops, Conservation International, water hyacinth, and household gardens make the final chapter the book's clearest bridge to present-day Ecological Constraint. Mann rejects both purity nostalgia and simple globalist optimism. Introduced crops can become beloved local practice, while invasive species can damage rivers, farms, and hillsides. The chapter's active question is governance: whether communities can absorb movement without losing the capacity to decide what belongs.

Appendixes

Summary: The appendixes matter because Mann is unusually careful about language, historical framing, and the difficulty of naming world-scale processes precisely. Appendix A, "Fighting Words," threads the terminological quicksand of its subjects: "Asian" vs "Oriental," the Parián's chino and the pejorative sangley in Manila, "European" as a late collective noun (people instead identified by nationality or region, or collectively as "Christian"), and race — noting that modern concepts of race did not exist at the dawn of globalization, that negro entered wide use only in the 1450s (Central Africans distinguishing negro for slaves from preto for free people), and Mann's own scheme of referring to people by geographic or ethnic origin, including "Indian" for indigenous Americans. Appendix B, "Globalization in Beta," answers why Fujian became the center of the silver trade: the fabled port of Zaytun, one bay north of Yuegang, was the eastern terminus of the maritime Silk Road and the site of a "first pass at globalization" reaching its apogee in the fourteenth century. Zaytun was a cosmopolitan, multi-ethnic emporium of Muslim trading families; when the Song fell to the Mongols in the 1270s, fleet superintendent Pu Shougheng betrayed the Song prince and delivered the city and its ships to the fledgling Yuan dynasty, after which Sunni-Shi'ite militias warred until Zaytun collapsed and its foreigners died or fled into the hills. Foreign trade resumed openly two centuries later not at Zaytun but at Yuegang to the south — and the Yuegang merchants of the silver age were descendants of Zaytun's trading families, agents "doing the work of the centuries." Source anchors: Fighting Words; Asian/Oriental; chino/sangley; Parián; European/Christian; race; negro/preto; Indian/Native American; Globalization in Beta; Zaytun; maritime Silk Road; Pu Shougheng; Song fall; Mongol/Yuan transition; Yuegang.

Analysis: Language, historical framing, Europeans, Africans, Asians, the chino/sangley labels, race, Indian/Native American, and the Zaytun-to-Yuegang story make the appendixes useful anchors for Legibility and Language and Ideology. Mann shows that naming is not neutral: categories let empires classify mixed or distant populations, assign status, and narrate movement in manageable terms, as with the loose, sometimes pejorative Spanish terms chino and sangley applied in the Parián. Appendix B extends the lesson backward in time by showing that the silver-trade "new world" grew out of an earlier, already-globalized Eurasian emporium, so the appendix pairs a claim about naming with a claim about the longue durée of trade. The appendixes therefore support the whole book by showing that the world created after 1492 was not only biologically and commercially mixed; it also forced people to inventory, label, and classify that mixture.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • The tomato anecdote is the best shorthand for the book's larger point that the familiar is often globally made.
  • Silver to China is one of the book's key arguments against Atlantic-only globalization narratives.
  • Tobacco and malaria are the strongest paired examples for Atlantic ecological-political co-production.
  • Potatoes, sweet potatoes, and maize in Eurasian and Chinese contexts are essential for thinking about demographic pressure and unintended ecological feedback.
  • Rubber is the clearest later example of how a biological transfer can become an industrial revolution input.
  • The maroon chapters are the strongest reminder that global integration was also a history of escape and counter-order, not only domination.
