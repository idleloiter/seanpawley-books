Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer.

Core concepts

  • Coercive Labor: "RED LEAVES" and the Jefferson service stories make domination visible through bodies captured, pursued, worked, or abandoned under customary authority.
  • Informal Institutions: Jefferson gossip, barbershop rumor, household service, village silence, and shipboard intimacy all operate through unwritten codes stronger than formal rule.
  • Language and Ideology: citations, civic obligations, "justice," lynching as protection, and nationalist talk repeatedly rename violence as honor, order, or necessity.
  • National Identity: the war stories use uniforms, accents, fatherlands, brotherlands, India, Ireland, England, America, and Jefferson memory to make belonging unstable after the Armistice.
  • Knowledge Preservation: photographs, censored letters, burial stories, childhood narration, and publication acknowledgments decide what survives after war, murder, and local silence.

Detailed overview

These 13 gathers Faulkner stories into three visible clusters: a war group that moves through France and Britain after the Armistice, a Yoknapatawpha and Jefferson group built around slavery, caste, gossip, and household violence, and a final set of European and seafaring pieces where Americans watch sex, religion, police power, and fantasy from the edge of other people's lives. The extracted source opens with a contents page naming Part I, Part II, and Part III; the body then begins with "VICTORY," which the local manifest omits even though the text clearly prints it before "AD ASTRA." The collection therefore works here as a sequence of fourteen real sections if the acknowledgment is included, or thirteen stories plus the final publication note.

The war stories are not battlefield heroics in any simple sense. "VICTORY" follows Alec Gray from Clydeside shipwright family to penal battalion, officer's pips, unemployment, begging, and selling matches in Piccadilly. "AD ASTRA" sets Sartoris, Comyn, Bland, Monaghan, a subadar, an American military policeman, and a captured German in a French cafe on the first night of peace, where Armistice celebration turns into drunken nationality, race, class, and grief. "ALL THE DEAD PILOTS" looks back from thirteen years after the war through photographs, letters, a dog, Captain Spoomer, Johnny Sartoris, Amiens, Cambrai, Camels, and the death notice sent to Mrs Virginia Sartoris in Jefferson. "CREVASSE" compresses the war into a patrol that literally falls through the French earth into an older grave of Senegalese troops.

Part II relocates death and coercion to Mississippi. "RED LEAVES" uses Three Basket, Louis Berry, Issetibbeha, Moketubbe, Doom, slaves, red-heeled slippers, a steamboat house, and a fugitive body servant to make the burial of a chief depend on the capture of a living man. "A Rose for Emily" turns Jefferson's civic memory into a locked house: Colonel Sartoris' tax dispensation, the Board of Aldermen, Homer Barron, arsenic, china-painting lessons, Tobe, and the upstairs bridal room all become pieces of the town's long refusal to read what it has seen. "A Justice" returns to Doom and the steamboat through Quentin Compson listening to Sam Fathers, so the Choctaw material is reframed as childhood inheritance rather than public ritual.

The Jefferson stories after that keep narrowing the distance between community talk and bodily harm. "Hair" lets a traveling salesman reconstruct Hawkshaw's annual April journeys, the Starnes mortgage in Division, Susan Reed's haircuts at Maxey's barber shop, and the final marriage that overturns the town's dirty assumptions. "That Evening Sun" uses Quentin, Caddy, Jason, Father, Mother, Dilsey, Nancy, Jesus, Aunt Rachel, and the ditch near Nancy's cabin to show how a child's narration can preserve terror without understanding the adult arrangements that create it. "Dry September" starts from a rumor about Minnie Cooper and Will Mayes and follows McLendon, Hawkshaw, Butch, a drummer, a barber shop, an ice plant, and an abandoned brick kiln into racial murder before ending in McLendon's violence at home.

Part III makes expatriate observation unstable. In "Mistral," the American walkers Don and the narrator follow signs around a dead fiance, Giulio, a priest's ward, the Cavalcanti household, a policeman disguised as a soldier, a cafe waiter, and the wind itself; they understand enough to flee, but not enough to intervene. "Divorce in Naples" turns George and Carl's shipboard intimacy into a comic and painful account of sexual initiation, jealousy, jail, the Prefecture, a messenger, the consul, and a return voyage toward Galveston. "Carcassonne" abandons ordinary plot for a garret above Luis' cantina in Rincon, where a man under tarred roofing talks with his skeleton while imagining a buckskin pony, Crusader horses, rats, Standard Oil property, Mrs Widdrington, Byron, and the desire to "perform something" beyond stillness.

Across the collection, Faulkner repeatedly lets institutions misname what they manage: the British Army turns Alec Gray's revenge into a citation, Jefferson turns Emily Grierson into a civic "obligation," Doom calls coercion "justice," McLendon calls lynching protection, and the Italian village lets the church, the police, and the cafe translate suspicion into silence. The concrete systems matter because they decide who gets counted as a person, a servant, a witness, a body, a joke, or a public problem. The stories do not join neatly into one plot, but they do share a method: each section traps a person inside a local code of honor, race, sex, class, office, or memory, then shows the cost in a body that cannot simply speak its way out.

Section-by-section notes

VICTORY

Summary: "VICTORY" begins after the war with Alec Gray arriving from the Marseilles express at the Gare de Lyon, passing through the Gare du Nord, Amiens, and Rozieres as French onlookers read him as an English "milord" with damaged eyes. The story then returns to Private 024186 Gray in a Scottish battalion, where a colonel, a sergeant-major, Sergeant Cunninghame, and a penal battalion turn the fact that he is "not old enough to shave" into discipline. Gray's Clydeside family includes Matthew Gray, old Alec, John Wesley, Jessie, a new baby Elizabeth, a Victoria Cross in a wooden box, and monthly letters about ships still going out of Clyde. In the trenches near Arras and later at a bridge under German pressure, Gray kills the sergeant-major, earns citations, becomes Captain A. Gray, M.C., D.S.M., returns home to Matthew, rejects the shipyard, joins the Officers' Association in London, loses his office job, begs with a hat, washes under an embankment, and finally sells matches in Piccadilly to Walkley on Christmas Eve. Source anchors: Marseilles express; 024186 Gray; Clydeside; Victoria Cross; penal battalion; Captain A. Gray; Piccadilly matches.

Analysis: "Marseilles express" and "Piccadilly matches" frame Alec Gray's collapse from military spectacle into public destitution, while "024186 Gray" and "Captain A. Gray" show how the Army gives him identities that sever him from Clydeside work. The Victoria Cross in Matthew's box first seems to authorize service, but the later M.C. and D.S.M. cannot buy a place in London or repair the break with the shipyard. The penal battalion and the murdered sergeant-major matter because Gray's official valor grows out of private revenge, so military language hides the mechanism that made him useful to the Army and unusable afterward.

AD ASTRA

Summary: "AD ASTRA" takes place on the night of the Armistice among airmen who had started as Americans but now wear British tunics, British wings, ribbons, and mixed allegiances. The narrator rides toward Amiens with Sartoris, Comyn, Bland, and a turbaned subadar who speaks of brotherhood, Oxford, color, and men moving in water; they meet Monaghan, an American military policeman, and a wounded German prisoner from Beyreuth. In the Cloche-Clos, French officers, a one-eyed officer, a patronne with broken cups from shellfire, Comyn of the Irish nation, Bland with his invented wife, Sartoris grieving a twin, Monaghan with torn-off insignia, the subadar, and the German argue about art, defeat, barons, fatherland, brotherland, India, Ireland, France, England, and America. The cafe erupts into a fight, the M.P. is thrown, the German is carried toward a brothel by Monaghan and Comyn, and Bland ends the night crying at a fountain for his "poor little wife." Source anchors: British tunics; subadar; Beyreuth; Cloche-Clos; patronne; Comyn; Monaghan; poor little wife.

Analysis: The British tunics, Comyn, Monaghan, and the M.P. make national identity look improvised rather than natural, while the subadar and the German from Beyreuth supply the only sustained language for what victory and defeat do to people. The patronne's broken cups keep the scene tied to French civilian loss, so the drunken cafe fight is not just servicemen behaving badly but an occupation of a wounded room. Bland's "poor little wife" turns his cynical performance back on him: after mocking Beyreuth, the subadar, and Monaghan, he becomes the proof of the subadar's claim that the surviving generation is already dead in some essential way.

ALL THE DEAD PILOTS

Summary: "ALL THE DEAD PILOTS" opens from thirteen-year-old snapshots of lean airmen beside wire, wood, canvas, brass, leather, and machines flown without parachutes, then contrasts them with later photographs beside newer aircraft with cowling, engines, slotted wings, and a saxophone-age public. The narrator, working at Wing Headquarters in 1918 with a mechanical leg and censoring squadron mail, recalls Captain Spoomer, Spoomer's K.G. uncle, Ffollansbye, Johnny Sartoris from Mississippi, Kit in London, a dog that eats at the men's mess, Amiens, Cambrai, Camel squadrons, and the gunnery sergeant. Sartoris humiliates Spoomer by stealing his uniform, dressing a drunken ambulance driver in it, and sending him back from Amiens while Spoomer returns in a woman's skirt and shawl; Sartoris is reduced and transferred to a night-flying Camel squadron. The story ends with the narrator opening a parcel to Mrs Virginia Sartoris in Jefferson, finding goggles, a watch, a buckle, Aunt Jenny's letters, and Major C. Kaye's awkward notice that Johnny died near Saint Vaast on July 4, 1918. Source anchors: thirteen years; Wing Headquarters; Spoomer; Sartoris; Amiens; Camel squadron; Mrs Virginia Sartoris; Saint Vaast.

Analysis: The thirteen years and Wing Headquarters give the story a documentary surface, but Spoomer, Sartoris, and the dog turn military order into farce before the death notice closes the joke. Amiens and the Camel squadron matter because Sartoris' revenge happens inside an actual retreat and an actual machinery gap, where bad planes and collapsing roads make private humiliation inseparable from military failure. Mrs Virginia Sartoris and Saint Vaast pull the story back to Jefferson and burial records, showing knowledge preservation at its thinnest: a spectacular life in the air survives only as censored letters, damaged belongings, and a badly written official condolence.

CREVASSE

Summary: "CREVASSE" follows a patrol skirting a barrage across shell craters, a churned wheat field, a canal, a barge bridge, chalk ridges, burning houses, and a line of shallow depressions while two men drag a wounded soldier who keeps muttering about his bonnet. A captain, a subaltern named McKie, a sergeant, rifle carriers, water bottles, trenching tools, a soiled map, contaminated canal water, dead-looking grass, and a French 1914 rifle all appear before the party recognizes that the strange ground is a buried killing place. The floor collapses beneath them, releasing the smell of rotted flesh and dropping the survivors into chalk caverns full of Senegalese soldiers from the May fighting of 1915, probably killed by gas while sitting upright in the tunnels. Fourteen men survive the fall, dig toward air through a fissure, drag the wounded man through the tunnel, and emerge to kneel while one reads from a Bible. Source anchors: barrage; wounded man; chalk ridge; French 1914; Senegalese troops; gas; fourteen men; Bible.

Analysis: The barrage and chalk ridge make the living patrol seem to be navigating a normal battlefield, but the French 1914 rifle and Senegalese troops reveal a lower layer of the same war buried inside the ground. The wounded man, who repeats that he is not dead, matters because his terror says plainly what the others keep doing by drill and map: they are walking over graves that can open at any moment. The Bible at the end does not resolve the scene; after gas, fourteen men, and the dead Senegalese in the cavern, it registers the only speech the survivors can manage after the earth itself has turned into a tomb.

RED LEAVES

Summary: "RED LEAVES" begins when Three Basket and Louis Berry cross the clan plantation to the slave quarters after Issetibbeha's death and find the cabins empty because the dead chief's body servant has fled the burial obligation. The story reconstructs Doom's rise from Mingo to "the Man" through New Orleans, the Chevalier Soeur Blonde de Vitry, Carondelet, General Wilkinson, a pregnant woman from a West Indian family, a steamboat deck house hauled twelve miles, slave breeding, Memphis traders, Paris, Pompadour's girandoles, and slippers with red heels that fascinate Moketubbe. Issetibbeha's corpse waits, horse and dog are tethered for burial, guests eat baked dog, succotash, yams, and barbecue, Moketubbe is carried in a litter with the red-heeled slippers, and the fugitive runs through creek bottoms, pawpaw thickets, mud, drums, ants, a cottonmouth bite, and six days of pursuit. The captured man is brought back hungry and thirsty, cannot swallow food or water, and is led from the well toward the grave where Issetibbeha, the horse, and the dog wait. Source anchors: Three Basket; Louis Berry; Issetibbeha; Moketubbe; red heels; steamboat; cottonmouth; well.

Analysis: Three Basket and Louis Berry speak of slavery as an inconvenience, which lets Faulkner show coercive labor through routine rather than surprise. Issetibbeha, Moketubbe, the steamboat house, and the red heels join wealth, imported status, and bodily decay: the shoes cannot fit Moketubbe, yet they organize his desire and the whole burial hunt. The cottonmouth and the well keep the fugitive's body central; after six days of pursuit, his inability to swallow is what the clan's "usage" finally produces from the man they need to send with Issetibbeha.

A ROSE FOR EMILY

Summary: "A ROSE FOR EMILY" begins with Miss Emily Grierson's funeral in Jefferson and the town's curiosity about a decaying house that no one but Tobe, her old manservant, has entered for years. The town remembers Colonel Sartoris' 1894 tax remission, the Board of Aldermen's failed visit, the crayon portrait of Emily's father, Judge Stevens and the lime scattered against the smell, the father who drove suitors away, and the three days Emily refused to release his body. Homer Barron arrives with the sidewalk construction crew, a yellow-wheeled buggy, matched bays, Elks' Club bachelor talk, Alabama cousins, a silver toilet set marked H.B., men's clothing, a nightshirt, arsenic labeled "For rats," and a final dusk entrance through the kitchen door. After Emily dies at seventy-four, the town breaks into the upstairs room, finds rose-colored bridal furnishings, Homer Barron's body in the bed, and an iron-gray hair on the second pillow. Source anchors: Miss Emily Grierson; Colonel Sartoris; Tobe; Homer Barron; arsenic; H.B.; iron-gray hair.

Analysis: Colonel Sartoris and the Board of Aldermen show Jefferson managing Emily as a legal exception before it ever understands her as a person. Homer Barron, arsenic, H.B., and the nightshirt make the town's marriage gossip materially wrong: the objects point to a bridal chamber, but also to murder and preservation. Tobe and the iron-gray hair matter because the house's secret survives through informal institutions of labor, silence, deference, and town memory; the servant exits as the town enters, and the final hair proves that Emily's isolation has been a lived arrangement, not merely a rumor.

A JUSTICE

Summary: "A JUSTICE" is narrated by young Quentin Compson after Saturday trips with Grandfather, Roskus, Caddy, and Jason to a farm four miles from town, where Sam Fathers works as a carpenter among barns, smokehouses, quarters, breast-yokes, wagon wheels, clay pipes, and tobacco. Sam explains that his old name was Had-Two-Fathers, given by the Choctaw chief Doom after the story of Doom's return from New Orleans with six black people, a gold coat, a gold salt box, a puppy, and poison hidden in bread. Through Herman Basket's account, Doom becomes the Man by removing the existing chief, the chief's son, and Sometimes-Wakeup; he then tries to haul a dead steamboat twelve miles from the river, bargains with white men, loses and regains black people, outmaneuvers Craw-ford over a woman, stages a cockfight, and confronts the birth of a "yellow" child. Doom names the child Had-Two-Fathers and orders Craw-ford and Herman Basket to build a palisade fence around the black man's cabin, which the black man later vaults while displaying another child above the fence. Source anchors: Quentin; Sam Fathers; Had-Two-Fathers; Doom; gold salt box; steamboat; Craw-ford; palisade fence.

Analysis: Quentin and Sam Fathers make the story a lesson transmitted across race, age, and property lines rather than a detached legend. Doom, the gold salt box, the steamboat, and Craw-ford show power operating through naming, poison, trade, sexual possession, and comic evasion, so the title's "justice" is never separable from the chief's control of bodies and stories. Had-Two-Fathers and the palisade fence matter because Sam's identity comes from an official naming that fails to contain the black man's continuing revenge, embodied in the second child lifted over the fence.

HAIR

Summary: "HAIR" follows Susan Reed, an orphan in the Burchett household, from her first frightened haircut in Maxey's barber shop to painted adolescence, the ten-cent store, hidden dates, a faked school record, and town gossip that men avoid mentioning in front of Hawkshaw. Hawkshaw, really Henry Stribling, watches Susan pass the window, gives peppermints and Christmas gifts, buys a sixty-dollar wristwatch he never gives her, and takes annual two-week April trips that Maxey, Matt Fox, and the traveling salesman narrator cannot explain at first. The narrator traces those trips to Division, where Stribling had been engaged to Sophie Starnes, cut her fevered hair, lost her hair-framed photograph in the mail, buried Sophie and Mrs Starnes, paid the Starnes mortgage from 1917 to 1930, cleaned the house, polished the stove, filled the woodbox, scrubbed the headstones, and planted an apple shoot. After the mortgage is marked "Paid in full. April 16, 1930," Gavin Stevens tells the narrator that Hawkshaw returned to Jefferson, married Susan Reed, and took her away with him. Source anchors: Susan Reed; Hawkshaw; Maxey's; Matt Fox; Sophie Starnes; Division; Paid in full; Gavin Stevens.

Analysis: Susan Reed and Hawkshaw look sordid to Maxey's barbershop because the men there know only the Jefferson surface of haircuts, painted clothes, and rumor. Sophie Starnes, Division, and "Paid in full" disclose the hidden discipline behind Hawkshaw's silence: his life has been organized around a dead fiancee, a mortgage, and annual maintenance of a house that is not his. Gavin Stevens' final news matters because it reverses the narrator's own assumption that Susan is only a failed substitute; Hawkshaw's marriage to Susan Reed is not innocent, but it is more deliberate and less predatory than the barbers' talk can imagine.

THAT EVENING SUN

Summary: "THAT EVENING SUN" is remembered by Quentin from Jefferson, where Monday laundry has changed from black women carrying bundles on their heads to motor cars from the city laundry. Fifteen years earlier, Nancy cooked for the Compsons while Dilsey was sick, carried washing through the ditch and fence, was struck by Mr Stovall after demanding payment, lost teeth, tried to hang herself in jail while pregnant, and feared Jesus, the short black man with the razor scar. Father walks Nancy home while Mother complains of being left alone, Caddy and Jason ask literal questions, Dilsey gives coffee, Aunt Rachel is named as a possible refuge, and Nancy says Jesus is in the ditch with a razor and has left a bloody hogbone by the lamp. Nancy lures Quentin, Caddy, and Jason to her cabin with talk of fun, a queen story, popcorn, and a barred door, but Father retrieves the children and leaves Nancy sitting with the lamp and fire while her not-singing sound follows them up the lane. Source anchors: Quentin; Nancy; Jesus; Dilsey; Mr Stovall; hogbone; ditch; not singing.

Analysis: Quentin's laundry memory establishes how much adult violence can be hidden inside routine household service, and Nancy's work for Dilsey and the Compsons makes that violence close rather than distant. Jesus, Mr Stovall, the hogbone, and the ditch provide the specific threats around Nancy: sexual exploitation by white men, retaliation by a black husband, and a landscape where danger has a fixed location that Father keeps denying. Caddy and Jason matter because their questions expose what the adults refuse to say; Nancy's not-singing sound then becomes preserved testimony when the family walks away.

DRY SEPTEMBER

Summary: "DRY SEPTEMBER" starts in a Jefferson barber shop during a bloody twilight after sixty-two rainless days, where a rumor about Miss Minnie Cooper and Will Mayes passes through Hawkshaw, Butch, a drummer, McLendon, an ex-soldier, and men trapped in stale pomade, lotion, and heat. Hawkshaw insists that Will Mayes, the ice-plant night watchman, did not assault Minnie, but McLendon, decorated for valor in France, gathers a posse and drives to the ice plant with an automatic pistol, handcuffs, and two cars. The men seize Will, beat him, ignore his pleas to Mr John and Mr Henry, take him toward an abandoned brick kiln, and Hawkshaw jumps from the moving car before the killing is completed offstage. Minnie Cooper then walks to the picture show in a pink voile dress with female friends, receives deferential attention from men on the square, laughs uncontrollably, and is taken home; at midnight McLendon returns to his neat new house and assaults his wife for waiting up. Source anchors: sixty-two rainless days; Miss Minnie Cooper; Will Mayes; Hawkshaw; McLendon; ice plant; brick kiln; pink voile.

Analysis: The sixty-two rainless days and barber shop heat make the rumor combustible, but Will Mayes' presence at the ice plant and Hawkshaw's insistence on his character are the only facts the mob has. McLendon converts Miss Minnie Cooper's vague story into action because his war decoration and pistol give local racism a military posture. The brick kiln, pink voile, and McLendon's wife matter because racial protection language does not end with the unseen murder; it rewards Minnie publicly and sends McLendon's private violence home.

MISTRAL

Summary: "MISTRAL" follows two American walkers, Don and the narrator, down an Italian mountain after finishing the last Milanese brandy and noticing a soldier with a bicycle turn onto a narrow path. At a shrine they meet a deaf man and his wife, learn of a funeral for a rich fiance, and hear the history of the priest's ward, Giulio Farinzale, Caporetto, a mother seen in a Milan brothel, repeated postponed weddings, a sudden draft call, and a sudden death. In the village, the bell tolls, the wind begins, the travelers stop at Cavalcanti's, see the soldier's bicycle, meet the soldier, the aunt, the priest, and the white-dressed girl, then retreat to a cafe where an atheist waiter hints that the soldier is a policeman and that parish changes occurred three years earlier. The narrator later finds the priest writhing by a wall, sees the hidden bicycle near a copse, drinks fiery liquor with Don, and walks into the mistral with the soldier and girl still beyond reach. Source anchors: Milanese brandy; Don; bicycle; Giulio Farinzale; priest's ward; Cavalcanti; atheist waiter; mistral.

Analysis: The bicycle first turns a casual mountain walk into a pursuit of hidden relations, and Don's reading of Giulio Farinzale, the draft call, and the sudden death supplies the story's detective logic. Cavalcanti, the priest's ward, and the atheist waiter show informal institutions split among church, family, police, and cafe knowledge, where everyone seems to know part of the crime but no one can make it public. The mistral matters because it turns recognition into flight; the Americans understand enough to sense danger around the priest and bicycle, but they leave the girl, the soldier, and the dead fiance inside the village's machinery.

DIVORCE IN NAPLES

Summary: "DIVORCE IN NAPLES" begins in a Naples cafe with Monckton, the bosun, Carl, George, the narrator, and three women after thirty-four days at sea, while George anxiously guards Carl's supposed virginity. The backstory brings George and Carl aboard at Galveston with a portable victrola, a cracked single record, imitation leather bags, freshly laundered white jackets, a Philadelphia childhood, Scandinavian-American shipwrights, Gibraltar, messboy duties, and nightly dancing on the after well deck above Texas cotton and Georgia resin. George leaves Carl with a gold-toothed woman, loses him, shouts "Porteus ahoy," is arrested as a political prisoner for stepping on the King's coin, sends a note through an Italian prisoner, and returns from jail to search Naples while Carl stays away. Carl comes back before daylight, bathes furiously, hides a garment through the porthole, works the bridge-deck brightwork, avoids the dock side, finally reconciles with George near Gibraltar, and later asks George near Tortugas to buy him pink silk teddybears when they reach Galveston. Source anchors: Monckton; bosun; Carl; George; Galveston; victrola; King's coin; pink silk teddybears.

Analysis: Monckton, the bosun, and the narrator treat George and Carl as comedy, but George's possessiveness around Carl gives the cafe episode the structure of a marriage breakup. Galveston, the victrola, and the repeated dancing show that their bond was made through shipboard routine before Naples introduces sex, police, and shame. The King's coin and pink silk teddybears matter because both are absurd material objects that mark Carl's passage: first George's helpless arrest over money and authority, then Carl's attempt to translate his experience into a gift he cannot quite name.

CARCASSONNE

Summary: "CARCASSONNE" places an unnamed man under tarred roofing in a garret above Luis' cantina in Rincon, where his skeleton talks back to him while he imagines riding a buckskin pony with blue-electric eyes and a tangled-fire mane into "the high heaven of the world." The room belongs in practical terms to the Standard Oil Company and Mrs Widdrington, and the darkness also contains rats, palm fronds, a ship's siren, tarred paper bedclothing, spectacles, Byron, Luis, and a sense that wealthy people own even darkness and silence. The man's mind keeps returning to Crusader images of Godfrey of Bouillon, Tancred, a Saracen Emir, a Norman steed cut in half, a chamfron, and bones knocking under the sea while his skeleton supplies unwanted facts. He wants to perform something bold, tragical, and austere, but the story ends with the horse and rider becoming a fading star against the grave figure of the Earth, his mother. Source anchors: buckskin pony; skeleton; tarred roofing; Rincon; Standard Oil; Mrs Widdrington; chamfron; Earth.

Analysis: The buckskin pony and skeleton split the speaker between heroic motion and bodily stillness, so the whole section happens inside a body that can imagine galloping but can only lie under tarred roofing. Rincon, Standard Oil, Mrs Widdrington, and the rats keep the fantasy from becoming pure escape; even the dreamer borrows someone else's garret and darkness. The chamfron, Crusader horse, and Earth matter because the speaker's desired performance depends on old martial and religious images, yet the ending reduces them to a small fading point inside a maternal, indifferent world.

ACKNOWLEDGMENT

Summary: "ACKNOWLEDGMENT" records permissions for several stories in the volume rather than adding another fiction scene. It names American Caravan IV for "AD ASTRA," The Saturday Evening Post for "RED LEAVES," The Forum for "A ROSE FOR EMILY," The American Mercury for "HAIR" and "THAT EVENING SUN," and Scribner's Magazine for "DRY SEPTEMBER." The note does not list every story in the extracted contents, but it fixes part of the collection's publication history across magazines and a literary annual. Source anchors: American Caravan IV; Saturday Evening Post; The Forum; American Mercury; Scribner's Magazine; DRY SEPTEMBER.

Analysis: American Caravan IV and The American Mercury place "AD ASTRA," "HAIR," and "THAT EVENING SUN" in periodical contexts before their gathering here, while The Forum and Scribner's Magazine attach "A ROSE FOR EMILY" and "DRY SEPTEMBER" to named first-publication venues. The Saturday Evening Post entry for "RED LEAVES" is especially useful because it ties one of the collection's Mississippi slavery stories to a mass magazine context rather than to the war cluster. The acknowledgment therefore functions as knowledge preservation for the book's assembly, not as a narrative ending.
