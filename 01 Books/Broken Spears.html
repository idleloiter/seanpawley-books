Broken Spears

Buy on Amazon — Broken Spears

Position in the vault

This note lives under the shelf hub Empire, Trade, and Maritime Worlds, and is read thematically alongside the Identity, Culture, and Social Formation map of content, because the book is primarily an account of a conquered people's identity, memory, and social formation. It pairs naturally with 1491 and 1493, which widen the same confrontation between indigenous Mesoamerican worlds and the Atlantic system that León-Portilla narrates from the inside.

Detailed overview

Miguel León-Portilla's book is built as an editorial recovery of Nahua testimony rather than as a conventional single-author narrative of the conquest. He gathers passages from the Codex Florentino, the Codex Aubin, the Codex Ramírez, the annals of Tlatelolco, the Cantares mexicanos, Alva Ixtlilxochitl, Chimalpain, and later Nahuatl petitions to let the fall of Mexico-Tenochtitlan be narrated by those who endured it. That method changes the center of gravity at once. Hernán Cortés, Pedro de Alvarado, and the Spanish brigantines still appear, but the decisive voices are Motecuhzoma, Cuitlahuac, Cuauhtemoc, the messengers to the coast, the anonymous authors of Tlatelolco, and the poets who sing over broken spears, burned temples, and the ruined market.

The book also does more than reverse sympathy. León-Portilla and the texts he assembles show a specific indigenous logic for understanding catastrophe. Omens blaze in the sky like a bleeding ear of corn; Quetzalcoatl is feared as a possible returning presence; Tezcatlipoca appears in disguise; sacrificial rites, auguries, and ritual speech shape how the first reports of ships, horses, arquebuses, and foreigners are processed. Motecuhzoma's paralysis is therefore not just political indecision. It is a failure of interpretation inside a world in which cosmic signs, divine history, and statecraft are still inseparable.

Once the Spaniards move inland, the narrative becomes relentlessly concrete. Tlaxcala, Cholula, Popocatepetl, Iztactepetl, Tezcoco, Xoloco, the Toxcatl festival, the Tlacopan causeway, the brigantines on the lake, and the siege of Tenochtitlan all appear from the side of people watching their own ceremonial and military order collapse under steel, cavalry, firearms, allied indigenous enemies, epidemic disease, and starvation. The famous scenes in the book are memorable not because they are generalized laments but because they preserve detail: severed hands, intestines spilling onto the patio, Spaniards fingering gold "like monkeys," dead bodies heaped at the canals, and smallpox sores covering the city.

León-Portilla is also careful not to flatten indigenous Mexico into a single political body. The Tlaxcaltecas are present as allies of Cortés and as rivals of Cholula and Mexico-Tenochtitlan. The Tezcocan material carries its own motives. The Tlatelolco account preserves local pride even inside defeat. That matters because the conquest does not appear here as a simple contest between Spaniards and "the Aztecs." It appears as a political shattering in which old enmities, tributary resentments, local ambitions, and Spanish violence all combine against the Mexica center.

The final sections are what make the book more than a conquest chronicle. The elegies on the fall of the city turn the destruction of Tlatelolco and Tenochtitlan into lyric memory, and the chapter on aftermath extends Nahua testimony far beyond 1521 into petitions, chronicles, letters to the Spanish king, and later compositions about daily coexistence with Castilians and their successors. The point is not merely that the conquered suffered. It is that they wrote, petitioned, translated, remembered, and kept producing Nahuatl texts after the conquest. Broken Spears therefore matters as a record of collapse and as evidence that indigenous historical consciousness survived the fall of the city itself.

Major people, societies, and motivations

This is a nonfiction editorial anthology, so the "characters" are historical actors, peoples, and sources whose motivations the texts and León-Portilla's framing make legible.

  • Motecuhzoma (Montezuma) — the last tlatoani to rule Tenochtitlan before its fall; the book's great tragic figure. His defining trait is interpretive paralysis: he waits, consults magicians, sends costly gifts, and hesitates because he cannot decide whether the strangers are gods to be greeted or enemies to be repelled. His speeches of welcome to Cortés rest on the belief that the visitors are returning gods.
  • Hernán Cortés — the Spanish captain-general; shrewd, courteous in his own idiom, and driven throughout by a hunger for gold that the Nahua narrators diagnose precisely. He wins indigenous allies, receives ceremonial regalia as if they were gifts, and later tortures the defeated lords to extract the treasure lost on the Night of Sorrows.
  • Tenochtitlan and the Mexica (Aztecs) — the imperial capital on the lake and the people who built it, whose political, ceremonial, and tributary order the conquest destroys. The book treats the city not as an undifferentiated foe but as the sum of nobles, priests, warriors, commoners, and four great quarters.
  • The Tlaxcaltecas — a confederation of four republics that resisted Mexica domination and, resenting their tributary and sacrificial status, became Cortés's indispensable native allies and instruments in the Cholula massacre and the siege.
  • Cuauhtemoc — Motecuhzoma's successor in the final defense; he leads the last resistance from Tlatelolco, surrenders with dignity, and is later tortured by the Spaniards for gold. His capture and imprisonment anchor the elegies.
  • Cuitlahuac — elected ruler after Motecuhzoma; he had warned against admitting the Spaniards, but dies of the smallpox epidemic during the siege.
  • Pedro de Alvarado ("The Sun") — Cortés's deputy, responsible for the Toxcatl massacre during Cortés's absence, and a central figure of the cruelty remembered by the Nahua sources.
  • The priests and poets — the tlamatinime (wise men), survivors, and cuicapicque (song poets) who preserved omens, speeches, annals, and elegies; and the anonymous Tlatelolco authors who produced the earliest prose account, written in 1528.
  • León-Portilla's recuperative project — the editor and his collaborator-translators (notably Ángel María Garibay) who assembled, arranged, and translated these Nahuatl voices as an alternative to the Spanish triumphal record.

Supporting figures include the interpreter La Malinche, the Tezcocan prince Ixtlilxochitl and his relatives, the chiefs Cacama, Itzcuauhtzin, Tlacotzin, and the warrior Tzilacatzin.

Themes and concepts to track

  • The conquered as historians: indigenous people are shown producing omens, annals, chronicles, petitions, songs, and plays that survive the defeat, so historical agency does not end with the fall of the city.
  • Ritual interpretation of catastrophe: the omens, Quetzalcoatl and Tezcatlipoca appearances, and sacrificial rites show a civilization trying to comprehend the unprecedented through inherited sacred categories.
  • The fracture of imperial order: Tlaxcalan, Tezcocan, Tlatelolca, and Mexica positions are kept distinct, so conquest appears as the collapse of a tribute empire through rival peripheries and external force rather than a clean two-sided duel.
  • The cost of the siege: epidemic disease, starvation, and waterborne warfare compound Spanish and allied military pressure in the destruction of Tenochtitlan.
  • The elegiac register: the closing songs transform urban ruin into lyric memory; the book takes its title from the "broken spears" refrain, which belongs to the Tlatelolca narrative and the epigraph rather than to the closing elegies themselves.

Core concepts

  • Knowledge Preservation: León-Portilla's editorial method, Nahuatl manuscripts, Tlatelolca testimony, elegies, petitions, and later colonial texts make survival of memory the book's central act.
  • Language and Ideology: omens, Quetzalcoatl expectations, Nahua speeches, Spanish legal idioms, and postconquest petitions show political reality being fought through inherited vocabularies.
  • Empire and Periphery: Mexico-Tenochtitlan, Tlaxcala, Tezcoco, Cholula, Tlatelolco, and the Spanish invasion reveal conquest as imperial collapse through rival peripheries and external force.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: omens, sacrifice, Tezcatlipoca, Quetzalcoatl, Toxcatl, sacred regalia, and elegy show authority and catastrophe interpreted through ritual categories.
  • Legibility: messengers, coast reports, gifts, watch stations, emissaries, and mistranslated signs show the Mexica center struggling to classify unprecedented information.
  • Collapse and Resilience: besieged, starving, then crushed, the conquered city nevertheless survives as memory, language, and writing, so defeat and endurance are bound together.
  • Moral Economy: the Aztec gifting and tribute economy, in which ritual reciprocity is met by Spanish greed and coerced extraction, shows the collapse of shared norms of exchange.
  • Coercive Labor: tributary demands, victims for sacrifice, and the mayeques and slaves of the Aztec social order, later replaced by colonial tribute and servitude, make coerced obligation a structural constant.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Foreword

Summary: J. Jorge Klor de Alva frames the book as an intervention against the rule that victors write history. He argues that for almost 450 years the conquest of Mexico had been told mainly through Spanish voices, producing false images of a tiny band of Spaniards defeating Mexico-Tenochtitlan, erasing indigenous endurance, and reducing native peoples to silence or mestizaje. He places Miguel León-Portilla's 1959 Visión de los vencidos, its later Beacon Press English life, its use by Roque Dalton, and its circulation in Mexico, the United States, and postcolonial debates into one history of recovery, showing why Nahua testimony matters for Cuba, Chicanos, and wider struggles over nationalism, ethnicity, and resistance. Source anchors: J. Jorge Klor de Alva; Visión de los vencidos; Mexico-Tenochtitlan; Roque Dalton; Cuba; Chicanos.

Analysis: The foreword defines the book as a political and historiographical act before the conquest narrative even begins. Mexico-Tenochtitlan and Chicanos show how the book's Nahua materials are made to speak both to sixteenth-century defeat and to later identities in Mexico and the United States. By tying Cuba and postcolonial recovery to León-Portilla's editorial labor, the foreword frames Knowledge Preservation as a way to displace a Spanish triumphal script, and it treats the survival of the vanquished voice as evidence of Collapse and Resilience.

Introduction

Summary: León-Portilla opens with the Spaniards' entry into Mexico on November 8, 1519, the meeting between Hernando Cortés and Motecuhzoma on the southern causeway near Xoloco, and the belief that the strangers might be Quetzalcoatl or other returning gods. He then states the book's governing move: alongside Cortés's letters and Bernal Díaz del Castillo's True History, he will present Nahuatl accounts written by the victims and survivors themselves, some as early as 1528. The introduction ranges from the libraries in Paris, Florence, and Mexico City to descriptions of Pedro de Alvarado's massacre, Indian views of horses as great "stags," and a compressed sketch of Olmecs, Teotihuacan, Toltecs, Tula, and pre-Columbian Mexico in order to place Tenochtitlan inside a much longer civilizational sequence. It also sketches Aztec society, including the pipiltin, macehualtin, and mayeques, and the tributary and sacrificial order over which Itzcoatl and Tlacaelel presided. Source anchors: November 8, 1519; Cortés; Motecuhzoma; Quetzalcoatl; Bernal Díaz; Tenochtitlan; Tlacaelel.

Analysis: November 8, 1519, Cortés, and Motecuhzoma establish the encounter that the whole book keeps revisiting from the defeated side. Bernal Díaz and the Paris and Florence manuscripts matter because León-Portilla explicitly positions his project against the long dominance of Spanish narrative authority. Quetzalcoatl and Tenochtitlan are equally central, since the introduction insists that indigenous misunderstanding of the Spaniards and the grandeur of the city they entered both require Knowledge Preservation if the conquest is to be understood as more than a Spanish military feat. The portrait of Mexica society, with its nobles, commoners, and coerced mayeques feeding a tribute capital, anchors Coercive Labor in the preconquest order being destroyed.

Chapter One: Omens Foretelling the Arrival of the Spaniards

Summary: The first chapter gathers the ominous signs that Nahua sources associated with the coming disaster: a flaming ear of corn in the eastern sky, a temple fire, lightning, boiling waters, a weeping woman, monstrous apparitions, and portents recorded in Sahagún's informants and Diego Muñoz Camargo. The chapter's own prefatory note stresses that the first thirteen chapters tell the conquest chronologically while the last sections move toward Tlatelolco and elegy, but the material itself is all dread and forewarning. Motecuhzoma's Mexico does not yet know the name Cortés, but it is already living under signs that transform the future conquest into a cosmic rupture before the first Spanish landing is even narrated. Source anchors: Sahagún; Diego Muñoz Camargo; flaming ear of corn; eastern sky; weeping woman; Motecuhzoma.

Analysis: Sahagún, Muñoz Camargo, and the flaming ear of corn matter because the chapter is less about objective prediction than about retrospective indigenous meaning-making — a case of Language and Ideology working through Religion and Legitimacy. Motecuhzoma and the weeping woman show that the conquest is being cast from the outset as something registered in ritual and emotional language, not just in military intelligence. The eastern sky anchor is important too, because the Nahua narrative begins with signs coming from the same direction as the Spaniards, binding cosmology and geography together before any messenger reports a ship, and so foreshadows Collapse and Resilience.

Chapter Two: First Reports of the Spaniards' Arrival

Summary: This chapter follows the first direct news from the Gulf coast: towers or mountains floating on the sea, pale men with beards, and the resulting fear in Motecuhzoma's court. Fernando Alvarado Tezozomoc's material presents Motecuhzoma consulting magicians and seers, imprisoning them when they cannot explain the signs, and then interpreting the newcomers through inherited expectations that Quetzalcoatl and other divine beings might return from across the water. The chapter also records the decision to send gifts and emissaries to the strangers, showing how the first political response to the Spaniards is filtered through prophecy, coercive consultation, and imperial ritual rather than through straightforward reconnaissance. Source anchors: Gulf coast; Fernando Alvarado Tezozomoc; Quetzalcoatl; magicians; Motecuhzoma; towers on the sea.

Analysis: The Gulf coast, the towers on the sea, and Quetzalcoatl matter because the Nahua center first encounters the Spaniards through rumor and inherited symbolic categories. Tezozomoc and the imprisoned magicians show that Motecuhzoma is demanding interpretation under pressure, but imperial power cannot force Legibility from signs that resist old categories — the coast remains a periphery the empire cannot read. By centering Motecuhzoma's consultations and gifts, the chapter shows how the empire's first response already mixes state hierarchy with theological confusion, binding Religion and Legitimacy to Empire and Periphery in the halting, fatal diplomacy that follows.

Chapter Three: The Messengers' Journeys

Summary: León-Portilla then follows the actual emissaries Motecuhzoma sends to the coast. In the Codex Florentino they are instructed to keep watch at Nauhtla, Tuztlan, and Mictlancuauhtla, to carry ensigns, costumes, and treasures, and to treat the strangers as possible gods. The chapter records the first close observations of the Spaniards: Cortés receiving the gifts, the firing of an arquebus to terrify the envoys, the "deer" without horns that carry men, the mastiffs, the armor, and the hunger of the foreigners for gold. The messengers' travel creates the first actual bridge between Tenochtitlan and the coast, but it only increases fear when they return. Source anchors: Codex Florentino; Nauhtla; Tuztlan; arquebus; deer without horns; Cortés.

Analysis: The Codex Florentino, the arquebus, and the hornless "deer" matter because this is the chapter where marvel becomes tangible military fact. Nauhtla and Tuztlan locate the watch system materially along the coast, showing the empire trying to see before it understands — a struggle for Legibility against an external periphery. Cortés and the messengers' return are crucial because once gold, firearms, and horses are reported by firsthand witnesses, Motecuhzoma can no longer dismiss the strangers as rumor, yet still cannot absorb them into an adequate political category, leaving the empire exposed through its own Empire and Periphery.

Chapter Four: Motecuhzoma's Terror and Apathy

Summary: Here the texts dwell on Motecuhzoma's disorientation after the coast reports. He sends prophets, magicians, and captives to the Spaniards; the captives are sacrificed so the supposed gods may drink blood, but the Spaniards recoil, spit, and refuse food sprinkled with blood. The same passage notes that the foreigners eat hens, eggs, tortillas, guavas, avocados, and prickly pears while the animals they ride are fed reeds and grass. The failed magical mission and the Spaniards' abhorrence of sacrifice leave Motecuhzoma and the city in a state of fear, because the ritual script by which gods should be propitiated has plainly failed to control these beings. Source anchors: sacrifice; prophets; magicians; guavas; avocados; Spaniards recoil.

Analysis: Sacrifice, the recoiling Spaniards, and the magicians matter because the chapter demonstrates a direct breakdown in Religion and Legitimacy: the ritual means by which gods were placated produce no recognizable response. Guavas, avocados, and hens are not incidental; they show the emissaries still trying to feed, host, and classify the foreigners through ordinary offerings even as those same foreigners reject blood and upset expectations. Motecuhzoma's terror therefore comes not from passivity alone but from the visible failure of sacrificial and magical means, an interpretive collapse at the center of Empire and Periphery.

Chapter Five: The Spaniards March on Tlaxcala and Cholula

Summary: This chapter narrates the Spaniards' inland progress through indigenous political space rather than empty terrain. Sahagún's informants describe the first fighting at Tecoac, the reception in Tlaxcala, and the alliance-building that turns Tlaxcalan enemies of the Mexica into Cortés's indispensable partners. The account then presents the march into Cholula and the massacre there, while León-Portilla's introduction contrasts the Sahagún version, which emphasizes Tlaxcalan hatred of Cholula, with Muñoz Camargo's defense of Tlaxcalan conduct through the story of the murdered envoy Patlahuatzin. The chapter is therefore as much about indigenous rivalries as about Spanish ferocity. Source anchors: Tlaxcala; Cholula; Tecoac; Sahagún; Muñoz Camargo; Patlahuatzin.

Analysis: Tlaxcala, Cholula, and Patlahuatzin matter because the chapter makes it impossible to narrate conquest as a simple Spaniard-versus-Aztec duel. Sahagún and Muñoz Camargo preserve incompatible local memories, which León-Portilla does not flatten away, and that editorial choice is crucial. Tecoac and the Cholula massacre show Spanish violence operating inside a polycentric field of empire and periphery of preexisting Mesoamerican enmities, so indigenous alliance politics become one of the mechanisms by which Tenochtitlan is eventually isolated. The Cholultecas' faith in their idol Quetzalcoatl as a protector ties the slaughter to Religion and Legitimacy.

Chapter Six: The Gifts of Gold: The God Tezcatlipoca Appears

Summary: Among Popocatepetl and Iztactepetl, Motecuhzoma's envoys meet the advancing Spaniards with golden suns, quetzal feathers, necklaces, and insignia. Sahagún's informants emphasize the Spaniards' delighted response: they smile, finger the gold, and hunger for it "like pigs." The chapter also recounts Tzihuacpopocatzin's failed attempt to pass himself off as Motecuhzoma and the later appearance of a mysterious drunkard taken for Tezcatlipoca, who predicts Mexico's ruin and deepens the fatalism at court. Gold, disguise, and divine apparition are all entangled in the same failed effort to halt the invaders. Source anchors: Popocatepetl; Iztactepetl; Tzihuacpopocatzin; Tezcatlipoca; golden suns; quetzal feathers.

Analysis: Popocatepetl, Tzihuacpopocatzin, and the golden suns matter because this is where diplomacy and greed are shown nakedly together. The Spaniards' response to quetzal feathers and gold tells the Nahua narrators what kind of beings they are facing, while Tezcatlipoca's apparition shows the court still searching for divine explanation after deception has failed. By juxtaposing the envoys' costly offerings with the Spaniards' animal hunger, the chapter gives indigenous testimony a precise diagnosis of the failure of Moral Economy: ritual tribute meets material appetite rather than returning gods, within an imperial field of Empire and Periphery and a sacred order of Religion and Legitimacy.

Chapter Seven: The Spaniards Are Welcomed in Tezcoco

Summary: The narrative then turns to Prince Ixtlilxochitl of Tezcoco and the tensions inside the Acolhua world. The Codex Ramírez describes the Spaniards' descent from the mountains, Ixtlilxochitl's friendly reception of Cortés, the prince's whiteness being noted with surprise, and the efforts to draw Tezcoco away from Mexica dominance. León-Portilla's introduction underscores that this source may overstate Cortés's actual visit to Tezcoco, but the political meaning is unmistakable: Yacotzin, Cacama, Ixtlilxochitl, and Motecuhzoma are weighing whether to resist or profit from the strangers' arrival. Source anchors: Ixtlilxochitl; Tezcoco; Codex Ramírez; Cortés; Yacotzin; Cacama.

Analysis: Ixtlilxochitl, Tezcoco, and Cacama matter because the chapter tracks the fragmentation of the imperial coalition before the Spaniards even reach Tenochtitlan — the internal dissolution of Empire and Periphery. The Codex Ramírez is important not only as evidence but as a reminder that later indigenous sources preserved partisan local memories. The baptisms and Yacotzin's reluctant conversion then show how dynastic politics are being pulled into the conquest through Religion and Legitimacy, so the coming fall of Mexico-Tenochtitlan is prepared by fissures among subjects and rivals, not only by Spanish arms.

Chapter Eight: The Spaniards Arrive in Tenochtitlan

Summary: Sahagún's informants describe the formal entrance into Tenochtitlan on November 8, 1519, from Xoloco through Ixtapalapa toward the city center. Motecuhzoma, nobles, knights, and attendants come out with flowers, garlands, necklaces, and precious ornaments to receive Cortés. The preserved speeches between Motecuhzoma and Cortés, the rich description of the meeting at Huitzillan, and the later greed for treasure stored in the city's houses all create a sequence in which hospitality, ceremony, curiosity, and danger coexist in one carefully choreographed encounter. Source anchors: November 8, 1519; Xoloco; Ixtapalapa; Huitzillan; Motecuhzoma; Cortés.

Analysis: November 8, 1519, Xoloco, and Huitzillan matter because the chapter anchors the iconic meeting in specific Mexica ceremonial geography. Motecuhzoma and Cortés are not here abstract leaders but performers in a scripted exchange involving flowers, gold, and formal speech, which is why the later seizure of treasure reads as such a deep betrayal of the reciprocal bonds of Moral Economy. Ixtapalapa and the noble entourage also matter because León-Portilla is preserving the city's grandeur at the exact moment the strangers, guests within its imperial order of Empire and Periphery, begin to plunder it.

Chapter Nine: The Massacre in the Main Temple during the Fiesta of Toxcatl

Summary: This chapter records Pedro de Alvarado's massacre during the Toxcatl festival while Cortés is away confronting Pánfilo de Narváez. Nahua accounts from Sahagún's informants and the Codex Aubin describe linked songs, drums, dancers, and richly dressed celebrants suddenly cut apart in the patio of the main temple. Arms are severed, heads split, entrails spill out, and the temple court turns from sacred festival space into a killing ground. The surviving Mexica retaliate by besieging the Spaniards in Motecuhzoma's palace, making the massacre the irreversible break in coexistence inside the city. Source anchors: Pedro de Alvarado; Toxcatl; Pánfilo de Narváez; Codex Aubin; main temple; Motecuhzoma's palace.

Analysis: Pedro de Alvarado, Toxcatl, and the main temple matter because the chapter makes clear that open war in the city begins with a ritual slaughter, not with some spontaneous native treachery — the violent destruction of Religion and Legitimacy. Pánfilo de Narváez and Cortés's absence help explain the timing, but the Codex Aubin and Sahagún material emphasize the victims' ceremonial vulnerability and the sensory horror of the attack. Motecuhzoma's palace then becomes the place where Spanish safety depends on the very sacred authority the massacre has destroyed, while the enraged retaliation marks the point at which the city turns against the empire of which it had been the center, Empire and Periphery.

Chapter Ten: The Night of Sorrows

Summary: León-Portilla next follows the Spaniards' disastrous nocturnal retreat from Tenochtitlan. After days of fighting, Cortés decides to flee at midnight in rain and darkness along the Tlacopan causeway, carrying portable bridges to cross the canals. A woman drawing water raises the alarm; Mexica warriors attack from canoes, from the causeway, and at the breaks in the road. Motecuhzoma and Itzcuauhtzin are dead, three-fourths of the army is lost, and the retreat later known as la noche triste turns Spanish escape into a scene of revenge and drowning. Source anchors: Tlacopan causeway; portable bridges; canals; Motecuhzoma; Itzcuauhtzin; la noche triste.

Analysis: The Tlacopan causeway, the portable bridges, and the canals matter because the chapter shows Tenochtitlan's urban and lacustrine defenses finally working against the invaders who had entered them so confidently, the city's own geography turning from vulnerability into Collapse and Resilience for the Spanish army. Motecuhzoma's death and Itzcuauhtzin's body frame the retreat as the end of one political order, while la noche triste becomes the Nahua counter-memory to Spanish triumph. The woman who sounds the alarm is equally important, because the city's defense is not just royal command but collective vigilance erupting at the exact instant escape is attempted within the imperial order of Empire and Periphery.

Chapter Eleven: The Siege of Tenochtitlan

Summary: The city briefly resumes ritual life after the Spaniards' departure, and Cuitlahuac is elected ruler, but that normalcy is shattered by the plague that spreads through Tenochtitlan for seventy days. Sahagún's informants describe bodies covered in sores, the inability to move or turn, and the death of Cuitlahuac himself before Cortés returns with rebuilt forces from Tlaxcala and Tezcoco. The chapter then shifts into the siege proper: brigantines, attacks by water and land, and a city trying to defend itself while already weakened by an epidemic previously unknown among its people. Source anchors: Cuitlahuac; plague; seventy days; smallpox; Tlaxcala; brigantines.

Analysis: Cuitlahuac, the plague, and the seventy days matter because they show that the defense of Tenochtitlan is undermined by biological catastrophe — epidemic disease — before Spanish brigantines finish the work, a disaster feeding Collapse and Resilience. Tlaxcala and Tezcoco remain crucial here, because the renewed assault is again built on indigenous alliance as much as on Spanish persistence, exposing the fissures of Empire and Periphery. The brigantines then signal the new tactical environment on the lake: once epidemic loss and waterborne assault combine, the city is fighting on terms its earlier victories after la noche triste could not sustain.

Chapter Twelve: Spanish Raids into the Besieged City

Summary: During the long siege, Nahua accounts focus on repeated localized clashes rather than one decisive moment. Fifteen Spaniards are captured and sacrificed at the Tlacochcalco while their comrades in the brigantines watch helplessly. The besieged city endures hunger, raids on the market of Tlatelolco, the burning of the temple, and the bizarre Spanish effort to use a catapult set on the market platform. Cuauhtemoc's final attempt to revive sacred warfare by clothing Opochtzin in Ahuitzotl's regalia and arming him with the fire-serpent arrow fails, and calm gives way to terminal exhaustion. Source anchors: Tlacochcalco; brigantines; Tlatelolco market; catapult; Cuauhtemoc; Opochtzin.

Analysis: Tlacochcalco, the brigantines, and the Tlatelolco market matter because the chapter presents siege warfare as a grinding contest of spectacle, hunger, and exhaustion that strips away the city's capacity for endurance, testing Collapse and Resilience to its limit. Cuauhtemoc's use of Opochtzin and Ahuitzotl's regalia shows the defenders still trying to draw military efficacy from dynastic-sacred symbolism even at the edge of ruin, a last appeal to Religion and Legitimacy. The catapult anchor is equally telling, because it exposes Spanish improvisation and awkwardness, reminding the reader that Nahua resistance, still holding ground within Empire and Periphery, was inflicting strain and fear even in a losing struggle.

Chapter Thirteen: The Surrender of Tenochtitlan

Summary: The final destruction is narrated through several indigenous sources. A blazing omen appears over the lakeshore before the end, Cortés watches from Aztautzin's house near Amaxac, and Cuauhtemoc is eventually captured and brought before the conqueror. Sahagún's informants, Alva Ixtlilxochitl, and Chimalpain all emphasize the tragic aftermath: corpses, silence, terror, and Cortés's brutal search for hidden treasure, including the torture of Aztec lords to extract gold. Cuauhtemoc's plea that Cortés kill him with his dagger distills the defeat into a single scene of exhausted royal dignity before triumphant greed. Source anchors: Cuauhtemoc; Aztautzin; Amaxac; Cortés; Chimalpain; torture for gold.

Analysis: Cuauhtemoc, Cortés, and the torture for gold matter because the surrender is not narrated here as a clean military transfer but as humiliation, extortion, and mourning. Aztautzin and Amaxac place Cortés physically above the city's last agony, while Chimalpain and Alva Ixtlilxochitl widen the memory beyond one source and preserve the captured king's final gesture toward death. The blazing omen returns the conquest to Language and Ideology, showing that the city's end is remembered as both a human defeat and the fulfillment of long-anticipated catastrophe, and the coerced extraction of gold reveals the imposed demands of Coercive Labor and the collapse of Moral Economy in the empire's final hours.

Chapter Fourteen: The Story of the Conquest as Told by the Anonymous Authors of Tlatelolco

Summary: León-Portilla then presents a separate Nahuatl narrative written in 1528 by anonymous authors from Tlatelolco. This account covers Cortés's arrival, the gifts of yellow and white metal, the entry into the palace at Tlayacac, the violence of the conquest, and the later course of war in briefer but still highly specific form. Its value lies not in neat agreement with the earlier chapters but in its independent Tlatelolca memory, preserving local pride, variant emphases, and details absent elsewhere. The account's very existence so soon after 1521 also demonstrates that alphabetic Nahuatl historical writing began almost immediately after the fall. Source anchors: 1528; Tlatelolco; Tlayacac; yellow and white metal; Cortés; Nahuatl.

Analysis: 1528, Tlatelolco, and Nahuatl matter because this chapter is León-Portilla's strongest evidence that the conquered began writing their own prose history almost at once. Tlayacac and the gifts of yellow and white metal show continuity with earlier episodes, but the altered emphases prove that indigenous memory was not singular even within the defeated population. By preserving a Tlatelolca version rather than harmonizing it away, León-Portilla makes Knowledge Preservation archival as well as tragic: multiple native historical communities are still contesting what happened, each speaking its own Language and Ideology from within a fractured Empire and Periphery.

Chapter Fifteen: Elegies on the Fall of the City

Summary: The book then turns from chronicle to poetry in three Nahua songs of sorrow. The Cantares mexicanos lament the loss of Tlatelolco and Tenochtitlan with images of fugitives crossing the lake, besieged kings taken prisoner and bound in chains, and only grief remaining where flowers and songs once flourished. These poems are not retrospective patriotic abstraction; they preserve the immediate emotional register of 1523 and after, when the city, market, temples, and ceremonial life had only just been destroyed. The conquest is rendered as bereavement in a voice that can no longer separate political defeat from aesthetic and sacred devastation. Source anchors: Cantares mexicanos; Tlatelolco; Tenochtitlan; fugitives on the lake; bound kings; flowers and songs; 1523.

Analysis: Cantares mexicanos and flowers and songs matter because this chapter carries the book's most sustained lyric condemnation of the fall. Tlatelolco and Tenochtitlan remain named places inside the poems, which keeps the lament concrete rather than generic; its imagery of fugitives on the lake and imprisoned, chain-bound kings is distinct from the "broken spears" refrain that gives the book its title, which belongs to Chapter Fourteen's Tlatelolca account and the epigraph. The 1523 dating is important too, because it shows that lyric mourning followed almost immediately on military defeat, turning urban destruction into durable Nahua memory, an act of Collapse and Resilience, and the survival of a conquered people's Language and Ideology rather than leaving conquest to prose chronicle alone.

Chapter Sixteen: Aftermath

Summary: León-Portilla closes by following Nahua writing beyond 1521 into a colonial and modern aftermath. He begins with ruined temples, markets, schools, and priests, then shifts to annals, petitions, songs, plays, translations, and letters that record daily coexistence with Castilians and later Mexicans. The chapter includes the 1556 petition of Pedro Tlacahuepantzin and native governors from Tlacopan, Iztapalapa, and Coyoacan to the king of Spain, the 1560 Huejotzingo tribute petition, an eighteenth-century Ajusco land-title testimony, the play "Dance of the Great Conquest," and the 1918 Nahuatl manifestos of Emiliano Zapata. The conquest's aftermath is therefore not silence but a literature of grievance, endurance, and altered self-description. Source anchors: Pedro Tlacahuepantzin; 1556; Tlacopan; Iztapalapa; Coyoacan; Zapata; Nahuatl petitions.

Analysis: Pedro Tlacahuepantzin, 1556, and the Nahuatl petitions matter because the chapter refuses the idea that conquest ended indigenous historical agency. Tlacopan, Iztapalapa, and Coyoacan show native political actors learning the legal and political language of monarchy while still describing Spanish abuses in their own terms, and the Zapata manifestos carry that language into the twentieth century. By ending with later Nahua compositions rather than with Cuauhtemoc's capture, León-Portilla makes the aftermath central to the book's meaning: the city fell, but the language, documentary habits, and will to accuse and remember did not, so Knowledge Preservation and Collapse and Resilience complete the story.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • Title image: "broken spears lie in the roads" is the refrain that gives the book its name; it appears in Chapter Fourteen's epic description of the besieged city and as the epigraph ("ELEGY FOR TENOCHTITLAN") before Chapter One, not in the Chapter Fifteen songs themselves.
  • Key dates: the omens burn during the year 12-House (1517, two years before the Spaniards landed); the ships are first sighted off the coast in the year 13-Rabbit (1518); conquest spans April 22, 1519 (Cortés lands) to August 13, 1521 (surrender, year 3-House, day 1-Serpent); the Tlatelolco prose account is dated 1528; the first elegy may date to 1523–24.
  • The omens (Chapter One): flaming ear of corn, Huitzilopochtli's temple fire, lightning striking Xiuhtecuhtli's straw temple, daytime fire streaming eastward, boiling lake, the weeping woman (Cihuacoatl), the mirror-bird, and the two-headed men.
  • Motecuhzoma's welcome speeches (Chapter Eight): preserved by Sahagún's informants and translated by La Malinche; they treat Cortés as a returning, expected lord.
  • The Toxcatl massacre (Chapter Nine): the "song linked to song" passage, the drummers' severed arms, and victims' entrails spilling onto the patio; the Codex Aubin adds Tecatzin's warning about Cholula.
  • Night of Sorrows (Chapter Ten): the woman drawing water raises the alarm; the Canal of the Toltecs is choked with corpses; the Spaniards' portable bridges are abandoned.
  • The siege and surrender (Chapters Eleven–Thirteen): seventy-day smallpox plague; Tzilacatzin, the Otomi warrior; Opochtzin's fire-serpent arrow; Cuauhtemoc's hand on Cortés's dagger; the branding and torture of the lords at Coyoacan for gold.
  • Independent Tlatelolca account (Chapter Fourteen): the oldest native prose, in the National Library in Paris (Manuscript 22), emphasizing Tlatelolca pride and Tenochtitlan's failures.
  • Aftermath documents (Chapter Sixteen): the 1556 royal petition, the 1560 Huejotzingo letter, the eighteenth-century Ajusco land testimony, the "Dance of the Great Conquest," the Zapata manifestos of 1918, and the poetry of Joel Martínez Hernández and Natalio Hernández Xocoyotzin.
