The Decadent Society

Buy on Amazon — The Decadent Society

Position in the vault

This note belongs in the vault's book layer as a contemporary diagnosis of rich-world stagnation, low fertility, political stalemate, cultural repetition, virtual sedation, and failed renewal. It is useful beside Path Dependence and Institutional Drift for Douthat's account of wealthy systems repeating inherited patterns after their original vitality fades, and beside Informal Institutions for his attention to family formation, work, parties, churches, media franchises, universities, platforms, and the everyday habits that keep a society either generative or stuck.

Detailed overview

Ross Douthat begins with the moon landing because Apollo gives him a benchmark for modern expectation. In 1969, NASA, the Cold War state, industry, Norman Mailer's mystical reading of the decade, Star Trek's frontier liberalism, and the broader promise of space age expansion made it plausible that humanity had entered the first act of a much larger story. The book's title comes from the contrast between that expectation and a post-Challenger, post-Apollo civilization that still has satellites, robots, exoplanet searches, smartphones, and CGI space films but no sustained outward leap.

Douthat defines decadence as prosperity without clear advance: economic stagnation, institutional decay, cultural and intellectual repetition, and diminished confidence at a high level of wealth and technical development. He borrows from Jacques Barzun's From Dawn to Decadence, but he refuses the simple claim that decadence automatically means imminent collapse. A decadent society can be rich, safe, long-lived, active, anxious, entertained, and politically noisy while still circling through the same ideas, franchises, conflicts, technologies, and policy failures.

Part 1 names the four horsemen: stagnation, sterility, sclerosis, and repetition. The stagnation chapter moves from Fyre Festival, Theranos, Uber, WeWork, and venture-capital unreality to median household income, productivity, workforce participation, Robert Gordon, Tyler Cowen, David Graeber, Peter Thiel, Mark Steyn, antibiotics, AI winter, and the lost technological sublime. The sterility chapter reads Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale and P. D. James's Children of Men against subreplacement fertility in the European Union, Japan, South Korea, China, Singapore, Canada, Australia, and the United States. Sclerosis turns to Congress, Trump, the GOP, the European Union, the euro, Greece, Angela Merkel, Brexit, and Shinzo Abe, while repetition works through Back to the Future, Star Wars, Kurt Andersen, Mark Harris, The Sopranos, The Wire, Fukuyama, Fight Club, September 11, and the Great Recession.

Part 2 asks why decadence might last. Douthat does not claim that comfort makes people happy; he claims that screens, pornography, video games, marijuana, opioids, antidepressants, surveillance, drones, and online performance can keep discontent from becoming revolutionary. Ted Bundy, James Dobson, Andrea Dworkin, Pornhub, Grand Theft Auto, Japanese grass-eating boys, Dreamland, soma, Freddie deBoer, Eric Schmidt, NSA data, and the "pink police state" all help him describe an order that manages danger by moving it into virtual, pharmaceutical, monitored, or hidden forms.

The same middle section also deflates external threats. Houellebecq's Submission, Marine Le Pen, Mohammed Ben-Abbes, Cavafy's Waiting for the Barbarians, Islamism, Putin, illiberal democracy, Viktor Orban, China, climate change, Africa, and Michael Lind all appear in a chapter that asks whether any rival is vigorous enough to inherit the world. Douthat's answer is mostly no: Islamic civilization is convulsed, Russia lacks a serious civilizational alternative, China may converge toward its own stagnation, and climate change may deepen global inequality without necessarily overthrowing the wealthy metropole.

Part 3 then imagines endings: catastrophe, renaissance, and providence. Catastrophe includes Apocalypto, Aztecs, Incas, microbes, plagues, Black Death, nuclear accidents, debt crisis, Chinese slowdown, Xinjiang, Hong Kong, another 1929, climate warming, David Wallace-Wells, Bill McKibben, Africa's demographic expansion, and Eurafrica. Renaissance includes Trump in Poland, Cardinal Robert Sarah in the Vendee, Aime Cesaire, Leopold Senghor, negritude, Black Panther, Wakanda, energy, robotics, CRISPR, Mars, Chinese eugenics, neo-paganism, Islam, African Christianity, Chinese Christianity, and a restored alliance between science and religious imagination. Providence closes the book by returning to the heavens through Oumuamua, Avi Loeb, UFO videos, Robin Hanson's Great Filter, Pascal, Chesterton's Rome, C. S. Lewis, Madeleine L'Engle, divine intervention, and the need for either better rockets, renewed faith, or both.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Introduction. The Closing of the Frontier

Summary: Douthat opens with Apollo in July 1969, Norman Mailer's account of the sixties, Sputnik in 1957, Challenger in 1986, Walter McDougall's space-age predictions, 2001: A Space Odyssey, Star Trek, Alien, The X-Files, Apollo 13, Gravity, The Martian, Ad Astra, Frederick Jackson Turner's frontier thesis, the Ming dynasty's retreat from sea voyages, Freeman Dyson, Joseph Conrad, Jacques Barzun, Donald Trump, W. H. Auden, and G. K. Chesterton. He uses the closing of the physical frontier and the loss of confident space-age expectation to define decadence as stagnation, institutional decay, repetition, and exhaustion inside a wealthy society that still mistakes digital speed for deep change. Source anchors: Apollo; Challenger; Walter McDougall; Frederick Jackson Turner; Jacques Barzun; Donald Trump; W. H. Auden.

Analysis: Apollo, Challenger, Walter McDougall, Frederick Jackson Turner, Jacques Barzun, Donald Trump, and W. H. Auden give Douthat the book's scale and mood. Apollo and Challenger mark the rise and fading of space-age confidence, while Frederick Jackson Turner lets him treat frontier expansion as a deep source of modern purpose. Jacques Barzun supplies the technical meaning of decadence, Donald Trump becomes a symptom rather than a solution, and W. H. Auden gives the image of an empire lasting without creativity, warmth, or hope.

Chapter 1. Stagnation

Summary: The first horseman begins in Silicon Valley's aura of unreality, with a tech executive in a San Francisco venture-capital office asking whether outsiders think the Internet economy is real. Douthat then uses Billy McFarland and Fyre Festival, Elizabeth Holmes and Theranos, Uber's $11 billion in revenue and $5 billion quarterly loss, WeWork, median American family income above $60,000 in 2017, workforce participation, prime-age male nonwork, John F. Kennedy, Scott Alexander's canceled singularity, Robert Gordon's indoor-plumbing thought experiment, David Graeber on missing space stations, Mark Steyn's 1890-to-1950 comparison, Peter Thiel's flying cars line, antibiotics, AI winter, and Jonathan Huebner's innovation ratio. Source anchors: Fyre Festival; Theranos; Uber; $60,000; Robert Gordon; Peter Thiel; AI winter.

Analysis: Fyre Festival, Theranos, Uber, $60,000, Robert Gordon, Peter Thiel, and AI winter show why twenty-first-century capitalism can look busy while underdelivering. Fyre Festival and Theranos expose fantasy and fraud, while Uber supplies a real service through subsidized losses and future promises. Robert Gordon, Peter Thiel, and AI winter sharpen the distinction within Technological Change: digital convenience diffuses quickly, but water, transport, medicine, energy, and space lack the complementary infrastructure and broad productivity gains that made earlier inventions civilization-restructuring.

Chapter 2. Sterility

Summary: Douthat reads Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale and P. D. James's Children of Men as paired fertility dystopias: Gilead controls women because children are scarce, while James imagines global male sterility after 1995 and a senescent England of suicide, immigrants, cats in prams, cults, Omegas, and police rule. He then turns to actual below-replacement fertility: 2.1 births as replacement, 1.6 in the European Union, 1.41 in Japan, 1.25 in South Korea, 0.82 in Singapore, 1.87 and then 1.7 in the United States, Israel's 3.1, baby boom exceptionalism, Mormon and Orthodox Jewish fertility, Finland's baby box, Japan's sexlessness, and professed desires for marriage and children. Source anchors: The Handmaid’s Tale; Children of Men; 2.1 births; Singapore 0.82; Israel 3.1; baby boom; Finland’s baby box.

Analysis: The Handmaid’s Tale, Children of Men, 2.1 births, Singapore 0.82, Israel 3.1, baby boom, and Finland’s baby box let Douthat treat low fertility as cultural, economic, sexual, and religious at once. The Handmaid’s Tale and Children of Men dramatize the political fear attached to childlessness, while 2.1 births and Singapore 0.82 establish the demographic shortfall. Israel 3.1 and baby boom show that rich societies can have exceptions, and Finland’s baby box shows why policy alone cannot explain or reverse the decline.

Chapter 3. Sclerosis

Summary: The political chapter argues that rich societies have functioning institutions that increasingly cannot decide, reform, or adapt. Douthat moves through American constitutional veto points, the twentieth-century administrative state, polarization, Donald Trump, the GOP after Iraq and the financial crisis, Obama's opposition, woke progressivism, the European Union, Brussels, Francis Fukuyama, the euro, German fiscal terms, Greece, Spain, Italy, Ireland, Angela Merkel's refugee decision, Brexit, Viktor Orban, Matteo Salvini, Syriza, Poland, Britain, and Shinzo Abe's Abenomics in Japan. He treats Abe as a limited counterexample: nationalist, experimental, and more effective than many peers, but still unable to restore pre-1990 Japanese growth or fertility. Source anchors: Donald Trump; Iraq War; European Union; euro; Angela Merkel; Brexit; Abenomics.

Analysis: Donald Trump, the Iraq War, European Union, euro, Angela Merkel, Brexit, and Abenomics show sclerosis on both sides of the Atlantic and in Japan. The European Union and euro expose a State Capacity problem: institutions can enforce fiscal constraints yet cannot build shared political authority capable of resolving unequal adjustment costs. Merkel and Brexit produce action without a clean settlement, while Abenomics shows a government executing policy more decisively but still failing to overcome demographic and productivity drag.

Chapter 4. Repetition

Summary: The cultural chapter starts with Back to the Future Day on 10/21/2015 and asks why the distance from 1985 to 2015 feels smaller than the distance from the 1950s to 1985. Douthat uses Marty McFly, Darth Vader from the planet Vulcan, The Force Awakens, J. J. Abrams, Star Trek, Star Wars, presold intellectual property, Kurt Andersen's Vanity Fair essay, Mark Harris on comic-book franchises, Blade Runner 2049, Ready Player One, Joker, The Sopranos, The Wire, Mad Men, Game of Thrones, George R. R. Martin, Lasch's Culture of Narcissism, Ta-Nehisi Coates, Jordan Peterson, campus social justice cycles, Wikipedia, Nicholas Carr's The Shallows, the iPhone, and Francis Fukuyama's end of history. Source anchors: Back to the Future; The Force Awakens; Kurt Andersen; Mark Harris; Game of Thrones; Wikipedia; Francis Fukuyama.

Analysis: Back to the Future, The Force Awakens, Kurt Andersen, Mark Harris, Game of Thrones, Wikipedia, and Francis Fukuyama make repetition visible in mass culture, intellectual life, and ideology. Back to the Future gives Douthat a generational measuring tool, while The Force Awakens and Mark Harris show Hollywood converting inherited properties into the central business model. Game of Thrones illustrates success turning into stalled invention, Wikipedia gives one shared hive source where many reference worlds once existed, and Francis Fukuyama names the deeper feeling that no rival idea has replaced liberal modernity.

Chapter 5. Comfortably Numb

Summary: Douthat begins sustainable decadence with Ted Bundy's 1989 interview with James Dobson and the claim that hard-core pornography caused his murders. He contrasts that Reagan-era fear, shared in different ways by Andrea Dworkin and Catherine MacKinnon, with the Pornhub age, Todd Kendall's research, UCLA sex-criminal surveys, falling reported sexual assault, Grand Theft Auto, first-person shooters, teen fighting falling from 42 percent in 1992 to 22 percent in 2015, teen pregnancy, Snapchat, suicide attempts, gaming men after the Great Recession, Call of Duty, Red Dead Redemption, Japan's grass-eating boys, parasite singles, celibacy syndrome, soma, marijuana, opioids, Andrew Sullivan, Sam Quinones's Dreamland, Huxley's feelies, and the Violent Passion Surrogate. Source anchors: Ted Bundy; James Dobson; Pornhub; Grand Theft Auto; Call of Duty; grass-eating boys; Dreamland.

Analysis: Ted Bundy, James Dobson, Pornhub, Grand Theft Auto, Call of Duty, grass-eating boys, and Dreamland let Douthat claim that vice may stabilize rather than inflame a decadent order. Pornhub and Grand Theft Auto are not defended morally; they are treated as substitutes that can reduce certain real-world risks while increasing loneliness, anxiety, and unreality. Call of Duty and grass-eating boys connect entertainment to male nonwork and sexual withdrawal, while Dreamland shows chemical numbness performing a similar social function among people harmed by stagnation.

Chapter 6. A Kindly Despotism

Summary: This chapter describes a soft system of control that combines online policing, data surveillance, hidden violence, and managed disorder without becoming a classic Orwellian police state. Douthat uses the pink police state, Freddie deBoer's world of snitches, James Poulos, Alexa, Slack, smartphones, social media mobs, Google residue, Eric Schmidt's claim that people with secrets should not be doing those things, NSA access to major Internet companies, the Nothing to Hide Twitter account, YouTube shame, mass incarceration, abortion, Depo-Provera, pharmaceutical abortion, drones, special forces, Samuel Moyn on humane war, ISIS recruitment, Alex Jones, Trump, shadow banning, deplatforming, and the Fake News Complex. Source anchors: pink police state; Freddie deBoer; Eric Schmidt; NSA; Nothing to Hide; Depo-Provera; Samuel Moyn.

Analysis: Pink police state, Freddie deBoer, Eric Schmidt, NSA, Nothing to Hide, Depo-Provera, and Samuel Moyn define Douthat's soft despotism. Social-media mobs, Google residue, deplatforming, and shadow banning create Platform Governance because private information layers set visibility, memory, and participation rules while users perform much of the enforcement horizontally. Eric Schmidt, NSA, and Nothing to Hide show why vertical surveillance enters as convenience or safety, making control less visible and therefore easier for a comfortable society to tolerate.

Chapter 7. Waiting for the Barbarians

Summary: Douthat asks whether any rival can end Western decadence from outside, beginning with Michel Houellebecq's Submission, Marine Le Pen, Mohammed Ben-Abbes, Charlie Hebdo, Joris-Karl Huysmans, polygamy, C. P. Cavafy's Waiting for the Barbarians, Islamism, Osama bin Laden, Sayyid Qutb, Wahhabis, Iran, ISIS, Saudi Arabia, European Muslim fertility, Vladimir Putin, Ukraine, Recep Tayyip Erdogan, Viktor Orban, Law and Justice in Poland, the Institutional Revolutionary Party in Mexico, China, Confucian bureaucracy, African elites, climate change, and Michael Lind's managerial minority. He argues that Islam, Russia, illiberal democracy, China, and climate pressure may trouble the West without yet supplying a fully persuasive successor. Source anchors: Submission; Mohammed Ben-Abbes; Cavafy; Osama bin Laden; Putin; illiberal democracy; China.

Analysis: Submission, Mohammed Ben-Abbes, Cavafy, Osama bin Laden, Putin, illiberal democracy, and China organize the chapter's refusal of easy apocalypse. Mohammed Ben-Abbes is powerful in fiction because he seems to offer movement, but Osama bin Laden and contemporary Islamism do not generate a broad elite conversion or coherent world order. Putin and illiberal democracy look more like degraded versions of existing liberal forms than a replacement, while China is the strongest possible rival but may inherit aging, debt, surveillance, and stagnation of its own.

Chapter 8. Giving Decadence Its Due

Summary: Douthat pauses to acknowledge that rich-world decadence has real goods: peace, safety, longevity, comfort, lower crime, ordinary pleasures, and space for private virtue. He warns against antidecadent fantasies by naming Verdun, Passchendaele, the Fuhrer, the thousand-year Reich, post-9/11 hysteria, Hilaire Belloc's nurse couplet, Adam Smith on ruin in a nation, Easter Island, Dr. Seuss's Lorax, the atomic age in 1962, climate dynamism in the developing world, Steven Pinker, Zoloft, Barack Obama's managerial temperament, Don't do stupid shit, Michael Lind, Dietrich Vollrath, Evelyn Waugh's Sword of Honour, Guy Crouchback, Brave New World's Mustapha Mond, Fight Club, and Tyler Durden. Source anchors: Verdun; Hilaire Belloc; Adam Smith; Barack Obama; Don’t do stupid shit; Guy Crouchback; Mustapha Mond.

Analysis: Verdun, Hilaire Belloc, Adam Smith, Barack Obama, Don’t do stupid shit, Guy Crouchback, and Mustapha Mond let Douthat discipline his own critique. Verdun and Guy Crouchback warn that a hunger for meaning can turn into war fever, while Hilaire Belloc and Adam Smith justify a provisional preference for safety over romantic rupture. Barack Obama and Don’t do stupid shit name managerial decadence at its most defensible, but Mustapha Mond shows why stewardship of comfort can become a smooth apology for a posthuman social order.

Chapter 9. Catastrophe

Summary: The catastrophe chapter opens with Mel Gibson's Apocalypto, Jaguar Paw, Will Durant's line about civilizations destroyed from within, Hernan Cortes, Francisco Pizarro, Montezuma, the Great Inca, microbes after 1492, Roman plagues, the Black Death, Y2K, nuclear warning systems, Captain Trips, Skynet, the meteor, Greece, quantitative easing, debt-to-GDP above 100 percent, Brad DeLong's Medici Bank analogy, Chinese slowdown, Xinjiang, Hong Kong, Silicon Valley capital, Uber, another 1929, Grexit, Italexit, David Wallace-Wells's Uninhabitable Earth, Bill McKibben's shrinking planet, Syrian refugees, Africa's population projection of 4.5 billion by 2100, and Eurafrica. Source anchors: Apocalypto; 1492; Black Death; debt-to-GDP; Xinjiang; Uninhabitable Earth; 4.5 billion.

Analysis: Apocalypto, 1492, Black Death, debt-to-GDP, Xinjiang, Uninhabitable Earth, and 4.5 billion give Douthat several ways decadence could end without a heroic renaissance. 1492 and Black Death show random or external shocks overwhelming societies regardless of internal moral condition. debt-to-GDP and Xinjiang make a financial or Chinese crisis plausible inside existing trends, while Uninhabitable Earth and 4.5 billion connect climate stress and African demography to migration pressures that Europe may be too rigid to absorb.

Chapter 10. Renaissance

Summary: Douthat imagines renewal through politics, migration, technology, religion, and combinations of all four. He begins with Donald Trump's 2017 Poland speech, then contrasts it with Cardinal Robert Sarah in the Vendee, African Christianity, Emmanuel Macron, Aime Cesaire, Leopold Senghor, Gary Wilder's Freedom Time, negritude, Gerard Manley Hopkins, T. S. Eliot, W. B. Yeats, Chinua Achebe, V. S. Naipaul, Black Panther, Wakanda, Kevin Kelly's 2114 prompt, Mars, artificial wombs, CRISPR, Chinese genetic engineering, Khan Noonien Singh, neo-paganism, Spinoza, Nietzsche, Emerson, Whitman, Oprah, Deepak Chopra, Burning Man, Islam, African and Chinese Christianity, Butlerian Jihad, Jack Parsons, Aleister Crowley, L. Ron Hubbard, Apollo 8, Bill Anders, and Kendrick Oliver. Source anchors: Robert Sarah; Vendee; Aime Cesaire; negritude; Wakanda; CRISPR; Apollo 8.

Analysis: Robert Sarah, Vendee, Aime Cesaire, negritude, Wakanda, CRISPR, and Apollo 8 show why Douthat treats renaissance as plural rather than single-cause. Robert Sarah and Vendee imagine a Eurafrican Christianity that renews Europe from outside its tired categories, while Aime Cesaire and negritude imagine a political-cultural synthesis between former colony and former metropole. Wakanda and CRISPR point toward technological or Afro-futurist renewal, and Apollo 8 shows the older alchemy between scientific daring and religious meaning that Douthat thinks would mark a real escape from decadence.

Chapter 11. Providence

Summary: The conclusion returns to the heavens through Oumuamua in 2017, Avi Loeb at Harvard, possible alien spacecraft, Pentagon UFO videos, the Great Filter proposed by Robin Hanson, George Mason University, light speed, Mars, terraforming, asteroid strikes, nuclear-launch accidents, Blaise Pascal's terror before infinite spaces, post-Apollo pessimism, Elon Musk, Genesis's command to fill the earth and subdue it, UFO providentialism after the atomic bomb, G. K. Chesterton's Everlasting Man, Rome, Jesus Christ, C. S. Lewis's space trilogy, Madeleine L'Engle's A Wrinkle in Time, spiritual quarantine, God, stars, and warp drive. Douthat ends by saying decadence may require either a new way to the stars or an intervention from beyond the current material horizon. Source anchors: Oumuamua; Avi Loeb; Great Filter; Pascal; Elon Musk; Chesterton; warp drive.

Analysis: Oumuamua, Avi Loeb, Great Filter, Pascal, Elon Musk, Chesterton, and warp drive return the book from policy and culture to metaphysical scale. Great Filter and Pascal make earthbound civilization itself a possible source of despair, while Elon Musk and warp drive name the material hope that renewed spacefaring might reopen the frontier. Chesterton gives Douthat the Christian counterpoint: Rome's decadence looked like man reaching his limit, and such a limit may be precisely where providence, revelation, or judgment enters the story.
