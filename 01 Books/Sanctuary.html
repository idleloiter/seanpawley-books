Sanctuary

Buy on Amazon — Sanctuary

Detailed overview

William Faulkner’s Sanctuary is built as a collision between Horace Benbow’s genteel legal conscience, Temple Drake’s college-world recklessness and terror, Popeye’s mechanical violence, and Jefferson’s appetite for public punishment. The novel begins not in court or in a drawing room but at a spring near the Old Frenchman place, where Popeye silently watches Horace drink, interrogates him about a book, and escorts him to Lee Goodwin’s ruined bootlegging house. That first encounter fixes the book’s method: social order, law, education, and family respectability are present, but they are useless when crossed by Popeye’s cigarette, pistol, Packard, and dead-eyed stillness.

The Old Frenchman place concentrates the novel’s main social worlds before they scatter. Ruby Lamar cooks for Lee Goodwin, protects a sick baby in a box behind the stove, and understands the danger in the house better than Temple, Gowan Stevens, Horace, or Tommy. Gowan’s Virginia manners collapse into drunken cowardice after a night of town boys, rotgut, and missed trains; Temple’s Jackson judge father and school probation cannot protect her once Gowan drives her to Goodwin’s for whiskey. Tommy, the barefoot “feeb” whom others mock, becomes the only man who tries to guard Temple in the barn, and Popeye kills him for it.

Faulkner refuses to make the crime a simple detective puzzle. Temple is assaulted and taken to Memphis, but the official case turns on Tommy’s murder and Lee Goodwin’s false prosecution. Horace knows Popeye is the real menace, Ruby knows the night’s breathing and movements, Goodwin fears Popeye’s reach from Memphis, and Temple becomes both necessary witness and unreliable survivor. Jefferson’s jail, courthouse, hotel, Baptist talk, and gossip about Ruby’s marital status convert violence into a spectacle where the community can condemn Goodwin while ignoring Gowan, Popeye, Clarence Snopes, and its own traffic in liquor and women.

The Memphis chapters place Temple inside Miss Reba’s brothel, where Minnie, Doctor Quinn, the dogs Reba and Mr Binford, Red, and Popeye’s paid routines expose another economy of exploitation. Miss Reba’s house is not genteel, but it often sees more clearly than Jefferson: Reba understands that Popeye’s relation to Temple is “funny,” recognizes Horace’s sincerity, and eventually lets him question Temple. Yet Memphis also gives Clarence Snopes a market for information, lets Popeye use Red as a sexual proxy, and turns Temple’s trauma into clothes, diamonds, gin, and stories told with unstable bravado.

Horace’s legal mission decays as he moves between Ruby, Narcissa, Miss Jenny, Miss Reba, Temple, Clarence Snopes, and Eustace Graham. He wants Goodwin acquitted because the facts demand it, but he also drags into the case his failed marriage to Belle, his fixation on Little Belle, his disgust with shrimp, his inherited Jefferson house, and his sister Narcissa’s obsession with social cleanliness. The trial’s failure is therefore not just a miscarriage of evidence; it shows how Horace’s moral imagination cannot survive contact with Temple’s testimony, the corn-cob exhibit, Eustace Graham’s political ambition, and a jury that needs eight minutes.

The closing movement splits punishment from guilt. Goodwin is convicted and burned by a Jefferson mob for a murder he did not commit, while Popeye is arrested elsewhere for a different killing he also did not commit and allows himself to be hanged without using the alibi that would save him. The final image of Temple and Judge Drake in the Luxembourg Gardens does not restore innocence; it relocates Temple into European decorum, damp music, and a compact mirror after the novel has shown how little fatherhood, law, money, schooling, or travel can undo what happened at the Old Frenchman place and in Miss Reba’s Memphis rooms.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Illicit Markets: Bootlegging, Memphis prostitution, Clarence Snopes's information trade, bribable movement, and Popeye's networks run faster than the formal legal case.
  • Informal Institutions: Gossip, sexual reputation, family respectability, hotel exclusion, racialized service, and courtroom spectatorship decide what the law can hear.
  • Coercive Labor: Ruby's work, Temple's confinement, Miss Reba's house, Popeye's proxy arrangements, and Goodwin's jail vulnerability show bodies controlled through dependence and threat.
  • Language and Ideology: Terms such as sanctuary, good family, Mrs Goodwin, trial testimony, and public punishment expose how social words hide coercion or redirect blame.
  • Moral Economy: Horace, Ruby, Tommy, Miss Reba, and Gilligan-like witnesses test what care is owed when respectable institutions refuse inconvenient people.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: Judge Drake, the courthouse, Temple's family display, and the mob reveal legitimacy decaying into spectacle when procedure ratifies falsehood.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter 1

Summary: Popeye watches Horace Benbow drink from the spring, questions the book in Horace’s coat pocket, and holds him for two hours among cane, brier, cypress, gum, tire tracks, a felled tree, and the repeated bird call. Horace identifies himself as a Kinston lawyer going to Jefferson, but Popeye’s black suit, straw hat, cigarettes, dollar watch, and pistol-like pocket keep the encounter under silent coercion. The owl on the road terrifies Popeye, and Horace recognizes the Carolina wren before they reach the Old Frenchman place. Ruby Lamar is cooking meat in the kitchen, a child is hidden in a box behind the stove, Tommy retrieves a jug from under the floor, and Popeye taunts Ruby about Manuel Street and Lee Goodwin’s shoes. Source anchors: Popeye; Horace Benbow; spring; Carolina wren; Old Frenchman place; Ruby Lamar; box behind the stove; Tommy.

Analysis: Popeye, Horace Benbow, and the spring form the novel’s first test of language against force: Horace can name birds, books, Jefferson, and Kinston, but Popeye’s watch, cigarette, and pocket make naming useless. The Carolina wren and owl show that Popeye’s power is mixed with animal panic rather than courage. Ruby Lamar, Tommy, and the box behind the stove widen the first chapter from a roadside threat into a household where dependence, liquor, sex work, and infant care already coexist before Temple ever appears.

Chapter 2

Summary: Horace eats at Goodwin’s rough plank table with Popeye, Tommy, Goodwin, Ruby, and the blind old man whom Goodwin leads in and out like a dependent relic. Ruby serves food, listens from inside the door, and judges Horace’s drunken monologue about Belle, Little Belle, the grape arbor, mirrors, shrimp, a train ride, and his need for a hill. Horace touches Ruby’s face, explains that he lacks courage, and follows her to the kitchen where she shows him the child in the box to keep rats away. Goodwin sends Horace off with Tommy toward Popeye’s truck, and Tommy explains Popeye’s fear, the dead dog, the felled tree, Lee’s whiskey trade, and the money in quick runs to Memphis. Source anchors: plank table; blind old man; Little Belle; shrimp; orange stick; Popeye's truck; dead dog; Memphis.

Analysis: The plank table and blind old man put Horace inside a household he wants to interpret but cannot enter cleanly. Little Belle and shrimp expose why Horace is vulnerable to stories about endangered young women: his own domestic revulsion has already displaced him from Kinston. Ruby’s request for an orange stick and Tommy’s account of Popeye’s truck turn Horace’s “harmless” curiosity into obligation; by the time Memphis is named, the bootlegging network has already linked the ruined house to the city.

Chapter 3

Summary: Horace reaches Narcissa’s country house four miles from Jefferson and resumes the family comedy with Miss Jenny, Narcissa, young Benbow Sartoris, and Gowan Stevens. Miss Jenny needles Horace about Belle and about his tendency to run from marriage, while Narcissa asks after Belle and Little Belle with guarded disapproval. Gowan appears as the Virginia-trained suitor in flannels and a blue coat, boasts lightly, spars with Horace about Oxford and Harvard, and sends roses for supper. After supper, Miss Jenny identifies Gowan as the same young man courting Narcissa, but he leaves for Oxford and a university dance with a red-headed girl. Source anchors: Narcissa; Miss Jenny; Gowan Stevens; Benbow Sartoris; Belle; Little Belle; Virginia; Oxford.

Analysis: Narcissa, Miss Jenny, and Gowan Stevens give Jefferson’s respectable face before the novel reveals what that respectability conceals. Gowan Stevens is introduced through Virginia polish and Oxford social mobility, which makes his later drunken abandonment of Temple more damning than if he were already marked as criminal. Belle and Little Belle remain active pressures on Horace; his family scenes are not an escape from the Old Frenchman place but the source of the anxieties that make him respond to Temple and Ruby as he does.

Chapter 4

Summary: Temple Drake is shown at the University, slipping from the Coop, riding with town boys, attending Letter Club dances, and leaving with Gowan Stevens while resentful local boys watch. Doc, another town boy, spreads broken glass on the road and later rides with Gowan, who asks for liquor before the Starkville baseball trip. At the Shack near the depot, Gowan drinks from a fruit jar, makes whiskey sours with sugar, water, lemon, and Coca-Cola, boasts that Virginia taught him to drink like a gentleman, and passes out near the station. He misses Temple’s special train at Taylor by five minutes, drinks again from the jar, meets her drunk and disheveled, drives toward Starkville, detours to Goodwin’s for whiskey, and crashes into the felled tree where Popeye and Tommy appear. Source anchors: Temple Drake; Coop; Doc; Shack; fruit jar; Taylor; Starkville; felled tree.

Analysis: Temple Drake’s campus life at the Coop and Gowan’s performance at the Shack turn “good family” behavior into the route to the Old Frenchman place. Doc and the broken glass show how resentment around class, cars, and women is already violent before Popeye enters the scene. Taylor, Starkville, and the felled tree convert Gowan’s boast about gentlemanly drinking into concrete failure: he does not protect Temple, he delivers her to Popeye and Tommy.

Chapter 5

Summary: Tommy leads Temple and Gowan from the wrecked car through the sand to the Old Frenchman place, while Temple struggles in high heels and worries about the gaunt house, cedar grove, apple orchard, abandoned outbuildings, and absence of any planted field. She enters the hall, finds the back porch, sees Pap’s blind yellow eyes, notices patched undergarments on the line, and panics when Popeye appears near the kitchen. Gowan wants Lee Goodwin to get a car, but Temple asks Tommy his name, asks after Mrs Goodwin, and repeatedly says she does not want to go inside. The chapter ends with Temple in the kitchen facing Ruby, who is smoking at the table. Source anchors: Tommy; high heels; cedar grove; Pap; patched undergarment; Popeye; Mrs Goodwin; kitchen.

Analysis: Tommy, high heels, and the cedar grove measure Temple’s physical displacement from the University into terrain she cannot navigate. Pap and the patched undergarment show her that the house contains poverty, age, sexuality, and domestic work in forms her judge’s-daughter vocabulary cannot manage. Popeye’s appearance and Ruby’s kitchen place Temple between predatory power and practical warning; the chapter makes the kitchen the only possible refuge, but not a secure one.

Chapter 6

Summary: Popeye orders Tommy away and scrapes mud from his shoes with a platinum penknife while Gowan continues drinking in the barn with Tommy. Tommy tells Gowan about Pap, the previous talkative stranger, and Popeye’s habit of calling him Jack, and then Popeye summons them back from below. Temple reports Ruby’s warning that she should leave before dark, mentions the baby in the box, asks Gowan to request Popeye’s Packard, and then directly asks Popeye to drive them to town. Popeye refuses, insults her, Gowan blusters and then shoves Temple into the house, and Temple runs between hall, kitchen, shotgun, Pap, and the baby box until Goodwin lifts her by the neck and asks what she is doing in his house. Source anchors: platinum penknife; Gowan; Pap; Packard; shotgun; baby box; Goodwin; Jack.

Analysis: The platinum penknife and Packard define Popeye’s cold possession of objects, while Gowan’s drunkenness strips him of any protective role. Temple’s movement from shotgun to baby box to Goodwin shows her desperate search for a symbol that might save her: weapon, child, law, husband, father. Goodwin’s entrance is ambiguous because he can restrain Van and shelter Ruby, but he also treats Temple as an intruder in his house rather than as someone whom Gowan and Popeye have trapped.

Chapter 7

Summary: Temple talks with Ruby in the kitchen while Gowan drinks again and men’s voices come from the hall. She invokes Judge Drake of Jackson, four brothers, Hubert, the governor, probation at school, town boys with cars, Starkville, Taylor, Dumfries, and Gowan getting drunk three times in one day. Ruby answers with her own history: Frank, her father’s shotgun, Lee Goodwin’s Philippine killing, Leavenworth, medals, prison visits, night-shift work, jazzing to pay a lawyer, and the price of loving a “real man.” Temple holds Ruby’s baby, offers a fur coat, and begs Ruby to get a car, while Ruby calls her a “gutless fool” but finally says she will try to help if Temple leaves forever. Source anchors: Judge Drake; Hubert; probation; Dumfries; Leavenworth; Frank; fur coat; real man.

Analysis: Judge Drake, Hubert, and probation are Temple’s failing credentials; she repeats them because she believes family and school discipline should still count. Ruby’s Leavenworth, Frank, and “real man” history cut through that belief by showing a world where men are measured by violence, prison, work, and sexual possession rather than by college or judgeship. The fur coat offer proves how badly Temple misreads Ruby, but Ruby’s decision to help links contempt to care in a way no respectable Jefferson woman will later manage, making Ruby's kitchen a harsh case of Moral Economy outside respectability.

Chapter 8

Summary: Temple enters the dining room, tries to move toward Tommy, and is intercepted by Van, who pulls her toward his lap until Goodwin orders him to let her go. Tommy brings her food, worries that Van and the other men are pestering her, and later watches her through the window of the room where she wedges a chair against the door. Temple arranges her coat, raincoat, dress, slippers, hat, compact, watch, and canteen around the bed as if objects could secure her, while Gowan is beaten and carried into the room. Van rips at the raincoat, Goodwin fights him, Popeye approaches Temple, Goodwin warns him not to touch her, and Ruby accuses Goodwin of planning to finish what Van began. Source anchors: Van; Tommy; raincoat; canteen; chair against the door; Gowan; Goodwin; Popeye.

Analysis: Van and Gowan turn male rivalry into immediate danger for Temple, while Tommy’s watching is both protective and helpless. The raincoat, canteen, and chair against the door show Temple trying to build a private barrier out of random objects in a lockless room. Goodwin’s confrontation with Popeye matters because it briefly proves he can oppose the house’s violence, yet Ruby’s accusation shows that his authority is still entangled with the same possession that endangers Temple.

Chapter 9

Summary: In the dark room Ruby stands inside the lockless door while Gowan snores and Goodwin returns only to remove Van’s raincoat from Temple. Ruby smells Popeye’s brilliantine before seeing him, senses Tommy creeping behind him, and listens to faint sounds from the shuck mattress while Popeye stands over the bed. After Popeye leaves, Ruby tries to quiet Temple, leads her from the room, and takes her toward the barn crib. Temple struggles with Ruby as if any touch were another attack, while the men continue moving around the house and porch. Source anchors: lockless door; Gowan snoring; raincoat; brilliantine; Tommy's eyes; shuck mattress; Ruby; barn crib.

Analysis: The lockless door, Gowan snoring, and raincoat make plain how little separates Temple from the men around her. Ruby’s recognition of brilliantine and Tommy’s eyes gives the chapter its sensory precision: she knows the house by breath, smell, and darkness because survival requires it. The barn crib is not sanctuary in any holy sense, but Ruby’s best available tactic for moving Temple away from Popeye after the shuck mattress scene.

Chapter 10

Summary: Temple wakes in the barn crib among cottonseed hulls, corn-cobs, chaff, and traces of rats, believing the door locked and then recoiling when Pap shuffles past with his stick. She thinks of Sunday church bells, dormitory girls, and clean spring clothes while walking in stockings back to the house, where she finds the cold stove, dirty skillet, empty room, one slipper in the fireplace, the U.S. canteen, dress, hat, and compact. Ruby gives her coffee, bread, and pages torn from a mail-order catalogue for the barn privy, and Temple runs through the barn, down among jimson weeds and sand, where she sees a squatting man watching her. She flees back to Ruby, burns her hand on the cold stove in panic, breaks free, climbs into the barn loft, falls through a trap into a crib, and faces a rat at close range. Source anchors: cottonseed hulls; Pap; Sunday church bells; U.S. canteen; mail-order catalogue; jimson weeds; squatting man; rat.

Analysis: Cottonseed hulls, Pap, and the rat make Temple’s fear bodily rather than rhetorical: every surface threatens contact. Sunday church bells and the U.S. canteen show her mind importing normal order into a place where it has no force. The mail-order catalogue and jimson weeds turn ordinary rural materials into instruments of humiliation and panic, and the squatting man prepares the barn for Popeye’s return by making Temple feel watched even before she knows by whom.

Chapter 11

Summary: Temple remains trapped in the crib after falling through the trap, hammering at the planks while the rat squeaks and moves in the cottonseed hulls. The chapter concentrates on the enclosed space: loose hulls, gnawed corn-cobs, the crossbeam, the door, the trap above her, and the sense that the barn has become a sealed container. Temple’s hands scrape at the wood, her body recoils from the rat, and the ordinary objects of storage become part of her terror. The scene extends the previous chapter’s flight by denying her any exit except through the same men who control the barn. Source anchors: crib; trap; cottonseed hulls; corn-cobs; crossbeam; rat; planks; barn.

Analysis: The crib, trap, and planks reduce Temple’s world to architecture designed for animals and crops, not rescue. Cottonseed hulls, corn-cobs, and the rat matter because they replace the social labels Temple keeps invoking with immediate matter pressing against her body. The barn’s sealed space also gives Popeye the physical arrangement he needs; the chapter’s confinement is the mechanism that turns flight into capture.

Chapter 12

Summary: Ruby confronts Goodwin in the kitchen after realizing Temple is gone, and Goodwin, drunk and white-nostriled, drags her aside, slaps her repeatedly, and leaves with the child briefly set on the table. Ruby takes the baby, puts on a sunbonnet and old fur-trimmed coat, walks past Tommy and Pap to the spring, and sits there until Popeye appears, complaining about the house, Horace, Tommy, and the drinking. Popeye says he is clearing out, then circles back through the orchard where Goodwin watches the barn, crosses behind the cedars, enters the barn from the rear, climbs through a hay rack into the loft, and descends toward the crib. Source anchors: white nostrils; butcher knife; sunbonnet; spring; clearing out; orchard; hay rack; loft.

Analysis: Goodwin’s white nostrils and the butcher knife show that Ruby is endangered by the man she has sacrificed for as well as by Popeye. The spring returns the novel to the place where Horace first met Popeye, but now Ruby and the baby occupy it, making the opening threat part of a repeated circuit. Popeye’s claim that he is clearing out, followed by the orchard, hay rack, and loft route, exposes his evasive method: he announces withdrawal while moving toward the crime.

Chapter 13

Summary: Temple finally opens the crib door and finds Tommy in the barn hallway, then retreats when she sees Goodwin near the house. Tommy repeats that Lee says it will not hurt her and fastens the door from outside, promising to keep everyone away. Popeye descends from the loft into the crib, orders Tommy to open the door, pushes Tommy’s face back, tells him to watch Goodwin, and shoots him with a sound Temple hears as small as a match strike. Popeye turns toward Temple with the pistol, and her mind fractures into a scream addressed to Pap’s blind yellow eyes, insisting that something is happening to her. Source anchors: Tommy; Lee says; crib door; loft; pistol; match strike; Pap's eyes; something is happening.

Analysis: Tommy’s “Lee says” and his promise at the crib door show his moral confusion: he wants to protect Temple but still speaks in Goodwin’s terms. The loft and pistol let Popeye bypass Tommy’s guard, while the match strike sound turns murder into something almost administratively small. Pap’s eyes and Temple’s cry that something is happening are crucial because no witness who could legally save her is available; sight, speech, and protection all fail at once, leaving Coercive Labor and sexual coercion hidden inside barn architecture and household dependence.

Chapter 14

Summary: Ruby returns from the spring because she forgot the baby’s bottle and sees Popeye’s car pass with Temple in it, Temple’s face blank beneath her hat and giving no sign of recognition. Ruby finds Goodwin shaving and preparing to walk to Tull’s to telephone the sheriff, but she insists on going herself. At Tull’s house, with the family eating Sunday dinner around the telephone, she reports a dead man at the Old Frenchman place and identifies herself as Mrs Goodwin. The chapter moves the crime from barn secrecy into official procedure through Ruby’s public call. Source anchors: baby bottle; Popeye's car; Temple's face; Goodwin shaving; Tull's; telephone; sheriff; Mrs Goodwin.

Analysis: The baby bottle and Temple’s face set Ruby’s private maternal errand against the public fact of abduction. Goodwin shaving before the sheriff call suggests a man preparing an appearance, while Tull’s telephone turns rural neighborliness into the route by which law enters the case. Ruby calling herself Mrs Goodwin matters because that identity will later be attacked; she uses it to report murder even though Jefferson will use it to discredit her.

Chapter 15

Summary: Horace settles at Narcissa’s house after Belle wires that she has returned to Kentucky with Little Belle, and he tells Miss Jenny and Narcissa about Goodwin’s place, Popeye, Ruby, Tommy, the blind old man, the baby, and the orange stick. He then opens the old Benbow house in Jefferson, scrubs floors, buys bedding and tinned food, refuses Narcissa’s bedclothes, and walks through the Saturday square among wagons, cars, courthouse crowds, radios, phonographs, ballads, fruit hulls, and mail-order finery. On Monday, townspeople gather at the undertaker’s parlor to look at Tommy’s body, barefoot in overalls, with dried blood and powder burns, while the coroner tries to learn his last name. No one knows it, though many have known him for years. Source anchors: Belle; Kentucky; orange stick; Benbow house; Saturday square; undertaker's parlor; Tommy's body; last name.

Analysis: Belle and Kentucky free Horace physically but not morally, because the orange stick and Ruby’s child have already entered his conscience. The Benbow house and Saturday square place him back inside Jefferson’s public gaze, where private acts become communal material. Tommy’s body and missing last name expose the town’s Legibility hierarchy: Tommy was visible enough to mock and view at the undertaker’s parlor, but not enough to be known as a full person.

Chapter 16

Summary: Goodwin is jailed beside a Black murderer who sings at the barred window while people gather outside to listen. Horace visits Goodwin, who refuses to name Popeye, insists the state must prove its case, and worries more about Ruby and the child than about legal strategy. Horace takes Ruby and the baby to his Jefferson house, provoking Narcissa’s fury and Miss Jenny’s warnings about collusion, then moves Ruby to the hotel after she recognizes the social danger of staying in his family house. The chapter ends with Horace’s memories of the child in the box, Ruby’s orange-stick request, Isom driving them, and Ruby admitting she knows how his kinwomen will judge her. Source anchors: barred window; Black murderer; Goodwin; Ruby; Jefferson house; Narcissa; Miss Jenny; Isom.

Analysis: The barred window and Black murderer create a grim echo of Goodwin’s situation before the trial even begins. Goodwin’s silence about Popeye is not ignorance; it is fear of retaliation from a man who can reach him beyond the law. Ruby, Narcissa, Miss Jenny, and Isom show Horace trying to enact charity through family property, only to discover that Jefferson’s propriety can expel Ruby as efficiently as any court order.

Chapter 17

Summary: Horace explains Goodwin’s case to Miss Jenny while the jail’s heaven tree drops its last blooms and the Black murderer sings about his coming execution. Goodwin refuses bond, says the sheriff has destroyed his still, and gives Ruby money buried in a can in the barn. Narcissa enters as Miss Jenny jokes about Gowan Stevens jilting her, and Horace reads Gowan’s letter saying he has gone through an experience he cannot face and will not see Narcissa again. Horace realizes Gowan abandoned Temple, but Goodwin still refuses to expose Popeye because he fears being shot from hotel windows or on the way back from court. Source anchors: heaven tree; buried money; Gowan's letter; Narcissa; Popeye's presence; hotel windows; sheriff; June term.

Analysis: The heaven tree and the singing prisoner make punishment part of Jefferson’s daily weather, not an abstract legal endpoint. Buried money and Gowan’s letter connect two male failures: Goodwin’s criminal savings support Ruby, while Gowan’s polished note hides cowardice behind Virginia manners. Popeye’s presence and hotel windows explain why Goodwin’s silence is practical rather than noble; he knows the law cannot shield him from Memphis violence.

Chapter 18

Summary: Popeye drives Temple away from the Old Frenchman place in the Packard, passing Ruby with the baby at the roadside and Gowan’s wrecked car near the felled tree. Temple bleeds, screams, powders her face in the windshield mirror, eats a sandwich Popeye buys at Dumfries, hides behind a greasy barrel when she imagines a schoolboy has seen her, and is forced back into the car by Popeye’s grip on her neck. They reach Memphis in midafternoon, and Popeye brings her to Miss Reba’s house, where Minnie, the dogs Reba and Mr Binford, gin, beer, Doctor Quinn, washed clothes, blood, and a one-handed flowered clock surround Temple’s first hours. Source anchors: Packard; Ruby with baby; Dumfries; windshield mirror; greasy barrel; Miss Reba; Doctor Quinn; one-handed clock.

Analysis: The Packard, Dumfries, and windshield mirror show Popeye converting abduction into controlled transport, grooming Temple’s appearance while ignoring her injury. Ruby with baby is the last witness from the Old Frenchman place, and Temple’s failure to recognize her marks the split between survival and comprehension. Miss Reba, Doctor Quinn, and the one-handed clock move Temple into a new enclosure where women tend her body, but the tending is tied to Popeye’s ownership and brothel economics.

Chapter 19

Summary: Ruby tells Horace what happened at the Old Frenchman place while the sick child lies on the bed and men pitch dollars under the courthouse trees outside the window. She insists Temple was all right when Popeye drove her away, then reconstructs the night: Gowan drunk, Van pestering Temple, Lee’s white nostrils, the raincoat, Gowan snoring, Popeye’s brilliantine, Tommy’s catlike eyes, and Ruby taking Temple from the bed to the crib. Horace visits Miss Jenny and learns Gowan had proposed to Narcissa before fleeing, then returns to town, looks at Little Belle’s photograph, and travels to Oxford searching for Temple. At Oxford, he finds that Temple quit school two weeks earlier and sees her name penciled on the lavatory wall. Source anchors: courthouse trees; Ruby's account; brilliantine; Gowan snoring; Little Belle's photograph; Oxford; quit school; lavatory wall.

Analysis: Ruby’s account and brilliantine supply the factual case Horace needs, but her insistence that Temple was “all right” shows how little the available facts can capture. Little Belle’s photograph and Oxford turn Horace’s investigation into a confrontation with his own uneasy fixation on young female sexuality. The lavatory wall is devastating because it reduces Temple’s social identity to a dirty public inscription, the same kind of public material Jefferson will later use against Ruby and Goodwin.

Chapter 20

Summary: On the return train from Oxford, Horace encounters Clarence Snopes, a bulky political operator who calls him “Judge Benbow,” offers cigars, and pries into his search. Clarence is linked to Frenchman’s Bend, restaurant origins, family migration into Jefferson, a greasy velvet-collared overcoat, a soiled hat, and a ringed hand. Back in Jefferson, Horace tries to keep Ruby housed, but the hotel expels her after gossip about her status with Goodwin spreads. Narcissa refuses to take Ruby into her house, and Miss Jenny watches Horace discover that local opinion now treats Ruby’s presence as scandal rather than as witness protection. Source anchors: Clarence Snopes; Judge Benbow; Frenchman’s Bend; soiled hat; hotel; Ruby; Narcissa; Miss Jenny.

Analysis: Clarence Snopes introduces a commerce in information that parallels Popeye’s commerce in liquor and bodies, and the chapter is a case for Illicit Markets because the same world that traps Temple runs on buying and selling what law refuses to recognize. Frenchman’s Bend and the soiled hat mark him as a different kind of social climber from Gowan, but he is just as damaging because he monetizes Temple’s location, Ruby’s scandal, and the case’s secrets through hotel gossip, keyhole spying, and “color-blind” money. The hotel, Ruby, Narcissa, and Miss Jenny show Horace losing control of the case before trial; the community can sabotage his witness without ever touching the evidence, because the illicit market in information moves faster than the formal one in testimony.

Chapter 21

Summary: Virgil Snopes and Fonzo arrive in Memphis with imitation leather suitcases, new hats, and little knowledge of the city. They reject the Gayoso Hotel as too expensive, wander from Main Street to a dingy frame-house district, mistake Miss Reba’s brothel for a boarding house, and rent a room while misunderstanding Minnie, the women in kimonos, the bath, the mechanical piano, and Reba’s business. Fonzo grows obsessed with the women in the house, the boys attend barber’s college, spend three dollars at another brothel, and are later found by Clarence Snopes, who takes them to a Black brothel and declares money “color-blind.” Source anchors: Virgil Snopes; Fonzo; Gayoso Hotel; Miss Reba; barber’s college; three dollars; Clarence Snopes; color-blind.

Analysis: Virgil Snopes and Fonzo supply comic distance, but the comedy is acidic because their innocence feeds the same Memphis market that traps Temple. The Gayoso Hotel and barber’s college mark their naive ambitions, while Miss Reba’s house becomes legible to the reader long before it is legible to them. Clarence Snopes and “color-blind” money make explicit that racial taboo, sexual commerce, and profit are connected in the world through which Horace must seek evidence.

Chapter 22

Summary: Horace finds Ruby and the child a room in the lean-to of a half-crazed white woman reputed to make spells for Black clients, then installs a telephone so Ruby can reach him. Clarence Snopes calls with information about Temple, arrives by car at Horace’s gate, hints crudely about Horace keeping a woman, and sells the knowledge that Temple is in a Memphis brothel. The transaction takes place under moonlight, honeysuckle, whippoorwills, cedars, and Snopes’s cigar smoke, while Horace recognizes that Frenchman’s Bend information networks have located what his legal search could not. Clarence ends by saying Temple is in a Memphis “ho-house.” Source anchors: lean-to room; spell woman; telephone; Clarence Snopes; honeysuckle; whippoorwill; Memphis brothel; price.

Analysis: The lean-to room and spell woman show how far Ruby has fallen from even the temporary protection of the hotel. The telephone seems like legal preparedness, but Clarence Snopes uses another telephone to turn Temple’s location into a commodity. Honeysuckle, whippoorwill, and price make the scene morally claustrophobic: Horace buys the truth he needs, but he must buy it from a man whose assumptions about women mirror the town’s corruption.

Chapter 23

Summary: Horace goes to Miss Reba’s Memphis house, where Clarence Snopes lurks outside with another address and warns him not to stay. Miss Reba describes Snopes’s earlier visits, his pinching of girls, his cheap whiskey and Coca-Colas, his keyhole spying, and Popeye burning his neck with a match while Minnie watched from the stairs. Reba refuses to invite police trouble, says Popeye’s outside business is his own, admits she supports four children in Arkansas, and finally decides Horace is not lying. She leads him upstairs to Temple, who hides under bedclothes in a dark room, demands drink and cigarettes, and begins telling the night at the Old Frenchman place. Source anchors: Miss Reba; Clarence Snopes; keyhole; burned match; Minnie; Arkansas children; dark room; Temple's testimony.

Analysis: Miss Reba, Minnie, and the burned match make Popeye’s authority in Memphis visible without showing him in the room. Clarence Snopes’s keyhole spying also makes him a grotesque double of Horace’s inquiry: both want Temple’s story, but one sells it and one needs it for Goodwin. The dark room and Temple’s testimony show why Reba is necessary; Horace cannot reach Temple through law alone, but Reba can create a temporary space for speech.

Chapter 24

Summary: Temple tells Horace and Miss Reba about the night in the room and crib, returning again and again to the shuck mattress, Gowan’s snoring, the canteen, the raincoat, the imagined iron belt, rats, cottonseeds, corn-cobs, and her effort to think herself into a boy or an old man. Horace tries to hurry her toward the crime, but Temple narrates the earlier terror with strange pride and evasive theatricality. She recalls Popeye’s cold hand, the shucks sounding like laughter, her fantasy of lying in a coffin, and the moment she fell asleep before Ruby led her to the crib. Leaving Miss Reba’s house, Horace thinks Temple might be better dead, returns by midnight train to Jefferson, and vomits while imagining the shucks under Little Belle’s body. Source anchors: shuck mattress; canteen; iron belt; cottonseeds; cold hand; coffin; midnight train; Little Belle.

Analysis: The shuck mattress, canteen, and iron belt show Temple translating terror into objects and fantasies because direct narration would destroy her composure. Cottonseeds, cold hand, and coffin also make clear why her eventual courtroom speech will not operate like ordinary testimony. Horace’s midnight train and Little Belle hallucination are central to his collapse: he cannot keep Temple’s story separate from his own stepdaughter, so the legal case invades his body as nausea.

Chapter 25

Summary: Miss Reba and other Memphis women attend Red’s chaotic funeral, where Gene supplies free liquor, musicians debate whether to play solemn hymns or popular songs, the proprietor worries about the bier, and mourners transform the service into drinking, fighting, and spectacle. A woman in red shouts, the crap table is uncovered, the bouncer fights several men, the coffin crashes open, and Red’s corpse rolls out with a wreath wire caught in his cheek and a plugged bullet hole in his forehead. After the procession, cabs and Packards peel away until only the hearse and hired cars continue, and Miss Reba returns home with Miss Myrtle, Miss Lorraine, and Uncle Bud to drink beer and gin. Source anchors: Red's funeral; Gene; free liquor; bier; woman in red; bouncer; bullet hole; Uncle Bud.

Analysis: Red’s funeral supplies the missing aftermath of Popeye’s Memphis arrangement with Temple: the man used as proxy becomes a corpse and then a public carnival. Gene, free liquor, and the bouncer show the same appetite for spectacle that Jefferson will display at Goodwin’s trial and lynching. Uncle Bud and the women’s polite drinking afterward bring the grotesque back into domestic conversation, letting Miss Reba reveal what she knows about Popeye, Temple, and Red.

Chapter 26

Summary: Horace spends the night writing Belle for a divorce, sees Clarence Snopes with a black eye and adhesive tape from a Memphis beating, and tries to locate Narcissa, who has gone to Eustace Graham’s office. Graham, the club-footed District Attorney with a poker history, congressional ambitions, and a record of convictions to advertise, tells Narcissa that Goodwin has no chance and that Horace should be removed from the case. Narcissa reveals Snopes’s call and Horace’s Memphis trip, asks when the case will end, and later writes Belle that Horace will be home on the twenty-fourth. Snopes complains in the barbershop about a Memphis Jewish lawyer paying him less than Horace and Graham for the same information. Source anchors: divorce letter; Eustace Graham; club foot; poker game; Narcissa; twenty-fourth; black eye; Memphis lawyer.

Analysis: The divorce letter and Narcissa’s visit to Eustace Graham show Horace’s private life and the prosecution’s public machinery converging against Goodwin. Graham’s club foot and poker game matter because Faulkner makes his ambition bodily, local, and transactional rather than principled. The twenty-fourth and the Memphis lawyer show that Narcissa and Snopes both treat the case as something to manage for private advantage, leaving truth dependent on people who do not value it.

Chapter 27

Summary: On the eve of trial, Horace calls Miss Reba and learns that Temple and Popeye have vanished, with Reba worrying about unpaid rent and refusing police involvement. The trial opens with the bullet from Tommy’s skull and a stoneware jug of corn whiskey on the table, and Ruby testifies with the child on her lap in her gray dress, darned veil, and purple ornament. Eustace Graham asks about her marriage to Goodwin, Horace objects, and Goodwin accuses Horace of killing him by using Ruby. That night Horace drills Ruby in the jail cell while Goodwin sleeps, bringing milk, candy, and cigars, and Ruby reveals that she assumed Horace wanted sexual payment for his legal help. Source anchors: vanished; bullet; stoneware jug; darned veil; purple ornament; marriage question; jail cell; candy.

Analysis: Temple’s disappearance makes Ruby’s testimony the only living bridge to the Old Frenchman place, so the bullet and stoneware jug become dangerously thin evidence. The darned veil, purple ornament, and marriage question show Graham shifting the jury’s attention from murder to sexual legitimacy. The jail cell and candy scene is one of Horace’s most painful lessons: Ruby’s assumption about payment comes from experience, and Horace’s offended idealism cannot erase that history. The trial's language is Language and Ideology because naming Ruby's marital status becomes a way to disqualify what she knows.

Chapter 28

Summary: Court resumes with the square packed by wagons, overalls, khaki, sunbonnets, tobacco smell, stale sweat, and the courthouse’s musty air. Goodwin and Ruby sit at one table, while Eustace Graham produces the corn-cob found at the scene and calls Temple Drake, now dressed in a black hat, black satin, platinum bag, rouge, painted mouth, pale tan coat, and purple shoulder knot. Temple falsely identifies Goodwin as her attacker while Horace sits frozen and fails to cross-examine effectively. Judge Drake enters the courtroom, takes Temple away, and the jury returns after eight minutes with a conviction. Source anchors: courthouse; corn-cob; Temple Drake; black hat; platinum bag; Judge Drake; eight minutes; Goodwin.

Analysis: The courthouse, corn-cob, and Temple Drake’s costume convert private violation into staged public evidence, and the chapter is a case for Informal Institutions because the trial is governed by community appetite rather than by the formal procedure Horace relies on. Black hat, platinum bag, and Judge Drake show Temple restored to family display at the exact moment she destroys Goodwin, so the courtroom becomes a performance of respectability that the town reads through gossip and social rank. The eight minutes are the measure of Jefferson’s appetite for a conclusion; Horace’s silence before Goodwin reveals that legal procedure cannot work when the central witness uses it to escape her own exposure and the community’s unwritten rules have already decided the verdict.

Chapter 29

Summary: After the verdict, Horace leaves the courthouse at dusk among wagons heading twelve and sixteen miles home, sees Ruby standing near the jail fence with the child so Goodwin can see them, and breaks down in Narcissa’s car. Crowds linger around the jail, drummers comment that Jefferson lets Goodwin get away with “that corn cob,” and the night marshal tells people the show is over. Later the mob returns, pulls Goodwin from the jail, and burns him, while Horace hears reports and begins to leave Jefferson. The chapter makes the legal conviction the prelude to extrajudicial punishment rather than the end of the case. Source anchors: verdict; jail fence; Ruby and child; drummers; night marshal; show's over; mob; burned.

Analysis: The jail fence and Ruby and child make Goodwin’s last human attachment visible after the verdict. The drummers and night marshal use entertainment language, which shows how trial, gossip, and lynching belong to one public performance. The mob and burned body complete what the eight-minute jury began; Jefferson’s law supplies authorization, then Jefferson’s crowd supplies the fire, collapsing Legitimacy and Succession into ritualized punishment without truth.

Chapter 30

Summary: Horace reaches Kinston by train, encounters the old hack driver who reports that a man was burned in Jefferson, calls Belle, and enters the domestic life he had fled. The chapter turns to Popeye’s origins: his mother’s brief marriage, his Christmas birth, delayed walking and talking, fires set by his grandmother, the house burning, his frail body, a doctor’s warning that alcohol would kill him, and his mutilation of lovebirds and a kitten. Popeye later writes from Mobile, New Orleans, and Memphis, visits his mother in Pensacola, and is arrested for a killing in a town where he was not present because he was elsewhere committing another murder. He refuses meaningful legal help, treats the trial with boredom, is convicted in eight minutes, and waits in jail with cigarettes and Ed Pinaud lotion. Source anchors: Kinston; Belle; Christmas birth; lovebirds; kitten; Pensacola; eight minutes; Ed Pinaud.

Analysis: Kinston and Belle show Horace’s retreat, but the chapter immediately denies him closure by giving Popeye a biography. Christmas birth, lovebirds, kitten, and the doctor’s alcohol warning make Popeye’s violence both monstrous and sterile, not the romantic outlawry Jefferson imagines. Pensacola, eight minutes, and Ed Pinaud place him inside another court system where guilt and conviction again fail to match; he is punished, but not for Tommy, Temple, Ruby, or Goodwin.

Chapter 31

Summary: Popeye spends his last days in a gray jail cell, smoking, ignoring newspapers, refusing a Memphis lawyer, counting cigarette stubs in twelve wall spaces, and letting a minister pray beside him. At six o’clock he is taken to the scaffold, where his concern is not guilt, repentance, Tommy, Temple, or Goodwin, but the hair displaced by the rope; he asks the sheriff to fix it before the trap springs. The novel then moves to the Luxembourg Gardens, where Temple and Judge Drake sit amid chestnut trees, toy boats, four sous chairs, damp music by Massenet, Scriabin, and Berlioz, and women knitting in shawls. Temple yawns, opens a compact, and appears sullen, discontented, and sad beside her father under the gray Parisian weather. Source anchors: cigarette stubs; twelve spaces; minister; scaffold; fix my hair; Luxembourg Gardens; Judge Drake; compact.

Analysis: Cigarette stubs, twelve spaces, and the minister reduce Popeye’s final days to ritual without remorse. The scaffold and “fix my hair” request show that his execution supplies no moral balancing for Goodwin’s death; it is another official act attached to the wrong crime. Luxembourg Gardens, Judge Drake, and the compact finish Temple’s removal from Mississippi, but the gray weather and her sullen face make Paris a relocation of damage, not a cure.
