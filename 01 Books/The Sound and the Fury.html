The Sound and the Fury

Detailed overview

Faulkner builds The Sound and the Fury around four dated sections that circle the Compson household from radically different angles: Benjy's April 7, 1928 consciousness, Quentin's June 2, 1910 day in Cambridge, Jason's April 6, 1928 narration of household control and theft, and the Easter Sunday section of April 8, 1928 centered on Dilsey's work and endurance. Caddy Compson is the person most wanted by the first three narrators, but she is never given a narrating section; the book makes her visible through Benjy's sensory dependence, Quentin's sexual panic, Jason's financial exploitation, and the appendix's later record of divorce, exile, and wartime disappearance.

The Benjy section does not treat memory as background. Golfers calling "caddie" across the former Compson pasture, Luster's search for a lost quarter, the broken fence, Damuddy's funeral, Caddy's muddy drawers in the pear tree, and the repeated claim that Caddy smelled like trees all become triggers that collapse decades into one continuous present. The Compson decline is therefore first registered as physical loss: pasture becomes golf course, Caddy leaves the house, Benjy's name is changed from Maury, and the family learns to manage him with firelight, servants, and avoidance rather than love.

Quentin's Harvard section translates the same household disaster into a theology of time, virginity, and family honor. Grandfather's watch keeps ticking after Quentin breaks its hands; Shreve, Spoade, Deacon, Gerald Bland, and the little Italian girl keep pulling him into ordinary Cambridge life; and Caddy, Dalton Ames, Herbert Head, and Mr. Compson's fatalism keep returning him to Mississippi. The two flat-irons Quentin buys are not an isolated symbol but the practical instrument by which his obsession with Caddy's sexuality and his father's skepticism about purity end in the Charles River.

Jason's April 6 narration narrows the family ruin into account books, store work, school discipline, and revenge. Professor Junkin's report card call, Quentin's cut classes, Dilsey's attempts to shield the girl, Earl's store, Job's cultivators, cotton-market worry, Caddy's checks for her daughter, and Jason's habit of burning decoy checks for Caroline show him turning injured gentility into petty embezzlement. He understands the Compson name mostly as license to punish women, resent Black labor, and convert Caddy's money into his own locked hoard.

The Easter section gives the novel its only sustained view from outside Compson self-enclosure. Dilsey's cold morning labor, Caroline's hot water bottle, Luster's lateness after the show, Quentin's broken window, Reverend Shegog's sermon, Jason's failed pursuit to Mottson, and Benjy's panic at the wrong turn around the Confederate monument place the household inside a larger Black religious and communal life that the Compsons neither control nor understand. The appendix then stretches that household backward to Ikkemotubbe, Andrew Jackson's land patent, Quentin MacLachan after Culloden, Jason Lycurgus on the Natchez Trace, the Compson Domain, and forward to Benjamin's commitment at Jackson State Asylum, making the novel's broken Easter weekend one late episode in a longer history of land seizure, planter fantasy, and family exhaustion.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

April Seventh, 1928

Summary: Benjy's section begins on April 7, 1928, his thirty-third birthday, with Luster hunting through the grass for a lost quarter so he can attend the show while golfers call for their caddies beyond the fence. The word "caddie" throws Benjy into memories of Caddy freeing him from the broken fence, Versh and T.P. leading him through the yard, Uncle Maury's letter to Mrs Patterson, Damuddy's death, and Caddy climbing the pear tree with muddy drawers to see into the parlor. The day also contains Miss Quentin's secret meetings with a man in a red tie, Luster's teasing with matches and a golf ball, Dilsey's birthday cake, Caroline Compson's complaints, Jason's threats, and the sight of Quentin using the window and tree in a repetition of Caddy's old escape route. Source anchors: Luster's quarter, golf flag, Caddy smell, Mrs Patterson letter, Damuddy, muddy drawers, red tie, birthday cake.

Analysis: Luster's quarter gives the section its immediate 1928 errand, but the golf flag marks what has happened to the Compson pasture and lets a commercial leisure landscape call up Caddy's name by accident. Caddy smell, Damuddy, and muddy drawers make Knowledge Preservation bodily rather than explanatory: Benjy preserves the family story through cold, smell, firelight, crying, and obstruction instead of chronology. Mrs Patterson letter shows adult sexuality already being routed through children, while red tie and birthday cake bring the same pattern into Miss Quentin's generation, where Caddy's daughter repeats the escape from a house that has learned nothing except harsher surveillance.

June Second, 1910

Summary: Quentin's section follows June 2, 1910 in Cambridge, where Grandfather's watch pulls him back into time even after he breaks the crystal and removes the hands. He moves among Harvard rooms, Shreve, Spoade, streetcars, shops, the jeweller's window, Deacon, the bridge, and the Charles River while his thoughts return to Caddy, Dalton Ames, Herbert Head, Benjy, Father, and the idea of confessing incest as a way to preserve Caddy from ordinary marriage. The purchase of two flat-irons gives his wandering a physical destination, and encounters with the little Italian girl, Gerald Bland, and Mrs Bland show Cambridge as a place full of social scripts he cannot inhabit without translating them back into Compson disgrace. Source anchors: Grandfather's watch, Shreve, Dalton Ames, Herbert Head, two flat-irons, jeweller, Deacon, Charles River.

Analysis: Grandfather's watch and the jeweller make time a material pressure rather than a philosophical topic, because Quentin cannot stop either the mechanism or the public world that reads clocks normally. Dalton Ames and Herbert Head turn Caddy's choices into rival forms of male insult: one associated with sexual knowledge, the other with marriage and money. Shreve, Deacon, and the Charles River keep the section anchored in Cambridge, while two flat-irons convert Quentin's talk of honor, virginity, and family extinction into the practical means of suicide.

April Sixth, 1928

Summary: Jason's section opens on April 6, 1928 with Professor Junkin's report-card call exposing Miss Quentin's absence from school and Caroline Compson retreating into injury while Jason uses the news to attack the girl. At breakfast and in the kitchen, Dilsey tries to protect Quentin from Jason's belt and insults, and Jason then drives her toward school while calculating costs such as $11.65 books and reminding her that Caroline's money supposedly bought her clothes. At Earl's store he works among Job, cultivators, customers, cotton-market talk, telegraph anxieties, and racist complaint, while Caddy's check for Quentin's Easter dress reveals the central fraud: Jason intercepts Caddy's letters, cashes her support money, and stages burned checks for Caroline so that Caddy remains excluded from her daughter. Source anchors: Professor Junkin, report card, Dilsey, belt, $11.65 books, Earl, Job, Caddy check.

Analysis: Professor Junkin and the report card let Jason present his violence as household discipline, though the belt and Dilsey's intervention show that the scene is really about power over a young woman he resents because she is Caddy's child. Earl and Job place Jason in a middling commercial world far below the planter grandeur remembered in the appendix, and his fixation on $11.65 books makes even schooling a debit entry. The Caddy check exposes Jason's inverted Moral Economy: he turns support meant for Quentin into a private hoard, monetizes Caroline's weakness and Caddy's exclusion, and calls theft family duty.

April Eighth, 1928

Summary: The Easter Sunday section begins in cold rain with Dilsey leaving her cabin, dressing against the weather, starting the kitchen fire, feeding Benjy, and answering Caroline's repeated demands for a hot water bottle. Luster is late after the show, Jason discovers the broken window and Quentin's disappearance, and the house reacts through accusation, illness, and frantic search rather than grief. Dilsey takes Benjy, Frony, and Luster to Reverend Shegog's church, where the visiting preacher's Easter sermon gives Dilsey language for having seen the first and last of the Compsons; later Jason fails in his pursuit toward Mottson, and Benjy's ride with Luster only becomes calm when Jason forces Queenie back onto the expected route around the Confederate monument. Source anchors: Easter Sunday, Dilsey cabin, hot water bottle, Luster, broken window, Reverend Shegog, first and last, Queenie.

Analysis: Easter Sunday places resurrection language beside a household that can only repeat damage, and Dilsey cabin gives the day a starting point outside the Compson bedrooms. The hot water bottle and broken window contrast Caroline's cultivated helplessness with Quentin's decisive escape, leaving Dilsey to perform the labor that holds the morning together. Reverend Shegog and first and last make Religion and Legitimacy judge the Compsons from outside their own decayed status claims, while Queenie and the Confederate monument show Benjy's peace depending on a ritual route through the remnants of white family order.

Editor's Note

Summary: The Editor's Note explains that the corrected text rests on comparison among Faulkner's holograph manuscript, the carbon typescript in the Faulkner Collection at Alderman Library, and the 1929 first edition published by Jonathan Cape & Harrison Smith. It describes the editorial aim of recovering Faulkner's final intentions while admitting that no setting copy or galley proofs survive to settle every choice. The note tracks the book's movement from the early title Twilight, through Harcourt Brace's rejection, Harrison Smith's new firm, Ben Wasson's instructions about italics and line breaks, the October 7, 1929 publication, Noel Polk's 1984 corrected edition, and Joseph Blotner's later notes for the Library of America text. Source anchors: holograph manuscript, carbon typescript, Alderman Library, 1929 first edition, Noel Polk, Ben Wasson, Joseph Blotner.

Analysis: The holograph manuscript, carbon typescript, and Alderman Library matter because The Sound and the Fury depends on visual and typographic distinctions, especially in Benjy's and Quentin's sections, where italics and line breaks help readers track temporal movement. Ben Wasson and the 1929 first edition show that production decisions shaped the printed novel as well as Faulkner's manuscript, so the corrected text cannot simply claim transparent access to authorial intention. Noel Polk and Joseph Blotner turn Knowledge Preservation into editorial practice by treating wording, punctuation, and layout as evidence that must be repaired, compared, and preserved.

Note to the Appendix

Summary: The Note to the Appendix records Faulkner's 1946 message to Random House that he had written a new section for future reprintings and considered it a key to the book. That section was prepared for The Portable Faulkner, edited by Malcolm Cowley, and appeared the same year in the Modern Library dual edition of The Sound and the Fury and As I Lay Dying. The note explains that Faulkner initially wanted the appendix before the novel, that readers and scholars have debated whether it belongs inside the book or beside other Compson materials, and that Noel Polk edited the present text from Faulkner's carbon typescript. Source anchors: Random House, 1946, Portable Faulkner, Malcolm Cowley, Modern Library, Noel Polk, carbon typescript.

Analysis: Random House and 1946 locate the appendix as Faulkner's retrospective intervention, written after the novel had already entered print and after Cowley was reshaping Faulkner's public reputation. Portable Faulkner, Malcolm Cowley, and Modern Library explain why the appendix reads differently from the four dated sections: it is compressed genealogy and authorial chronicle rather than narrated consciousness. Noel Polk and the carbon typescript keep that retrospective material under the same textual scrutiny as the novel proper, which matters because the appendix changes how readers understand Caddy, Quentin, Benjamin, Jason, and the Compson land.

Appendix: Compson: 1699-1945

Summary: The appendix retells the Compson line from Ikkemotubbe, the dispossessed Chickasaw ruler also called Doom, through Andrew Jackson's land patent, Quentin MacLachan's flight from Culloden, and Charles Stuart Compson's wandering before the family takes root in Mississippi. Jason Lycurgus gains the Compson Domain through dealings with Ikkemotubbe's descendants and Natchez Trace horse culture, while later Compsons turn the property into a governor's house, a failed Confederate inheritance, mortgages, pasture sales, bungalows, and a golf course. The appendix then follows Quentin III's 1910 suicide, Candace's marriage to Sydney Herbert Head, divorce, Hollywood remarriage, Mexico divorce, Paris disappearance, Melissa Meek's wartime photograph, Jason IV's hoard and childlessness, Benjamin's castration and commitment to Jackson State Asylum, and Caddy's daughter Quentin escaping with Jason's money. Source anchors: Ikkemotubbe, Jackson, Quentin MacLachan, Jason Lycurgus, Compson Domain, Quentin III, Candace, Jason IV.

Analysis: Ikkemotubbe and Jackson make Compson property originate in dispossession and federal power, so Empire and Periphery frames the family's later losses as the afterlife of an earlier taking rather than pure tragedy. Quentin MacLachan, Jason Lycurgus, and Compson Domain give the family the kind of martial and political ancestry that Quentin III tries to convert into a code of honor, even though the dated sections show that code collapsing inside ordinary rooms, schools, stores, and roads. Candace and Jason IV receive the appendix's harshest afterlife: Caddy is pushed through marriage, exile, and rumor, while Jason's locked money and treatment of Benjamin reveal the final Compson heir as a custodian of cash, punishment, and institutional disposal.
