Place in the vault

This note lives under Empire, Trade, and Maritime Worlds. Use it as a North Sea synthesis node for medieval money, book circulation, Viking settlement, urban law, water management, plague regulation, and the commercial institutions that later feed Dutch and Atlantic expansion.

Detailed overview

Michael Pye begins with Cecil Warburton drinking Scarborough spa water in 1700, a scene that looks like leisure but hides cod heads, coarse hooks, coal boats, drying fish, and the labouring harbour. The book uses that contrast as its method. Pye keeps pulling the reader away from the later seaside fantasy of "Nobility, Quality and Gentry" toward the North Sea as a place of ferries, cargo holds, drowned settlements, salt marshes, abbeys, pirate fleets, and trading offices. Scarborough, Scheveningen, Domburg, Ostend, Boulogne, and Norderney are not picturesque backdrops; they are reminders that the sea made connections before nations made stories about themselves.

The Domburg material shows how Pye handles evidence. Storms in 1647, 1687, 1715, 1749, 1817, 1832, and 1866 expose Nehalennia altars, coffins, skulls, coins, brooches, wells, and a headless Victory on the Zeeland shore. Pye treats those discoveries as a rebuke to tidy national memory. The same beach holds Roman votive stones from merchants, the trace of St Willibrord's mission around 690, Alcuin's story of smashing an idol, and the aftermark of the Viking raid of 837. The sea preserves and destroys at once, and the book keeps returning to that rhythm: a storm, a cargo, a legal record, or a shipwreck suddenly makes visible a buried order of life.

The first half of the book follows practices rather than dynasties. Frisians on terpen around the Aelmere and Zuiderzee turn trade in salt, fish sauce, wine, cloth, pottery, and slaves into a habit of calculation, so money becomes a way to imagine value across distance. Monks at Wearmouth, Jarrow, Lindisfarne, York, Corbie, Murbach, and Tours make books into portable technology: separated words, punctuation, computus, Vulgate manuscripts, and copied Virgil all travel with gifts, letters, hawks, and borrowed exemplars. Vikings then appear as traders, explorers, slave dealers, raiders, and settlers, not simply as enemies. Pye moves from Ohthere's walrus ivory and the silver routes through the Volga to Dublin, York, Waterford, L'Anse aux Meadows, and the towns that grew where raiding became settlement.

The middle chapters show how social forms crossed the same water. Bergen shoes, Lucca silk, Greenland collars, French V-necks, Burgundian wardrobes, and Philip Stubbes's fury over clothing make fashion a record of social choice. Ordeals by cold water, Irish legal pleadings, runesticks from Bergen, the Fourth Lateran Council's ban on clerical participation in ordeal, and Bruges commercial courts show law moving from sacred tests toward written procedures, witnesses, notaries, intent, and debt enforcement. Pye then turns the North Sea shoreline into a technical laboratory: Waterland peat drainage lowers the land, the Aelmere becomes the Zuider Zee, dikes and water boards demand collective finance, and herring towns like Walraversijde make profit from a nature they can never fully command.

The later chapters connect calculation, commerce, and control. Terror of Mongols in 1241, apocalyptic readings of Alexander's wall and enclosed Jews, Roger Bacon's mathematics, university bursa, Ockham, Thomas Aquinas, Olivi, and Oresme all tie knowledge to money and measurement. The Hansa chapter makes that connection institutional: Lubeck, Bergen, Novgorod, London, Bruges, Scania, Gotland, and the Oresund produce an economic power with Kontors, initiations, stockfish, grain blockades, pirate wars, and Low German habits. Pye's account of beguines, Katelijne Vedelaer, Mergriete van Ecke, Ida of Louvain, Hadewijch, and Marie d'Oignies then places women inside the same commercial world as workers, lenders, teachers, household managers, mystics, and targets of marriage-by-abduction.

The plague chapter is the book's sharpest statement about how crisis produced administration. The Black Death brings Llywelyn Fychan's "brittle coal fragments," the Brut's villages emptied near Worcester and Cambridgeshire, Paris doctors advising flight, yersinia pestis, theriac, 1315 famine, the Statute of Labourers, Mile End in 1381, Edinburgh's 1498 rules, Scottish quarantine against ships from Gdansk, Pepys at Holehaven, and travel papers in Geneva. Pye argues through examples rather than a slogan: plague fear gave civil authorities reasons to fix workers, inspect ships, classify the poor, expel vagrants, demand passes, and make movement conditional.

The final chapter gathers the book's materials in Burgundy, Antwerp, Amsterdam, and Simon Stevin. Leo of Rozmital sees nuns dancing at Neuss, Burgundian dinners with eight dishes, Bruges markets full of lemons, furs, Rhine wine, sugar, saffron, Hansa merchants, Italian bankers, and gallows after rebellion. Burgundy invents political spectacle through the Order of the Golden Fleece, Jan van Eyck, Hans Memling, tapestries, ars nova, tableaux, triumphal entries, and a gilt wooden throne pretending to be gold. Antwerp adds bourse speculation, insurance, art dealers, and global information; Stevin adds decimals, Dutch technical vocabulary, Leiden's Duytsche Mathematique, sluices, town planning, and imperial cities such as Recife, Colombo, Cape Town, and Paramaribo. Pye closes by insisting that the "edge" was not peripheral: Frisian boats, Northumbrian manuscripts, Norse voyages, written law, dikes, Hansa credit, beguine households, plague passes, and Dutch mathematics supplied many of the habits later called modern.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Introduction

Summary: Pye opens at Scarborough in 1700 with Cecil Warburton, a Cheshire baronet's son, swallowing five pints of acidic spa water while refusing the full four-quart cure taken by other visitors. Scarborough's later guidebook world of earls, baronets, marchionesses, bathing huts, and social display is set against cod heads, drying fish, coarse hooks, coal boats from Billingsgate, and the harbour's labour. The introduction then moves to Domburg in Zeeland, where storms in 1647 exposed Nehalennia stones, altars to Neptune and Hercules, shell canopies, a dog, ship prows, apples, and stones carried from northern France. Later storms and low tides in 1687, 1715, 1749, 1817, 1832, and 1866 uncover coffins, skulls, brooches, a silver dagger, wells, buildings, and a headless Victory. Pye links that shoreline to St Willibrord on Walcheren around 690, Alcuin's idol-smashing story, a Viking raid in 837, and the dangers of later national myths built from Renan, Hobsbawm, Bede, Gildas, Vortigern, Hengist, Horsa, the Belgae, and the Saxon Shore. Source anchors: Scarborough waters, Nehalennia, Domburg storms, St Willibrord, Viking raid 837, Hengist and Horsa.

Analysis: Scarborough waters and Domburg storms give Pye two ways to make the North Sea visible: Warburton's spa visit shows a later leisure culture covering over cod heads and Billingsgate coal, while Nehalennia stones and Zeeland coffins show the sea exposing records that written national history had buried. St Willibrord, Alcuin, and Viking raid 837 put Christian mission, pagan worship, and seaborne violence on the same Walcheren shore, so the introduction trains the reader to expect overlapping evidence rather than a single origin story. Bede, Gildas, Vortigern, Hengist and Horsa are named because Pye wants the English "Anglo-Saxon" inheritance to look manufactured, not because he is replacing one nationalist beginning with another.

1. The invention of money

Summary: The first chapter begins with Pliny's salt-marsh people and the Frisians who lived along the "Frisian Sea" around Dorestad, the Aelmere, the Zuiderzee, Domburg, and Zeeland. Pye treats Frisian trade as the place where abstract value becomes everyday practice: merchants move salt, fish sauce, potter's clay, wine, cloth, pottery, cattle, sheep, dairy, meat, fish, slaves, wool, parchment, and leather across the Rhine, Calais, York, London, Southampton, Jutland, Birka, and Helgo. Nehalennia's temples at Colijnsplaat and Domburg connect trade, death, fertility, and safe passage, while thirty-metre Rhine barges and flat-bottomed Frisian craft make shallow-water commerce possible. Bede's story of a Frisian merchant buying a Northumbrian youth, Altfrid's account of Liudger's Frisian colony in York, the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle's notice of Frisian ship design in 896, Saint-Denis wine-fair records, and St Goar miracle stories supply written traces of the same network. The chapter also stresses terpen, grasswrack, sod walls, cooperation, and the relative freedom of marsh communities that had to manage water before lords could easily manage them. Source anchors: Frisian Sea, Dorestad, Nehalennia, terpen, Liudger in York, Frisian ship design.

Analysis: Dorestad, Nehalennia, and the terpen let Pye tie money to a physical landscape where small communities had to cooperate against water and then trade beyond it. Bede's Northumbrian youth, Liudger's York colony, and the 896 Frisian ship design matter because they move the Frisians out of archaeological guesswork and into named channels of slavery, migration, and technical borrowing. The chapter makes calculation a North Sea habit before it becomes a capitalist doctrine: salt, wine, cloth, slaves, and silver require people in Domburg, York, Saint-Denis, and St Goar to compare unlike goods across distance.

2. The book trade

Summary: Pye's second chapter turns from coins and cargo to books at Jarrow, Wearmouth, Lindisfarne, York, Corbie, Murbach, Tours, and Charlemagne's court. Bede's world is small in travel terms, but the manuscripts around him are cosmopolitan: Irish scribes separate words and develop punctuation, St Ambrose's silent reading becomes imaginable, Benedict Biscop trades a book of cosmographies for land, and the Vulgate tradition at Jarrow and Wearmouth depends on a manuscript from Naples. Boniface writes to Eadburga for gold-lettered Epistles as his eyesight fails, sends hawks and falcons to the King of Mercia, and uses letters to keep missionary alliances alive. Alcuin carries York learning to Charlemagne, Corbie copies and recopies texts, Murbach's librarian inventories books, and Bede's own list of works shows a scholar conscious of authorship. The chapter also follows Burginda, Ecburg, Eccard of Macon, Anskar's forty stolen books, Loup de Ferrieres worrying about robbers, AElfric Bata's scribe wanting cash, Dhuoda writing for William, and Gottschalk's predestination books burned after Quierzy in 849. Source anchors: Bede at Jarrow, Boniface and Eadburga, Benedict Biscop, Alcuin at court, Dhuoda, Quierzy 849.

Analysis: Bede at Jarrow, Wearmouth, and Lindisfarne shows a monk who scarcely travels living inside a North Sea system of Knowledge Preservation. Irish spacing and punctuation make texts easier to copy and read; Benedict Biscop and Boniface move exemplars through gifts and correspondence; Dhuoda writes authority across family separation. Gottschalk's books burned at Quierzy 849 reveal the same infrastructure's vulnerability, while Alcuin carries preserved techniques from York into Charlemagne's imperial centre.

3. Making enemies

Summary: The third chapter refuses to introduce Vikings only at the moment of raid. Pye first describes amber, furs, walrus ivory, square sails, tacking, woven wool sails, sun compasses, and routes to Byzantium, Kiev, the Black Sea, the Mediterranean, Spain, and North America. The Danes build an oak palisade in Jutland after Charles Martel takes Frisia in 734, while finds at Helgo, including a Buddha, a Coptic bronze ladle, and an Irish crozier, show northern trade reaching far beyond the North Sea. Ohthere speaks at King Alfred's court about Halogaland, the White Sea, walrus ivory, and six hundred reindeer, while dirhams and silver move through Russia, the Volga, and Constantinople. The chapter then turns to slave trading, Arab demand, Gaelic women in Iceland, Tyrkir, Paul the Deacon, Charlemagne's Saxon wars, Christmas 800, forced conversion, Anskar's bribery and bought boys, and the Heliand's warrior Jesus. Viking raids on Aquitaine in 799, Iona in 795, 802, and 806, Kells in 807, Frisia's hundred pounds of tribute, Ghent, Boulogne, Ermentar, Blathmac, Dicuil, and the Faroes make the enemy category vivid and useful to Christian writers. Source anchors: Helgo Buddha, Ohthere, walrus ivory, Christmas 800, Heliand, Iona raids.

Analysis: Helgo's Buddha, Ohthere's walrus ivory, and dirhams on the Volga make the Vikings merchants and explorers before Aquitaine, Iona, and Kells make them enemies. That sequence matters because Pye shows hostility being manufactured from contact: Charles Martel's Frisia, Charlemagne's Saxon laws, Anskar's mission tactics, and the Heliand's warrior Christ turn northern mobility into a religious and military opposition. The Iona raids and Frisia tribute then become evidence for a Christian need to name a foe, even as the same seafarers bring towns, silver, and routes into being.

4. Settling

Summary: The fourth chapter follows what happens when raiders stay. Irish records show attacks on St Patrick's Island, Inishmurray, Iona, Howth in 821, Clonmore on Christmas Eve, Armagh, the Boyne, the Liffey in 837 with sixty ships on each river, and winter camps at Lough Neagh. In 849 a "King of Foreigners" arrives with 140 ships, and the Irish distinction between Finngaill and Dubgaill marks factions among Danes, Norwegians, and other Viking groups. Pye then shifts to Dublin, Waterford, Leinster, Munster, York, and the trade in Irish metalwork, walrus ivory, amber, and furs, noting marriages and alliances that complicate any simple invader story. Irish monasteries such as Armagh and Clonmacnoise appear as towns, forts, dynastic storehouses, sanctuaries, and economic targets; Clonmacnoise has a bridge 160 metres by 5 metres, and Nendrum has tidal mills around 620. Dublin's palisaded embankment, wattle fences, gravel streets, end-on houses, money economy, Amlaib's poets, Jorvik after 866, Skye Arab coins, Gigha scales, L'Anse aux Meadows, Leif Eriksson, Tyrkir, Freydis, grapes, butternuts, and Svein Asleifarson's seasonal raiding extend settlement from Ireland to Vinland. Source anchors: Liffey 837, Finngaill and Dubgaill, Clonmacnoise bridge, Dublin embankment, Jorvik 866, L'Anse aux Meadows.

Analysis: Liffey 837, Finngaill and Dubgaill, and the Lough Neagh winter camps let Pye show raiding becoming residence, faction, and local politics. Clonmacnoise bridge, Nendrum, and Armagh explain why monasteries drew attacks: they were not merely holy places but bridges, mills, stores, dynastic assets, and sanctuaries. Dublin embankment, Jorvik 866, and L'Anse aux Meadows make settlement a city-making and route-making process, while Leif Eriksson, Tyrkir, Freydis, and Svein Asleifarson keep the Norse world mobile even after it has built streets.

5. Fashion

Summary: The fifth chapter treats clothing as a record of trade, status, gender fear, and personal choice. Pye starts with Bergen's fire of 1248, St Mary's, Matthew Paris's ship mast, and Kali from Orkneyinga Saga, whose Grimsby and Bergen connections make him a North Sea dandy. Snorri's stories of Olaf the Gentle, tight breeches, gold rings, trailing gowns, high embroidered shoes, and Erling's old-fashioned clothes in 1174 lead into Bergen shoes with silk embroidery, Lucca silk, Bruges and Ghent cloth, Jeanne of Navarre's comment on queens, and Greenland finds with tailored garments, buttonholes, collars, madder red, imported English or Irish cloth, and vadmal used as money. The chapter then moves through Robert of Naples, Florence bans on V-necks, Alexander Barclay, gold-woven wills from 863, monastic rules against dyed and tight clothing, Paris Parlement uniforms in 1303, fur hierarchy, Saint-Denis tournaments in 1389, the Roman de la Rose's Faker, and sumptuary laws in France, England, Hainault, and Scotland. Jehan de Meun, Mondeville's cosmetics, Richard the Redeless, St Birgitta's plague clothes, Cain and Abel's red socks, Christine de Pizan, William Harrison, Burgundian wardrobes, Thomas Smith, the Pastons, Le Menagier de Paris, Deserps's 1562 fashion book, Vecellio's 1589 costume book, and Philip Stubbes's 1583 Anatomie of Abuses show anxiety about clothes crossing rank and sex. Source anchors: Kali dandy, Bergen shoes, Greenland buttonholes, Paris Parlement 1303, Roman de la Rose, Philip Stubbes.

Analysis: Kali dandy, Bergen shoes, and Greenland buttonholes make fashion concrete before Pye turns to moral panic, so the reader sees cloth as imported silk, tailored wool, and embroidered leather rather than as a vague sign of change. Paris Parlement 1303, fur hierarchy, and sumptuary laws show authorities trying to make dress legible when Bruges cloth, Lucca silk, and Burgundian wardrobes let people buy new appearances. Philip Stubbes, Roman de la Rose, Christine de Pizan, and St Birgitta matter because their anger locates the fear: clothing from the North Sea trade let a ploughman, courtier, woman, monk, or foreigner become harder to classify on sight.

6. Writing the law

Summary: The sixth chapter begins with ordeal by cold water: a suspect in a wool shirt, bound hands, a rope tied to the longest hair, watercress, holy water, and a test in which sinking or floating decides guilt before any judge, jury, or evidence. Pye then contrasts that sacred test with written legal cultures in Ireland, Wales, Iceland, Cloyne, Cork, Slane, and the seventy-seven Irish legal texts, where complaint, denial, proof, rejoinder, counter-reply, witness lists, notaries, and red ink turn conflict into procedure. Runes and futhark appear through Kali Kolsson, victory runes, sea runes, birth runes, the Dynna and Hillersjo stones, Hrossketil, Thorir, Ragnelf, Mael Lochon, and Bergen runesticks carrying love messages to Gyda. Hot iron, boiling water, cold water, the Council of Tribur in 895, Eucharistic priests, knights after 1119, women with hot iron, Isolde, Gudrun, Poppo, Harold, and Innocent III's Fourth Lateran Council make the ordeal's decline specific. Pye follows the change into Roman law and custom through Gregory VII, Arras and Ghent charters, the murder of Charles the Good in 1127, Jan van den Berghe, Bruges courts in 1190, Hildebrandt Veckinchusen's debt, a Genoa banker in prison, and cases about madness involving Richard le Pessoner in 1285 and Hugh of St Martin in 1298. Source anchors: cold water ordeal, seventy-seven Irish texts, Bergen runesticks, Fourth Lateran Council, Charles the Good, Bruges courts 1190.

Analysis: The cold water ordeal, hot iron, and Eucharistic priests force truth through sacred matter; the seventy-seven Irish texts and red-ink pleadings create Legibility by translating accusation, denial, witnesses, and counterclaims into inspectable procedure. Bergen runesticks show that portable marks are not confined to clerical Latin. After the Fourth Lateran Council, Bruges courts can classify debt, murder, madness, and intent through records and notaries instead of waiting for God to float a body.

7. Overseeing nature

Summary: The seventh chapter begins with Matthew Paris's storm of 1 October 1250 and then follows lowland attempts to manage water, peat, fish, and land. Waterland north of Amsterdam drains peat, oxidation lowers the land by about two centimetres a year, Medemblik and the Aelmere help become the Zuider Zee, and dikes between 1100 and 1300 turn maintenance into collective survival. Dutch settlers in North Germany, the Bishop of Bremen and Hamburg, Dithmarscha, the slogan "free ground with free people," the Rhee canal, Romney Marsh, and water boards in Flanders show engineering tied to law and investment. Peat feeds breweries, brickworks, cloth dyeing, sugar refining, Antwerp industry, and the Norfolk Broads, while water taxes, abbeys, limited liability, Biervliet's abandonment, Monnickendam's cannon at Nieuwendam, a beheaded mayor, Haarlemermeer's 40,000 acres, and sluice eels of 10 to 20 tons make water politics material. Pye then moves through dairy cleanliness, Antonio de Beatis in 1517, Guicciardini in 1567, Amsterdam's miracle, Waterland skippers, Baltic trade, Kampen butter and cheese, the fishbone shift around 1000, herring, fishponds, pike, hunting parks, rabbits, the Peasants' Revolt of 1381, carp, and Walraversijde's herring, pirates, brothel, brewery, chapel, Genoa velvet, pomegranates, figs, melagueta, Spanish majolica, Dogger Bank finance, and abandonment. Source anchors: storm of 1250, Waterland peat, Zuider Zee, Haarlemermeer, Kampen butter, Walraversijde.

Analysis: Waterland peat, the Zuider Zee, and Haarlemermeer make Ecological Constraint cumulative: urban fuel extraction oxidizes and lowers peat, lower land demands dikes and sluices, and those defenses require taxes, water boards, investors, and coercion. Kampen butter and Waterland skippers show the wealth created by adapting to that environment. Walraversijde and Dogger Bank show the limit, because fish markets and engineering cannot prevent storms, silting, and abandonment from reclaiming profitable settlements.

8. Science and money

Summary: The eighth chapter begins with fear of Mongols: Ivo of Narbonne, William of Rubruck, Hungary, Poland, Russia, Novgorod, the Teutonic Knights, Bohemia, Saxony, Genghis Khan, Ogedei, Kiev's bones, catapults, gunpowder, couriers, signalling, John of Plano Carpini, and rumours of Christian Mongols. Pye shows terror being interpreted through Alexander's wall, enclosed Jews, the Jewish calendar's year 5000, Prague in 1235, blood libels, Gotland and Friesland boats stopped from Yarmouth in 1237-38, Joachim of Fiore's 1260 expectation, and Roger Bacon's costly dreams of machines and books. The chapter then moves from omen to explanation: Alcuin reads blood rain and Lindisfarne as signs, William of Conches explains blood rain through heat, wind, and moisture, Adelard of Bath insists on natural causes, Grosseteste values observation, the History of Norway describes volcanoes and boiling Iceland, and Roger Bacon turns optics and mathematics into tools. Universities enter through bursa, computus, nations, Jacques de Vitry's stereotypes, Paris brothels and schools, Petrus de Arenciaco's lease, the Paris strike of 1229, Cambridge, friars versus secular masters, Ockham's mathematics, Thomas Aquinas on just price, Olivi on profit, Henry of Ghent, Oresme's Traite des monnaies, Charles V, and coinage as a moral measure. Source anchors: Mongols 1241, Alexander's wall, Roger Bacon, William of Conches, Paris strike 1229, Oresme.

Analysis: Mongols 1241 in Hungary, Kiev, and Poland give Pye a shock that forces Europeans to ask how the world works, while Alexander's wall and blood libels show old explanatory habits producing persecution and panic. William of Conches, Adelard of Bath, Grosseteste, and Roger Bacon matter because they move inquiry toward mechanisms: heat, moisture, optics, mathematics, and observation. Paris strike 1229, Ockham, Thomas Aquinas, Olivi, and Oresme bring the chapter back to money, showing that calculation was not confined to astronomy or engineering but also shaped just price, profit, coinage, and royal manipulation under Charles V.

9. Dealers rule

Summary: The ninth chapter makes the Hansa an economic power built from towns, ships, offices, grain, fish, and discipline. Cogs police the Oresund in 1284 to blockade Norway, Bergen depends on Lubeck and Hansa supplies of grain, peas, beans, malt, and flour, and stockfish from the north becomes the export that pays for survival. Pye describes Lubeck, Bremen, Hamburg, Cologne, Gotland, Novgorod, London, Bruges, Scania, around two hundred towns, Kontors, Hansetag meetings, Lubeck law, Low German speech, brick taste, step gables, tile-stoves, and merchant nobility. Bergen's Bryggen appears through country toughs, language advantage, secrecy, outsider servants, marriage rules, children's toys, poor women, prostitutes, Hermann Luckow's trial, and brutal initiations involving keel-hauling, smoke, barrels, whipping, dogshit, catshit, shaves, and beer. Alv Erlingsson's pirate war, King Magnus's need for Hansa credit, famine, two thousand marks of silver and fish, Vitalienbruder, Rostock, Wismar, Bergen plunder, Bornholm, Gotland, Likendeelers, Bremen's pirate port, Grote Gherd, Portland Bill, the Firth of Forth, the Oresund, and Paul Bencke's seizure of Hans Memling's triptych show trade sliding into war. By the end, Cologne, Dutch Hanseatics, Holland, Zeeland, Olaf Nielsson's murder in 1445, Amsterdam's 1484 complaint, Dutch credit, and double-entry bookkeeping make the Hansa look powerful but increasingly out of fashion. Source anchors: Oresund 1284, Bergen stockfish, Bryggen, Hansetag, Vitalienbruder, Paul Bencke.

Analysis: Oresund 1284, Bergen stockfish, and King Magnus's credit needs show Polycentric Order acting like a state without a sovereign centre: towns, Kontors, Hansetag meetings, merchants, and fleets coordinate blockades, finance rulers, and enforce routes. Bryggen initiations, Low German, and marriage rules discipline members where no royal bureaucracy can. The Vitalienbruder and Paul Bencke expose dependence on violence, while Dutch credit and double-entry bookkeeping eventually outcompete the older network.

10. Love and capital

Summary: The tenth chapter begins with Katelijne Vedelaer shouting as Lievin van Aerlebecke, his brother, his servant, and Lizebette van Dudzele abduct her from the Bruges beguinage toward Harelbecke and Roeselaere in 1345. The Count of Flanders's bailiff, the sheriff, Bruges aldermen, the mayor, sealed records, banishment for one hundred years and a day, fines of fifty pounds, hanging, and burial alive show how seriously the law protected her refusal. Pye then explains beguine life through Bruges, Strasbourg, Cologne, Flanders, Brabant, Paris, Zeeland, Holland, Frisia, England, Hadewijch of Brabant, Philippe de Navarre's hostility to women's literacy, Henry of Ghent's restrictions, Marguerite Porete's execution in 1310, and church inspectors in 1328. Beguines wash wool, clean cloth, garden, eat coarse bread and pottage, teach girls Latin and theology, nurse lepers, handle the dead, manage households as Dame Agnes does in Le Menagier de Paris, trade cloth like Mergriete van Ecke in 1306, lend two hundred pounds to Calais at 10 percent, and endure mockery at Huy, in Middle Dutch farces, and in disputes over the word "beguine." The chapter then follows Ida of Louvain, St Begga, Elizabeth of Spalbeek, Christina Mirabilis, Marie d'Oignies, Jacques de Vitry, John Rykener in Cheapside in 1394, Bruges bathhouses, Pero Tafur in 1435, Southwark stews, Flemish women in London, straw-ring promises, Matilda of East Grinstead in 1276, contraception, Hildegard of Bingen's Physica, Albertus Magnus, Thomas Sanchez, late marriage, journeymen, windmills, renten, Edam, and East Anglian pensions. Source anchors: Katelijne Vedelaer, Bruges beguinage, Hadewijch, Mergriete van Ecke, John Rykener, Hildegard Physica.

Analysis: Katelijne Vedelaer, Lievin van Aerlebecke, and the Bruges beguinage sealed record give Pye a legal case where consent, property, and female community collide in a named city. Hadewijch, Marguerite Porete, Mergriete van Ecke, and Dame Agnes prevent the beguines from becoming only victims: they teach, lend, trade, nurse, pray, and manage urban households within the same commercial order that wants to marry them off. John Rykener, Bruges bathhouses, Hildegard Physica, and Albertus Magnus broaden the chapter from beguines to sex, work, contraception, and late marriage, so love becomes an economic force that sends journeymen, windmill knowledge, renten, and pension habits across the North Sea world.

11. The plague laws

Summary: The eleventh chapter begins with the Black Death's fever, ulcers, vomiting, diarrhoea, choked lungs, swellings, and Llywelyn Fychan's "brittle coal fragments" for the plague marks that killed his four daughters. Pye uses the Brut chronicle, London from Michaelmas to August, Worcester and Cambridgeshire manorial records, one person in three dead across Northern Europe, villages where four out of five died, flagellant processions, the Tres Riches Heures, Sorbonne doctors advising Philippe VI to flee "vite, loin, longtemps," Paris butchers moved to the Tuileries in 1415, yersinia pestis, Marseilles soap, arsenic, and theriac to show fear without effective knowledge. Scandinavian plague legends with black mist, blue vapour, children, old women, brooms, rakes, a three-footed goat named Hel, dead ships, Dracula at Whitby, Matthias van Neuenburg's well-poisoning libel, rats in York and Birka around 810, London overcrowding, curfews, Geoffrey de Meaux, Saturn, Mars, Jupiter, Conrad of Megenberg, Louis Sanctus, and opium-heavy theriac all turn uncertainty into stories and remedies. The social consequences run through the 1315 famine, grain prices six times normal, Northampton prisoners, John Gower's complaints, Henry Knighton, the French labour law of 1354, the 1377 Parliament petition, Mile End in 1381, the Statute of Labourers, illegal wages at Knightsbridge, sumptuary laws, 1388 travel controls, Swedish and Danish work laws after 1350, Antwerp outdoor relief, St Elizabeth's Hospital, Johann Bockel in Hamburg, Bridewell in 1552, the Spinhuis, Edinburgh's 1498 rules, Gdansk ships in 1564, Holehaven quarantine in Pepys's 1663 diary, John Crabbe, the Royal Charles, the Medway, Geneva certificates, Sir George Courthop, Ann Fanshawe, Fynes Moryson, and John Evelyn. Source anchors: Llywelyn Fychan, vite loin longtemps, yersinia pestis, Statute of Labourers, Edinburgh 1498, Holehaven.

Analysis: Llywelyn Fychan, the Brut, and Worcester and Cambridgeshire death records keep the plague laws grounded in bodies. "Vite, loin, longtemps," theriac, and missed rat ecology show Crisis Governance operating with partial knowledge: authorities know movement, ships, clothes, and proximity matter without understanding fleas or bacteria. 12. The city and the world

Summary: The final chapter begins with Leo of Rozmital leaving Pilsen in 1465 with forty companions, a jester, a lute-player, and a mission involving his Catholic sister and a Hussite marriage, then moving through Neuss nuns, Brussels, Burgundy, and England. Source anchors: Leo of Rozmital, Philip the Good, Hans Memling, Antwerp bourse, Simon Stevin, De Thiende.

Analysis: Leo of Rozmital, Philip the Good, Hans Memling, and the Order of the Golden Fleece let Pye show Burgundy turning trade wealth into political theatre: paintings, processions, jewels, singers, and staged entries do the work that a fragile territorial state cannot do by force alone.
