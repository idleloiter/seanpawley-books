Three Famous Short Novels: Spotted Horses Old Man The Bear (Vintage)

Buy on Amazon — Three Famous Short Novels: Spotted Horses Old Man The Bear (Vintage)

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer.

Core concepts

  • Moral Economy: "Spotted Horses" turns a comic auction into a dispute over household survival, trust, and whether law can recognize Mrs Armstid's five dollars as children's shoes rather than sport.
  • Legibility: horse ownership, prison status, discharge papers, and McCaslin ledgers show institutions making people and property readable in forms that can hide violence.
  • Ecological Constraint: the 1927 flood and the vanishing hunting wilderness force characters to confront water, land, animals, and bodily limits that legal categories cannot master.
  • Coercive Labor: "The Bear" makes Ike's inheritance crisis depend on ledger evidence that landholding and family descent rest on slavery, sale, incest, and constrained Black lives.
  • Knowledge Preservation: the Publisher's Note and Editors' Note preserve the three texts through copy-texts, variant histories, Library of America correction, and scholarly apparatus.

Detailed overview

This Vintage International volume gathers "Spotted Horses," "Old Man," and "The Bear" as corrected texts from Library of America editions prepared by Noel Polk, with supporting notes by Joseph Blotner. The publisher's note locates the three pieces in The Hamlet, If I Forget Thee, Jerusalem or The Wild Palms, and Go Down, Moses, rather than presenting them as freestanding curiosities. The edition therefore asks the reader to hold together three different Faulkner machines: Snopes comedy and rural law, flood adventure and prison logic, and the McCaslin hunting tale that becomes a reckoning with land, slavery, inheritance, and the vanishing wilderness.

"Spotted Horses" is the most openly comic of the three, but its comedy keeps turning into exposure. Flem Snopes returns to Frenchman's Bend with Buck Hipps, a wagon, mules, gingersnaps, pearl-handled pistols, and a string of violent Texas ponies that nobody can catch. Ratliff understands the danger before most of the Bend men do, Mrs Littlejohn watches with practical rage from washpot and kitchen, and Mrs Armstid sees most clearly that Henry Armstid's five dollars represent children's shoes, not sport. The horse auction becomes a Snopes transaction because no one can prove ownership, responsibility, or fraud in a form the law will accept.

"Old Man" moves from Yoknapatawpha comedy into the Mississippi flood of 1927. A tall convict imprisoned for an attempted train robbery learned from pulp magazines and a plump convict sentenced for a tangled episode involving a woman, a stolen automobile, the Mann Act, and a killing are sent from the State Farm into flood work after the levee breaks at Mound's Landing. The tall convict is separated with a pregnant woman, rescues her, fights through skiffs, bayous, a Cajan household, alligator hides, evacuation by motor launch, New Orleans refugee space, and a return upriver, all while trying to bring back the state-owned boat. The story's joke is severe: prison has made captivity orderly enough that freedom, work, money, women, and water all appear as threats to the convict's one desired destination, the penitentiary.

"The Bear" is the longest and most layered section. Isaac McCaslin's education begins with Sam Fathers, Major de Spain's camp, General Compson, Boon Hogganbeck, Tennie's Jim, Ash, Lion, and Old Ben; then it moves through the killing of the bear and Lion, Sam's death, the ledgers in the McCaslin commissary, and Ike's refusal to inherit the plantation. The fourth movement is not a pause in the hunt but the buried center of the story: the records of Carothers McCaslin, Buck and Buddy, Tomey's Turl, Tennie Beauchamp, Eunice, Tomasina, Lucas Beauchamp, and the money entries show that the land is tied to slavery, incest, sale, debt, and false possession.

The three fiction sections share an obsession with ownership that fails under pressure. Flem and Hipps can profit from horses without being accountable for them; the State Farm can call a convict dead, free, escaped, or punishable depending on paperwork; Carothers McCaslin's descendants can inherit land only because earlier men converted dispossession and slavery into ledger entries. The human bodies in the stories pay for those legal fictions: Mrs Armstid's labor, Tull's injuries, the pregnant woman's evacuation, the convict's added sentence, Lion's torn belly, Sam Fathers' final collapse, and Ike's renunciation all test what a document can and cannot make true.

Section-by-section notes

Publisher's Note

Summary: The publisher's note identifies this edition as a corrected Vintage International text built from Library of America versions edited by Noel Polk. It names "Spotted Horses" from The Hamlet (1990), "Old Man" from If I Forget Thee, Jerusalem or The Wild Palms (1990), and "The Bear" from Go Down, Moses (1994). It gives the copy-text as the ribbon typescript setting copy at the Alderman Library of the University of Virginia and says that the editors' note follows the text. It also states that Joseph Blotner prepared the line and page notes. Source anchors: Noel Polk; Library of America; The Hamlet; If I Forget Thee; Go Down, Moses; Alderman Library; Joseph Blotner.

Analysis: Noel Polk, Library of America, and the Alderman Library matter because the volume is not just recycling three famous Faulkner pieces; it is presenting them through knowledge preservation as a particular textual history. The Hamlet, If I Forget Thee, and Go Down, Moses remind the reader that each short novel has a larger home and carries structural pressure from that home into this collection. Joseph Blotner's notes give the book a scholarly apparatus, so the edition frames Faulkner's punctuation, spelling, and copy-text as part of the reading rather than as cleanup.

Spotted Horses

Summary: "Spotted Horses" starts when Flem Snopes and Buck Hipps arrive at Varner's store in Frenchman's Bend with a covered wagon, mules, and a string of gaudy, barbed-wire-tethered Texas ponies that are "wild as deer" and "deadly as rattlesnakes." Quick, Freeman, Jody Varner, Eck Snopes, Eck's boy Wallstreet, Mrs Littlejohn, Ratliff, and the Bend men watch as Hipps eats gingersnaps, displays a pearl-handled pistol, takes blows from the ponies, and sets up an auction. Ratliff warns the men that a Snopes transaction is operating, but Eck is given one horse, Henry Armstid spends the five dollars his wife earned weaving for children's shoes, Mrs Armstid curses the sale, and the buyers cannot catch what they have bought after the lot gate is left open. The aftermath moves to Whiteleaf, where Armstid sues Snopes, Mrs Tull sues Eck over Tull's injuries, Lump Snopes swears that Flem returned Henry's money to Hipps, the Justice of the Peace finds that oral ownership cannot make Eck liable, and the final county-court trial sends another Snopes to Parchman while he calls for Flem. Source anchors: Flem Snopes; Buck Hipps; Frenchman's Bend; gingersnaps; Wallstreet; Mrs Armstid; Whiteleaf; Parchman.

Analysis: Flem Snopes and Buck Hipps profit because Frenchman's Bend wants spectacle more than proof, and the gingersnaps, pistol, and ponies keep everyone looking at Hipps while Flem stays legally evasive. Mrs Armstid and Wallstreet are crucial counterweights: her five dollars makes the auction's moral economy concrete, while the boy's near trampling turns the joke into bodily danger. Whiteleaf and Parchman show the official end of the transaction, where legal legibility can parse ownership but cannot repair the harm and where Flem's name remains the power behind a punishment that lands elsewhere.

Old Man

Summary: "Old Man" begins at the Mississippi State Farm in May of the 1927 flood year with two convicts: a tall attempted train robber who blames Diamond Dick, Jesse James, the Detectives' Gazette, and third-class mail for misleading him, and a plump convict serving one hundred and ninety-nine years after a case involving a woman, a stolen automobile, the Mann Act, a filling-station robbery, and a dead attendant. The plump convict reads Memphis newspaper reports about Cairo, Memphis, sandboils, Mound's Landing, Governor's orders, National Guard troops, Red Cross trains, and Herbert Hoover until the levee breaks and trucks carry shackled men into the flood. The tall convict is swept away with a pregnant woman, tries to return her and the skiff, fights the Old Man, survives bayous and backwater, shelters with a Cajan man and woman, kills alligators for hides with a knife and club, is evacuated before dynamite blows a levee, escapes a New Orleans armory, and finally returns the boat and woman to an officer. The Governor's office then rules that he had been officially discharged as dead, but because he was absent for weeks and returned with state property, the machinery of prison adds ten years to his sentence. Source anchors: 1927 flood; State Farm; Diamond Dick; Mound's Landing; pregnant woman; Cajan; alligator hides; ten years.

Analysis: The State Farm and Diamond Dick explain the tall convict's mind before the flood: he thinks in systems of instruction, debt, and false promises, which is why the Old Man's water feels less like liberation than like bad information made physical. Mound's Landing, the pregnant woman, the Cajan, and the alligator hides give him a temporary life where work and money become possible outside prison, but ecological constraint makes that life unstable and unreadable to him. The ten years at the end makes the state resemble the flood itself, a force whose paperwork and legibility can rename death, freedom, custody, and obedience after the fact.

The Bear

Summary: "The Bear" follows Isaac McCaslin from childhood trips to Major de Spain's hunting camp into a long education under Sam Fathers, the son of a Chickasaw chief and a black slave woman, who teaches him how to read woods, tracks, fear, and restraint. In the early sections Ike leaves his gun, watch, and compass to see Old Ben; later he learns the crooked footprint, kills his buck, sees Lion captured and starved into obedience by Sam, watches Boon Hogganbeck bond with the blue dog, and joins the annual pursuit with General Compson, Walter Ewell, Tennie's Jim, Ash, Major de Spain, and McCaslin Edmonds. The hunt reaches its violent end when Lion locks onto Old Ben's throat, Boon kills the bear with a knife, Lion is gutted, Sam collapses without visible wound, Doctor Crawford misreads Sam's condition, Lion dies at sundown, and Boon guards Sam's burial platform beside Lion's grave. At twenty-one, Ike and McCaslin debate the plantation ledgers in the commissary, including Carothers McCaslin, Buck and Buddy, Tomey's Turl, Tennie Beauchamp, Eunice, Tomasina, Lucas Beauchamp, Ikkemotubbe, and the supposed sale and inheritance of land; the final return to the woods shows the lumber company's concrete markers, Old Ben's paw in an axle-grease tin, Sam's grave gifts, a rattlesnake addressed as "Chief" and "Grandfather," and Boon under a gum tree claiming every squirrel as his. Source anchors: Isaac McCaslin; Sam Fathers; Old Ben; Lion; Boon Hogganbeck; commissary ledgers; Carothers McCaslin; gum tree.

Analysis: Isaac McCaslin, Sam Fathers, Old Ben, and Lion make the hunt a school in humility before the story ever reaches the commissary ledgers. Boon Hogganbeck's knife kill matters because it ends the chase physically while also killing the order that Sam represented; Lion dies, Sam "quits," and Major de Spain breaks camp because the old arrangement cannot continue after its central animal is gone. Carothers McCaslin, Ikkemotubbe, the ledgers, and the gum tree bind the wilderness plot to inheritance: Ike's refusal grows from written proof that ownership rests on coercive labor, slavery, and incest, while Boon's final possessiveness over squirrels shows a degraded remnant of the old hunting world after ownership has returned in a smaller, crazier form.

Editors' Note

Summary: The editors' note explains the textual histories of all three short novels and the editorial principles used in this volume. It says "Spotted Horses" came from The Hamlet, which began as Snopes sketches in the mid-1920s, had versions titled "As I Lay Dying," "Aria con Amore," and "The Peasants," and was published by Random House on April 1, 1940, with copy-text from the Alderman Library ribbon typescript and supporting manuscripts, galleys, and Mississippi Department of Archives and History materials. It says "Old Man" was written in alternating stints with the "Wild Palms" sections, not created by mechanically interweaving two finished stories. It traces "The Bear" from the 1935 story "Lion," through Quentin Compson, Absalom, Absalom!, Go Down, Moses, the Saturday Evening Post reduction of May 4, 1942, Noel Polk's text, Joseph Blotner's notes, Merriam-Webster, and reference works by Blotner and Calvin S. Brown. Source anchors: mid-1920s; April 1, 1940; Alderman Library; Wild Palms; Lion; Saturday Evening Post; Noel Polk; Calvin S. Brown.

Analysis: The mid-1920s and April 1, 1940 place "Spotted Horses" inside Faulkner's long Snopes development, which helps explain why Flem's brief presence feels larger than the auction scene. The Wild Palms material is important because "Old Man" belongs to an alternating design even when this volume extracts it; the flood story still carries contrast with the omitted narrative. "Lion," the Saturday Evening Post, Noel Polk, and Calvin S. Brown show knowledge preservation as editorial practice: "The Bear" moved across drafts, titles, markets, and scholarly correction, so the edition's respect for variant spelling and punctuation is tied to Faulkner's compositional record.

William Faulkner (1897-1962)

Summary: The biographical section gives Faulkner's birth as William Cuthbert Faulkner in 1897 in New Albany, Mississippi, to Murry and Maud Butler Falkner, and notes the family's 1904 move to Oxford. It connects him to the Old Colonel, a Civil War veteran, railroad builder, legislator, author of The White Rose of Memphis, and possible duel victim, then follows Faulkner through leaving school, working in his grandfather's bank, the blocked Estelle Oldham marriage, failed U.S. Army enlistment, Canadian RAF training, and University of Mississippi poetry. It names Sherwood Anderson, New Orleans, Soldier's Pay, Mosquitoes, Flags in the Dust, Sartoris, The Sound and the Fury, Sanctuary, the power-plant writing of As I Lay Dying, marriage to Estelle in 1929, Alabama's death in 1931, Jill's birth in 1933, MGM and Warner Brothers screenwriting, Malcolm Cowley's Portable Faulkner, the 1949 Nobel Prize, the National Book Award, the Gold Medal, France's Legion of Honor, and Faulkner's death in Oxford on July 6, 1962. Source anchors: New Albany; Oxford; Old Colonel; Estelle Oldham; RAF; Sherwood Anderson; Portable Faulkner; July 6, 1962.

Analysis: New Albany, Oxford, and the Old Colonel matter because this edition's three short novels repeatedly return to invented Mississippi inheritances that echo biographical materials without becoming simple autobiography. Estelle Oldham, the RAF episode, Sherwood Anderson, and the power plant locate Faulkner's writing career among failed roles, borrowed performances, and practical labor, which helps explain the book's interest in work, myth, and false authority. The Portable Faulkner, Nobel Prize, and July 6, 1962 give the volume its retrospective posture: these texts are presented after the canon has already been consolidated around Faulkner's Mississippi fiction.

Also by William Faulkner

Summary: The "Also by William Faulkner" section lists and describes related books, including Absalom, Absalom!, As I Lay Dying, A Fable, Flags in the Dust, Light in August, The Reivers, Requiem for a Nun, The Sound and the Fury, and The Unvanquished. It identifies the Snopes trilogy as The Hamlet, The Town, and The Mansion, then adds Big Woods, Collected Stories, Go Down, Moses, Intruder in the Dust, Knight's Gambit, Pylon, Sanctuary, Three Famous Short Novels, Uncollected Stories of William Faulkner, and The Wild Palms. The descriptions link this volume's contents to Thomas Sutpen, the Bundrens, Flem Snopes, Lucas Beauchamp, Gavin Stevens, Temple Drake, Nancy, Quentin Compson, and the hunting stories later gathered in Big Woods. Source anchors: Absalom; Bundren; Snopes Trilogy; Big Woods; Go Down, Moses; Lucas Beauchamp; Temple Drake; Wild Palms.

Analysis: The Snopes Trilogy and Big Woods entries are directly relevant because "Spotted Horses" and "The Bear" are extracted from those larger clusters of Yoknapatawpha fiction. Go Down, Moses, Lucas Beauchamp, and Wild Palms help position the collection's race, land, and flood materials inside Faulkner's wider set of recurring families and places. Temple Drake, Nancy, Quentin Compson, and the Bundrens show how the marketing copy builds a network around famous names, but the section's value for this note is bibliographic rather than interpretive.

Academic Resources for Educators

Summary: The academic resources section is publisher back matter aimed at teachers and course adopters rather than part of the three short novels. It names Vintage Books and Anchor Books as American publishers of William Faulkner, Albert Camus, Willa Cather, James Joyce, Thomas Mann, Ralph Ellison, Franz Kafka, Toni Morrison, Michel Foucault, Simone de Beauvoir, Edward Said, and James Baldwin. It points readers to a Random House Academic Resources website with more than 5,000 academic titles, desk and examination copy requests, teacher's guides, subject catalogs, conference schedules, e-newsletters, and Vintage/Anchor Academic Marketing at 1745 Broadway in New York.
