The Last Kings of Shanghai

Buy on Amazon — The Last Kings of Shanghai

Detailed overview

Jonathan Kaufman frames the book through his own returns to Shanghai: the Peace Hotel lobby in 1979, the black-framed bicycles and Mao suits of post-Cultural Revolution China, the French-speaking bellhop, the 1989 visit to the Kadoorie mansion turned Children's Palace, and the 2002 encounter at the Sassoon synagogue museum with a Chinese caretaker who remembered the Cathay Hotel and the Sabbath stove-lighting work he had done for Jews. Those scenes make the book a recovery project. Kaufman is not simply telling the rise of two Jewish business dynasties; he is reopening a past that Communist China had compressed into the slogan Wu Wang Guo Chi, "Never Forget National Humiliation," and asking what happens when the same city later wants the glamour, capital, hotels, and cosmopolitan memory that it had condemned.

The first family line begins in Baghdad, where David Sassoon is not a poor immigrant but the intended Nasi, the "Prince of the Jews," heir to a community rooted in the Babylonian Captivity and to a commercial world of Hebrew, Arabic, Turkish, Persian, wool, spices, pearls, and Ottoman finance. His 1829 flight after imprisonment by Baghdad's Turkish rulers turns a lost birthright into a Bombay-based restoration. Kaufman follows David as he learns British law, steamships, dock space, cotton gins, Bank of Bombay finance, and standardized English correspondence while still sending his sons through a Baghdadi-Jewish family system disciplined by religion, salaries, rotation, letters, and obedience to the patriarch.

Shanghai enters through coercion and commerce together. The daotai tries to quarantine British traders in a swamp outside the walled city after the First Opium War and the 1843 opening of treaty ports, but Elias Sassoon turns that marginal land into the Sassoons' China base. The book is explicit about opium as both business engine and moral crime: the Second Opium War, Jardine Matheson, Malwa opium, Chaozhou distributors, telegraph pricing, steamship logistics, the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank, Royal Opium Commission testimony, and the eventual calculation of billions in opium-derived profit all sit behind later Sassoon real estate, factories, and hotels. Kaufman does not let the reader admire the Bund skyline without seeing the drug trade beneath it.

The Kadoorie line is built as a contrast. Elly Kadoorie arrives from Baghdad as a teenage employee in the Sassoon system, quits after the Weihaiwei disinfectant dispute, uses the alias E. S. Kelly on the Hong Kong Hotel veranda, befriends Robert Hotung, invests in electricity, hotels, the Peak tram, rubber, and land, and marries Laura Mocatta, whose London Jewish connections and philanthropy widen his reach. Laura's girls' schools in Baghdad and Shanghai, her driving on Bubbling Well Road, her Shanghai Red Cross work, and her death in the house fire while trying to save a governess become part of the Kadoorie claim that their money could be more civic-minded than the older Sassoon fortune.

The middle of the book makes interwar Shanghai a city where foreign privilege, Chinese ambition, and revolutionary politics develop in the same streets. The International Settlement and its Shanghai Municipal Council provide low taxes, police, tramways, gas, rickshaw regulation, and business confidence; the same relative freedom also gives shelter to Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, Chinese newspapers, labor organizers, and the Communist Party's first meeting. Elly's Marble Hall, the Majestic Hotel, Chiang Kai-shek's wedding, Victor Sassoon's Cathay Hotel, Emily Hahn's warnings, Yang Mengliang spying from inside the Cathay, and the Shanghai Banking Coup all show why foreign capitalists could become fabulously rich while misreading the Chinese politics that would eventually dispossess them.

World War II gives the book its clearest moral test. Victor Sassoon, Elly Kadoorie, Horace Kadoorie, Ho Feng-Shan, Erich Reisman, Laura Margolis, Captain Koreshige Inuzuka, the Joint Distribution Committee, the Conte Biancamano, Hongkew, the Kadoorie School, and 18,000 Jewish refugees converge in a city where no visa is required and Japanese anti-Semitic fantasy produces the strange "fugu" plan to use Jews rather than exterminate them. Victor bargains and stalls with Inuzuka; Horace builds a refugee school and youth association; Margolis feeds refugees under occupation; the Japanese seize the Cathay, Marble Hall, Stanley Camp, and Chapei, while Elly dies above the stables of the house that had once advertised his arrival.

After 1945, Kaufman follows the families' divergent answers to defeat. Victor sells badly, leaves on a Pan Am return ticket he never uses, loses the Cathay, Hamilton House, Embankment House, and his Shanghai fortune to Communist taxes and pressure on Lucien Ovadia, then builds a diminished life in Nassau, Dallas, and London. Lawrence and Horace shift the Kadoorie center to Hong Kong, rebuild China Light and Power, bring the Rongs and textile mills from Shanghai, electrify Kowloon and the New Territories, found the Kadoorie Agricultural Aid Association, withstand the 1967 riots by continuing the St. George's Building, and accept the need to remain useful to China as the 1997 handover approaches.

The final chapters return to memory and political compromise. Lawrence works with Deng Xiaoping, Margaret Thatcher, Sir Murray MacLehose, Li Peng, Daya Bay, the House of Lords, and "one country, two systems," yet refuses to condemn Tiananmen and opposes Christopher Patten's democratic reforms because his companies depend on Beijing's confidence. Michael Kadoorie later brings the Peninsula back to the Bund, partners with the Rong family, meets Jiang Zemin and Xi Jinping, and sees his family's history recast as friendship with China. The restored Cathay/Peace Hotel, Sir Elly's Bar, Hongkew's refugee museum, Ho Feng-Shan displays, missing Chiune Sugihara exhibit, and Lou Jiwei's "bygones" line make the close of the book about who gets to curate Shanghai's usable past.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Introduction

Summary: Kaufman begins in 1979 at the Peace Hotel on the Bund, when China had only recently established diplomatic relations with the United States, Deng Xiaoping had replaced Mao-era isolation with cautious opening, and official conversations still repeated denunciations of the Gang of Four. He returns in 1989 after the Tiananmen Square massacre to the Kadoorie mansion turned Children's Palace, then in 2002 to a Sassoon-built synagogue museum where a Chinese caretaker remembers the Cathay Hotel, Jewish Sabbath practices, and hatred of the Sassoons as British imperialists rather than as Jews. The introduction also introduces the woman with the mahogany bed and chest left by wartime Jewish tenants, the Communist slogan Wu Wang Guo Chi, the International Settlement, Victor Sassoon's Cathay Hotel, Sir Lawrence Kadoorie's Peninsula Hotel, Hongkew's 18,000 refugees, and the later Fairmont restoration of the Peace Hotel. Kaufman sets Shanghai itself as the central character: a city of opium, finance, publishing houses, film studios, Mao's first Communist meeting, Chiang Kai-shek's currency stabilization, Jewish refugee survival, and post-1978 capitalist revival. Source anchors: Peace Hotel, Gang of Four, Children's Palace, Cathay Hotel, Wu Wang Guo Chi, International Settlement, Hongkew, Fairmont restoration.

Analysis: The Peace Hotel, Children's Palace, Cathay Hotel, and synagogue museum let Kaufman build the book from physical remains rather than family legend alone. The Gang of Four scripts and Wu Wang Guo Chi explain why the Sassoons and Kadoories had been treated as proof of humiliation, while the Fairmont restoration and saved hotel artifacts show China turning the same foreign-built Shanghai into a source of prestige and tourist value. The mahogany furniture, Hongkew refugees, and International Settlement make Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation the introduction's mechanism: private objects and hotel fragments preserve a past that state history first condemned and later repackaged.

1. The Patriarch

Summary: Chapter 1 follows David Sassoon's 1829 escape from Baghdad after Turkish rulers imprison him and threaten hanging unless his family pays an exorbitant tax bill, forcing him to flee with pearls sewn into his cloak and a money belt around his waist. Kaufman reconstructs Baghdad's Jewish world from Nebuchadnezzar's Babylonian Captivity, the Psalm by the rivers of Babylon, the office of Nasi or Prince of the Jews, Ottoman finance, coffeehouses, bazaars, wool, pearls, and the Sassoon family's position as advisers and merchants. David moves through Bushire, where he sleeps in a warehouse with a gun against rats, then to Bombay, where British protection, Sir Robert Grant, Lord Palmerston's trade policy, the end of the East India Company monopoly, docks, steamships, cotton gins, the Bank of Bombay, and English accounting transform him into a British-aligned merchant prince. The chapter also shows David preserving Baghdadi identity through Judeo-Arabic, Hebrew-English checks, orthodox family discipline, and the training of sons such as Abdullah and Elias while building a business that could survive exile. Source anchors: Baghdad, Nasi, Babylonian Captivity, Bushire, Bombay, East India Company, Bank of Bombay, Judeo-Arabic.

Analysis: David's flight from Baghdad makes the Sassoon empire a restoration project rather than a simple immigrant success story. The Nasi title, Babylonian Captivity, Ottoman finance, and Bushire warehouse explain why he measures himself against lost authority, while Bombay, Sir Robert Grant, the East India Company, and the Bank of Bombay give him the legal and financial tools that Baghdad could no longer provide. Judeo-Arabic correspondence and English accounting show the mechanism Kaufman will track throughout the book: the Sassoons keep family cohesion through Baghdadi Jewish culture while using British imperial infrastructure to move money, cotton, opium, and sons across Asia.

2. Empire of the Sons-and Opium

Summary: Chapter 2 shifts to 1843 Shanghai, where the daotai receives the first British delegation after the First Opium War and tries to push the foreigners from the walled city into swampy river land outside the walls. Elias Sassoon arrives seven years later with his Baghdad cloak, eyeglasses, account books, money bags, assistants trained in Sassoon schools, and orders from David to build the China business through Shanghai, Canton, Hong Kong, Japan, and the Bund. Kaufman then lays out the opium system: Jardine Matheson, clipper ships, Jardine's Lookout on the Peak, Malwa opium, the Second Opium War, the burning of the Summer Palace, legal sale of opium, Chaozhou distributors, telegraph intelligence, steamships, and loans through the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank. The chapter follows the Sassoon takeover from Jardine, their control of 70 percent of the opium entering China by the 1870s, Sir Albert Abdullah Sassoon's political elevation, Anti-Opium League pressure, Royal Opium Commission testimony, Prince of Wales social ties, anti-Semitic British reactions, and the eventual 1909-1912 international prohibition that makes opium a criminal business. Source anchors: daotai, Elias Sassoon, Jardine Matheson, Second Opium War, Chaozhou, telegraph, Royal Opium Commission, Sir Albert Sassoon.

Analysis: The daotai's swamp and Elias Sassoon's warehouses show how foreign settlement begins as a containment strategy and becomes the commercial core of Shanghai. Jardine Matheson, Jardine's Lookout, Malwa opium, the Second Opium War, Chaozhou distributors, and telegraph codes make opium a Commodity Systems anchor: the Sassoons connect Indian supply, Chinese retail networks, faster information, finance, and family offices into an end-to-end chain. The Royal Opium Commission, Sir Albert Sassoon, the Prince of Wales, and anti-opium laws make the family's respectability inseparable from political access, because the same money that buys country houses and royal dinners comes from a trade Kaufman repeatedly identifies as devastating for Chinese society.

3. Laura and Elly

Summary: Chapter 3 introduces Elly Kadoorie as the youngest son of Rima and Salih Kadoorie, sent from Baghdad after his father's death to Sassoon schools and then to Hong Kong in 1880 as a fifteen-year-old apprentice earning thirty-seven rupees a month. Elly quits after the Weihaiwei bubonic plague dispute over a barrel of disinfectant, borrows 500 Hong Kong dollars from his brother Moses, uses the alias E. S. Kelly on the Hong Kong Hotel veranda, forms Benjamin, Kelly & Potts, invests in the Hong Kong Hotel, befriends Robert Hotung, and buys into electricity, hotels, and the Peak tram. His marriage to Laura Mocatta brings him into the London Jewish cousinhood, Frederick Mocatta's philanthropic circle, the Mocatta motto "Adhere and Prosper," and a partnership in which Laura supports girls' schools in Baghdad and Shanghai, Chinese orphanages, the Shanghai Red Cross, women's education, and suffragette-era public life. The chapter follows Elly through the rubber bubble, Thomas Jackson at the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank, more than 300 Malaysian rubber companies, the 1911 revolution, Sun Yat-sen, Liang Qichao, Robert Hotung's nationalism, Elly's failed British citizenship applications, World War I passport anxiety, Laura's driving and pistol shooting, and her death in a Bubbling Well Road fire after searching for the governess. Source anchors: Weihaiwei, barrel of disinfectant, E. S. Kelly, Robert Hotung, Laura Mocatta, rubber bubble, Sun Yat-sen, Bubbling Well Road.

Analysis: Elly's disinfectant dispute, E. S. Kelly alias, Robert Hotung friendship, and Hong Kong Hotel veranda distinguish the Kadoories from the Sassoons by making them improvisers rather than patriarchal heirs. Laura Mocatta, the girls' schools, Shanghai Red Cross, and Bubbling Well Road show why Kadoorie legitimacy depends on charity, women, and Chinese-facing civic work as well as returns on electricity and rubber. The rubber bubble, Thomas Jackson, Sun Yat-sen, and World War I passport trouble also define Elly's political position: he profits from British structures but remains insecure inside them, which makes Chinese partners such as Hotung more important to his future than British club acceptance.

4. Shanghai Rising

Summary: Chapter 4 opens after the Republic of China falters, Yuan Shikai declares himself emperor, warlords divide the country, and Shanghai rises because the International Settlement and Shanghai Municipal Council provide stable roads, taxes, police, trams, rickshaw rules, gas, and low-regulation business conditions. Kaufman describes 40,000 foreigners, 1 million Chinese inside the settlement, 2 million Chinese elsewhere in the city, Bubbling Well Road villas, the "Republic of Merchants," Mao Zedong and Zhou Enlai using the International Settlement and French Concession, more than 1,000 Chinese periodicals, Joseph Stilwell comparing the skyline to Philadelphia, and Lawrence Kadoorie remembering the city as the "Paris of the Orient." Elly returns from London after Laura's death, orders Lawrence out of Lincoln's Inn and Horace away from architecture or farming, builds Marble Hall with a 220-foot veranda, 3,600 electric lightbulbs, twelve bedrooms, forty-two servants, Chinese antiques, Persian carpets, and an Indian temple ceiling. The chapter then uses the Majestic Hotel, Whitey Smith's jazz orchestra, cheongsams, Douglas Fairbanks, Mary Pickford, King Faisal of Iraq, Haile Selassie, Elly's 1926 British citizenship and knighthood, Shanghai Land Co., Mao's first party meeting, Chiang Kai-shek's 1927 massacre of Communists, and Chiang's wedding at the Majestic to show the Kadoories moving into Shanghai's political and social center. Source anchors: Yuan Shikai, Shanghai Municipal Council, Republic of Merchants, Mao Zedong, Marble Hall, Majestic Hotel, Chiang Kai-shek, Shanghai Land Co.

Analysis: Yuan Shikai's failure, the Shanghai Municipal Council, and the Republic of Merchants make Shanghai a Commercial Society paradox: foreign rule protects business, speech, hotels, and utilities better than Chinese warlord politics, but it also humiliates Chinese residents. Marble Hall and the Majestic Hotel turn Elly's outsider anxiety into architecture, because the 220-foot veranda, lightbulbs, jazz, and Chinese access announce that the Kadoories can host a wider Shanghai than British clubs do. Mao Zedong, Zhou Enlai, Chiang Kai-shek, and the 1927 massacre show the danger Kaufman keeps pressing: the same International Settlement that enables Kadoorie wealth and hotel glamour shelters the revolutionary politics that will later destroy the foreign business order.

5. The Impresario

Summary: Chapter 5 centers on Victor Sassoon: monocle, pencil-thin mustache, Cambridge wit, handheld movie camera, photography studio, racing stables, two crutches, and the World War I plane crash that broke both legs and damaged his hip. Kaufman follows Victor from Harrow, Cambridge, "Nunkie," Bombay textile mills, Shanghai visits with Jacob Sassoon, the Royal Naval Air Service, bomber production in Italy, and the decision to take over Sassoon operations when Reginald, Edward, Philip, and the London relatives cannot manage the Asian business. Victor modernizes mills, expands Sassoon-controlled banks, moves money through Hong Kong trusts, debates currency and factory conditions in India's National Legislative Assembly, warns that Gandhi's name "is one to conjure with," and then abandons India for Shanghai after anti-foreign pressures and labor politics threaten the textile fortune. In Shanghai he builds Sassoon House and the Cathay Hotel on the Bund with a copper tower, national suites, Lalique sconces, Bubbling Springs water, Fortune's praise, Nationalist bond profits, Emily Hahn, Zau Sinmay, Yang Mengliang, 5,950 Chinese corpses collected in 1935, Lu Xun's elevator humiliation, Japan's 1932 bombing, Manchukuo, the Shanghai Banking Coup, and the 1937 Japanese assault near the Cathay. Source anchors: Victor Sassoon, Royal Naval Air Service, Gandhi, Sassoon House, Cathay Hotel, Emily Hahn, Shanghai Banking Coup, Manchukuo.

Analysis: Victor's crutches, plane crash, camera, and monocle explain why Kaufman treats him as both performer and strategist: he hides pain through spectacle and uses spectacle to make business contacts. Gandhi, the National Legislative Assembly, and the move from India to Shanghai show Victor choosing a place where British troops, low taxes, and weak Chinese sovereignty seem to protect capital better than India does. Sassoon House, the Cathay Hotel, Emily Hahn, Yang Mengliang, the 5,950 corpses, Lu Xun, Manchukuo, and the Shanghai Banking Coup then expose the limits of his intelligence; he can read currencies and buildings but cannot extend his empathy far enough toward the Chinese poor, leftist hotel workers, or Nationalist state control.

6. "Me Voila Therefore Walking a Tightrope"

Summary: Chapter 6 begins in November 1938 with the Conte Biancamano bringing Jewish refugees from Germany and Austria to Shanghai, including twelve-year-old Erich Reisman, whose mother had been forced to scrub Vienna streets after the Anschluss and whose brother had seen Nazi students throw a Jewish classmate from a fourth-floor school balustrade. Ho Feng-Shan, the Chinese consul in Vienna, issues hundreds and then thousands of Shanghai visas despite Nationalist caution toward Germany, enabling the Reismans to obtain exit permits, travel through Switzerland, Naples, the Suez Canal, Bombay, Singapore, and Hong Kong, and arrive to a German welcome sign declaring them citizens of the world. Kaufman then explains Japan's strange anti-Semitic policy through Jacob Schiff's $200 million in loans for Japan against Russia, the Kishinev pogrom, Takahashi Korekiyo, the Order of the Rising Sun, the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, Captain Koreshige Inuzuka, Manchukuo, the "fugu" idea, and Inuzuka's attempt to turn Victor Sassoon into a new Schiff. Elly asks Victor to stop being a playboy and lead the refugee effort; Victor raises money with Charlie Chaplin, negotiates with Inuzuka, confronts 10,000 then 12,000 then 18,000 refugees in Hongkew, asks the Joint Distribution Committee for $250,000 for a hospital, and leaves for India in 1941 while Horace builds the Shanghai Jewish Youth Association, the Kadoorie School, summer camps, vocational courses, and English classes under Lucie Hartwich before Japan seizes Shanghai on December 8, 1941. Source anchors: Conte Biancamano, Erich Reisman, Ho Feng-Shan, Jacob Schiff, Koreshige Inuzuka, fugu, Kadoorie School, December 8 1941.

Analysis: The Conte Biancamano, Erich Reisman, Ho Feng-Shan, and the Shanghai visas make refugee survival depend on a Chokepoint and Gateway: Shanghai's accidental openness, ship routes, exit permits, and the conscience of a Nationalist diplomat decide who can escape Europe. Jacob Schiff, Takahashi Korekiyo, the Protocols, Inuzuka, and the fugu plan explain why Japanese anti-Semitism does not follow the Nazi script in Shanghai; the Japanese imagine Jews as dangerous leverage, and that delusion gives Victor, Elly, Horace, and Laura Margolis room to keep refugees alive. The Kadoorie School, Hongkew, the Joint Distribution Committee, Lucie Hartwich, and Horace's camps also change Horace's role inside the family, because refugee work lets the overlooked brother do something more consequential than managing Marble Hall or Shanghai Gas.

7. War

Summary: Chapter 7 moves from Japanese troops occupying the Cathay and putting Horace under house arrest to Lawrence Kadoorie crossing Hong Kong harbor on December 8, 1941, after the Pearl Harbor attack, Japanese bombing of Hong Kong's airport, and the assault across the frontier from China. Lawrence has to destroy the Hok Un Power Station turbines, rescue China Light personnel, drive under shellfire to Victoria Peak, find Muriel, Rita, Michael, and the Chinese nanny, and then endure Hong Kong's Christmas Day surrender. The Japanese send the Kadoories and British civilians through Statue Square, past barbed wire and bodies, to Stanley Camp, where more than 2,000 prisoners live on watery rice, boiled lettuce, cigarettes, soup, chores, plays, gardens, and Elly's jokes about his credit still being good. In Shanghai, Laura Margolis of the Joint Distribution Committee negotiates with Captain Inuzuka from Victor's former suite, uses Swiss-bank credit to feed refugees, and refuses the fantasy that she can influence Treasury secretary Henry Morgenthau, while Lawrence's family is later sent by ship to Shanghai, Elly and Horace are locked above Marble Hall's stables, and Lawrence, Muriel, Rita, and Michael are imprisoned in Chapei with weevil rice, rotten vegetables, dog-meat stew, and cold showers. Source anchors: Pearl Harbor, Hok Un Power Station, Victoria Peak, Statue Square, Stanley Camp, Laura Margolis, Chapei, dog-meat stew.

Analysis: Hok Un Power Station, China Light, Victoria Peak, and Stanley Camp move Lawrence from confident Hong Kong modernizer into prisoner and military participant, forcing him to see how quickly colonial security can vanish. Laura Margolis, Inuzuka, Henry Morgenthau, and the Swiss-bank credit show relief work continuing only through improvisation inside occupation bureaucracy, not through the grand social power Victor and Elly once enjoyed. Chapei, Marble Hall's stables, dog-meat stew, and Elly's death turn Coercive Labor and captivity into a family lesson: occupation strips Kadoorie prestige down to bodily vulnerability, which is why Lawrence's postwar caution in Hong Kong cannot be read as merely conservative business prudence.

8. "I Gave Up India and China Gave Me Up"

Summary: Chapter 8 starts after American liberation with General Albert Wedemeyer in Victor's Cathay suite, U.S. soldiers at Marble Hall, Michael Kadoorie riding his tricycle on the 220-foot veranda, Horace hosting Americans and Royal Air Force guests, and Lawrence leaving for Hong Kong by British military plane. The end of extraterritoriality in 1943 means the International Settlement, Shanghai Municipal Council, foreign courts, and British military protection no longer shield the Sassoons and Kadoories, while Horace faces Chinese employees at Shanghai Gas Co., Nationalist corruption, inflation, Communist advances near Shanghai, and Lawrence's advice to cultivate Chinese officials over Baghdad meals at Marble Hall. Victor sends Lucien Ovadia to unload properties, struggles with the ruined Cathay, inflation that moves from twenty Chinese dollars to one U.S. dollar in 1944 to black-market millions by 1947, workers demanding rice or cloth, and a ballroom confrontation with employees while chefs stand nearby with meat cleavers. Horace watches garbage strikes, tram stoppages, Jardine Matheson labor trouble, Elly's tombstone, the six hundred ivories, Victor's Rotary Club warning, his own flight to Hong Kong, Communist artillery near Marble Hall, Nationalist machine guns in the Cathay, the People's Liberation Army entering Shanghai, Ovadia's exit being blocked by taxes and worker claims, Madame Sun Yat-sen's takeover of Marble Hall, and Lawrence concluding that everything in Shanghai is lost. Source anchors: Albert Wedemeyer, extraterritoriality, Shanghai Gas Co., Lucien Ovadia, ballroom confrontation, six hundred ivories, Madame Sun Yat-sen, People's Liberation Army.

Analysis: Albert Wedemeyer, Marble Hall, and the missing International Settlement reveal that liberation is not restoration: the foreign bubble has disappeared, and the Chinese state now owns the rules. Shanghai Gas Co., inflation, Lucien Ovadia, the Cathay workers, and the ballroom confrontation show foreign capital being trapped by currency collapse, labor demands, and Nationalist weakness even before the Communists arrive. Madame Sun Yat-sen, the People's Liberation Army, the blocked exit permits, and the surrender of Marble Hall make State Capacity visible through reversal: foreigners who once lived above Chinese law now negotiate taxes, workers' claims, property seizure, and permission to leave.

9. The Reckoning

Summary: Chapter 9 follows Lawrence from postwar Shanghai to Hong Kong by way of Kunming, a torn pair of trousers, U.S. army fatigues, and a British air force plane carrying new Hong Kong currency to replace Japanese occupation notes. Hong Kong is looted and dark: the Peninsula has been Japanese headquarters, St. George's Building was used by the Kempetai, the population has fallen from 1.25 million to 600,000, and China Light and Power is damaged after war and internment. Lawrence decides that Hong Kong "may become another Shanghai" but only if he changes the family's methods, living more modestly in Kowloon, helping British officials inventory lumber, wiring, bathtubs, doorknobs, fire stations, police headquarters, an immigration office, an abattoir, a mental hospital, ferry service, a business school, and school furniture. He also sends money with Alex Maisel for Australian investments, vows never to keep more than half the family's assets in China or Hong Kong, partners with the Rong family and Shanghai textile manufacturers, diverts a turbine from South Africa, builds Nanyang Cotton Mill, supplies 249 factories by 1949 and 367 more the next year, and later supports Horace's Kadoorie Agricultural Aid Association, Leung Chik's chickens, Farmer Fu, rural pig breeding, Lantau Island electrification, and China Light's expansion. Source anchors: Kunming, Japanese occupation notes, St. George's Building, China Light and Power, Alex Maisel, Rong family, Nanyang Cotton Mill, Kadoorie Agricultural Aid Association.

Analysis: Kunming, the currency plane, St. George's Building, and China Light make Lawrence's return to Hong Kong a financial and civic reconstruction rather than a retreat into luxury. Alex Maisel, Australian investments, and the rule about never keeping too much in China or Hong Kong turn Shanghai's loss into Capital Allocation discipline. The Rong family, Nanyang Cotton Mill, turbines, factory power, Kadoorie Agricultural Aid Association, and Lantau electrification explain how Lawrence converts refugee capital from Shanghai into Hong Kong's industrial base while Horace turns anti-Communism into rural aid, chickens, pigs, and practical improvements for New Territories families.

10. The Last Taipan

Summary: Chapter 10 presents Lawrence Kadoorie as Hong Kong's last great colonial taipan, driven from Kadoorie Avenue to the Star Ferry and St. George's Building while Horace heads to the Kadoorie Agricultural Aid Association or the Peninsula Hotel. By the 1970s the Kadoories control electricity, real estate, factories, trading, finance, hotels, the harbor tunnel, poultry and pork programs, and roughly 10 percent of Hong Kong's economy, while Lawrence insists that Hong Kong survives because it is useful to China. After the Cultural Revolution, Deng Xiaoping rehabilitates Rong Yiren, Madame Sun Yat-sen, and old Shanghai capitalists; Nixon signs the Shanghai Communique in Victor Sassoon's Hamilton House; Lawrence returns to China in 1978, sees Ling Ying, visits Elly and Laura's preserved grave, works with James Callaghan, Margaret Thatcher, Sir Murray MacLehose, Guangzhou officials, and Hua Guofeng, and pushes electricity sales and the Daya Bay nuclear plant. The chapter follows his 1981 peerage as Baron Kadoorie of Kowloon, his advisory role in Hong Kong's future, Deng's rejection of British administration, one country two systems, his House of Lords speech against Britain's passport downgrading of Hong Kong residents, his meeting with Deng about Daya Bay, and his silence or defense of Beijing after the 1989 Tiananmen massacre and during Christopher Patten's democratic reforms. Source anchors: Star Ferry, Kadoorie Agricultural Aid Association, Deng Xiaoping, Hamilton House, Daya Bay, Baron Kadoorie, one country two systems, Tiananmen.

Analysis: The Star Ferry, St. George's Building, China Light, and the Kadoorie Agricultural Aid Association show Lawrence as a colonial operator whose power comes from infrastructure rather than from a hotel ballroom. Deng Xiaoping, Rong Yiren, Hamilton House, Daya Bay, and Baron Kadoorie let Kaufman show Lawrence trying to turn utility contracts into a political bridge between Britain, Hong Kong, and China. One country two systems, the passport fight, Tiananmen, and Patten's reforms expose the Moral Economy cost of that bridge: Lawrence protects Hong Kong's commercial conditions and his own investments by accepting Beijing's political control more readily than many Hong Kong residents do.

11. Back on the Bund

Summary: Chapter 11 begins with Lawrence's final years in a wheelchair, his August 25, 1993 death at ninety-four, Horace's death two years later, and a San Francisco reunion of more than 1,000 Shanghai refugees, including Michael Blumenthal, Lucie Hartwich, Theodore Alexander, and alumni of the Kadoorie School. Michael Kadoorie inherits with Rita, overcomes a reputation for fast cars, helicopters, and limited business discipline, attends the Daya Bay opening, expands China Light and Power tenfold in market value, triples the hotel company, meets Jiang Zemin, partners with Rong Yiren's family through China International Trust and Investment Corporation, and uses a 25 percent China Light stake as insurance before buying it back. He returns the Peninsula brand to Shanghai on the old British consulate site after the Hongkong and Shanghai Bank building proves unavailable, opens the hotel with acrobatic bellhops, Chinese drummers, movie stars, officials, champagne, and the declaration that the family has come home. Kaufman then closes through the rediscovery of Shanghai memory: SMC manhole covers, murals in number 12 the Bund, Hongkew's refugee museum, Ho Feng-Shan panels, the missing Chiune Sugihara exhibit, Sir Elly's Bar, Pudong's towers, Victor's restored suite, Evelyn Cox bringing portraits of Victor and Barnsie, James Sassoon joining Jardine Matheson, and Lou Jiwei's "Let bygones be bygones" answer to property restitution. Source anchors: Michael Blumenthal, Lucie Hartwich, Daya Bay, Rong Yiren, British consulate site, SMC manhole covers, Ho Feng-Shan, Lou Jiwei.

Analysis: Michael Blumenthal, Lucie Hartwich, Theodore Alexander, and the Kadoorie School reunion give Horace's wartime work a human afterlife outside Shanghai and Hong Kong balance sheets. Daya Bay, Rong Yiren, China International Trust and Investment Corporation, and the British consulate site show Michael inheriting Lawrence's method of using Chinese political relationships as protection while presenting luxury hotels as cultural return. The SMC manhole covers, Ho Feng-Shan displays, missing Sugihara exhibit, Victor's suite, Evelyn Cox's portraits, James Sassoon at Jardine, and Lou Jiwei's joke about bygones return the book to Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation: Shanghai remembers the families when they are useful to the city's global image, but the losses, opium money, refugee rescue, and Communist confiscations remain curated by the state.
