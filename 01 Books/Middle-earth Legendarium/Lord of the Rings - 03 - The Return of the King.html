Lord of the Rings - 03 - The Return of the King

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer. It is the third and final volume of The Lord of the Rings and the capstone of the Middle-earth Legendarium series: it completes the War of the Ring that begins in The Fellowship of the Ring and intensifies in The Two Towers, and it sits beside The Hobbit and The Silmarillion as the volume that turns the whole legendarium's history into consequence. Thematically it is the series' most concentrated statement of War, Crisis, and Collapse and Legitimacy and Succession: it stages total war against the West, the sudden collapse of Sauron's realm, and the restoration of Gondor's kingship, and its prolonged epilogue asks how far any victory can repair what the war destroyed. Readers reaching this note from the series hub should track it together with its siblings as the culmination of one long decline-and-restoration arc.

Detailed overview

The Return of the King completes the war by splitting victory among people who cannot see the whole field. Gandalf and Pippin reach Minas Tirith, Aragorn takes the Paths of the Dead, Theoden musters Rohan, Denethor misreads the palantir and burns in despair, and Frodo and Sam are still trapped in Mordor's borderlands. Tolkien makes the final campaign depend on partial knowledge: Minas Tirith does not know whether Rohan will come, Rohan does not know whether Aragorn will return from the Stone of Erech, and the Captains of the West do not know whether Frodo is alive.

Book Five centers on public rule under pressure. Minas Tirith is a city of circles, archives, lineages, errand-riders, healers, guards, and failing stewardship; Rohan is a people of roads, songs, oaths, and musters. Denethor, Theoden, Eowyn, Faramir, Imrahil, Beregond, Hirgon, Ghân-buri-Ghân, and the Rohirrim all show different forms of service or refusal. The Siege of Gondor and the Pelennor Fields are not won by one hero but by a chain of decisions: Pippin tells Gandalf about Denethor's pyre, Eowyn and Merry strike the Witch-king, Aragorn arrives by the river, and the wounded are saved in the Houses of Healing.

Book Six narrows the end of the quest to Sam and Frodo after all military success has become a diversion. Sam rescues Frodo from the Tower of Cirith Ungol, but the two hobbits then cross Gorgoroth with no army, no reliable food, no water security, and no ability to resist the Ring by strength. Sauron's attention is drawn to the Black Gate while the real struggle occurs on the slopes of Orodruin, where Frodo claims the Ring and Gollum's last act destroys it. The book's victory is therefore not clean moral triumph; it is pity, endurance, failure at the last step, and providential consequence.

The restoration after Mordor matters because Tolkien refuses to end with battlefield success. Aragorn's coronation, Faramir and Eowyn's healing, the White Tree, Arwen's choice, Theoden's funeral, the partings at Rivendell and Lothlorien, and the hobbits' return all ask what victory can repair. The answer is limited. Minas Tirith can receive a king, Rohan can bury Theoden, and the Shire can be scoured, but Frodo's wound, the loss of the Elves, and the departure from the Grey Havens make restoration inseparable from diminishment.

The appendices turn the story into a historical artifact. Appendix A supplies the dynastic depth behind Aragorn, Arwen, Eorl, and Durin's Folk; Appendix B fixes the War of the Ring inside calendars and later lives; Appendix C grounds hobbit families in Baggins, Took, Brandybuck, and Gamgee lines; Appendices D, E, and F explain Shire calendars, pronunciation, scripts, Westron, Quenya, Sindarin, and translation. These sections do not merely add lore after the fact. They reinforce the fiction that the novel is a translated record of a world whose politics, language, timekeeping, and family memory outlast the quest.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Frodo Baggins: the Ring-bearer whose endurance carries the quest to the very Crack of Doom, only to fail at the last step when he claims the Ring ("I have come. But I do not choose now to do what I came to do. The Ring is mine!"). Physically broken by the Morgul-knife wound at Weathertop, Shelob's sting, and the Ring's weight, he finishes the Red Book and departs into the West, because restoration at Bag End proves impossible for him.
  • Samwise Gamgee: the servant whose loyalty substitutes for strategy. He rescues Frodo from the Tower of Cirith Ungol, rations water and lembas across Gorgoroth, carries his master up Mount Doom, and in the end embodies the Shire's renewal: he marries Rosie Cotton, plants the seed that becomes the mallorn of the Party Field, and is the one who returns home.
  • Aragorn (Elessar): Isildur's hidden heir who takes the Paths of the Dead, seizes the southern fleet, receives the city as healer before king, and is crowned King Elessar. His arc converts the ranger's wandering inheritance into the demonstrated legitimacy of healing, mercy, and timely return.
  • Gandalf: the strategist and war-leader of the West. He delivers Pippin to Minas Tirith, confronts Denethor, saves Faramir, leads the heraldry at the Black Gate, refuses the Mouth of Sauron, and finally sails West wearing Narya; the entire diversion of the Captains of the West is his design.
  • Denethor: the last Ruling Steward of Gondor, whose grief for Boromir and misreading of the palantir turn stewardship into despair. He burns on the pyre rather than see Aragorn rule, making him the volume's cautionary example of legitimacy refused.
  • Theoden: the renewed King of Rohan who musters the Riders, accepts the Woses' guidance, and dies in the charge on the Pelennor beneath his own horse Snowmane, his kingship completed in death.
  • Eowyn: the death-seeking shieldmaiden who rides as Dernhelm and, with Merry, destroys the Witch-king ("But no living man am I! You look upon a woman."). Healed in the Houses of Healing and in love with Faramir, she turns from glory-hunting to healing and the gardens of Ithilien.
  • Faramir: the Steward's surviving son who resists the lures of power, governs the City in the king's absence, surrenders the stewardship willingly at the coronation, and weds Eowyn; the moral counterweight to both his father and his brother.
  • Merry: the hobbit who becomes Théoden's esquire of Rohan, delivers the crucial wound to the Witch-king with his barrow-blade, and helps rouse the Shire against Sharkey's Men before settling back to Buckland life.
  • Pippin: the hobbit sworn into the Guards of the Citadel, whose service keeps Gondor in view from inside; he uncovers Denethor's pyre, calls Gandalf in time to save Faramir, and kills a troll-chief at the Black Gate.
  • Gollum (Sméagol): the tormented creature whose hunger for the Ring undoes Frodo's failure, biting off the finger and falling into the Fire; the living proof of the power of pity, even though he remains enslaved and unrepentant.
  • Saruman (Sharkey): the deposed wizard released by Treebeard, who returns to dominate the Shire through Lotho Sackville-Baggins's bought rule, petty bureaucracy, and gang violence until his servant Wormtongue kills him at Bag End.

Major linkages

  • Series: the volume is the capstone of the Middle-earth Legendarium series; it should be read with The Fellowship of the Ring and The Two Towers as a single narrative, and against The Hobbit (whose plot it completes — the Ring, Gollum, the Barrow-blade, the Shire) and The Silmarillion (which supplies the First Age depth that Appendix A summarizes).
  • Concepts the volume speaks to most directly (developed in Core concepts and threaded through the chapter analyses): War, Crisis, and Collapse, Legitimacy and Succession, Order and Governance, State Capacity, Empire and Periphery, Collapse and Resilience, Religion and Legitimacy, Knowledge Preservation, and Ecological Constraint.
  • Source material: the book rests on the full extracted text, linked under Source links; the appendix apparatus makes the extract itself an archive of Knowledge Preservation.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Victory through misdirection: the march of the Captains of the West to the Black Gate is a deliberate diversion; every commander acts on partial information and the decisive act is unseen by all the actors.
  • Kingship earned by service, healing, and succession: Aragorn's legitimacy is demonstrated through the Houses of Healing, the surrender of stewardship, and the sapling of the White Tree, not through conquest.
  • Stewardship as trust under pressure: Denethor's despair versus Faramir's willing surrender; Beregond's divided duty; the Stewards' centuries of carrying royal authority.
  • Pity and mercy as causal forces: Frodo's and Sam's mercy toward Gollum is what destroys the Ring; the Witch-king falls to actors overlooked by its prophecy.
  • Restoration inseparable from diminishment: the Shire is scoured, but Frodo cannot stay; the Elves depart; every repair has a cost.
  • The ruin and repair of the land: felled trees, spoiled fields, and the mallorn of the Party Field make ecological damage a measure of war and its aftermath.
  • The story as record: the Red Book, annals, genealogies, calendars, and writing systems all frame the war as something remembered and translated.

Core concepts

  • War, Crisis, and Collapse: the volume enacts total war — the Siege of Gondor, the Pelennor Fields, the Black Gate — and then Sauron's realm collapses in an hour when the Ring is unmade; the collapse of Barad-dûr is simultaneous with the last battle.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: Aragorn's claim is validated by healing, the surrender of the stewardship, and a coronation that he does not perform on himself; Théoden names Éomer heir; Denethor's refusal to accept succession is his defining failure.
  • Order and Governance: Gondor's circle-city and Guards of the Citadel, Pippin's oath, Beregond's broken command that still serves order, and the Shire's self-rule and rising at Bywater all test what legitimate administration looks like under siege and under petty tyranny.
  • State Capacity: beacons, errand-riders, musters, gates, wains, healing-houses, and the deliberate expenditure of the last army of the West show what a polity can mobilize; Sauron's war economy of orc labor, roads, and forges is the mirror image.
  • Empire and Periphery: Gondor's relation to Rohan, the Corsairs of Umbar, the Haradrim, the Woses, Mirkwood, and the sundered northern realm of Arnor is the map on which the war is fought; the Scouring shows a neglected periphery.
  • Collapse and Resilience: the orc garrison at Cirith Ungol destroys itself before Sam can fight it; Isengard is dismantled; Barad-dûr falls; yet the Shire heals, the White Tree grows again, and new dynasties begin.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: Ioreth's proverb, athelas, the White Tree and its sapling on Mindolluin, and the coronation liturgy make royal authority a matter of signs and memory as much as of arms.
  • Knowledge Preservation: the Red Book, Appendix A's annals, Appendix B's Tale of Years, genealogies, calendars, and writing systems frame the entire war as a record passed on through hobbit hands.
  • Ecological Constraint: the Scouring's felled trees and spoiled fields, Sam's forestry work, and the mallorn of the Party Field tie political recovery to the slow repair of the land itself.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Book Five, Chapter 1: Minas Tirith

Summary: Gandalf brings Pippin to Minas Tirith, where the city rises in seven circles beneath the White Tower and the dead White Tree. Pippin meets Denethor, sees the Steward's grief for Boromir, swears service to Gondor, and is placed among the Guards of the Citadel under Beregond's guidance. The chapter introduces Faramir's danger, the palantir-shadow over Denethor, the errand-riders and defenses of the city, and Pippin's small but binding role inside a realm preparing for siege.

Analysis: Minas Tirith makes Gondor visible as architecture and duty before battle. Pippin's oath to Denethor places the Shire inside Order and Governance through personal service, while Beregond translates formal command into human loyalty. The White Tree and Faramir expose Legitimacy and Succession under strain because Gondor preserves royal symbols while a Steward's grief and pride distort the office meant to hold authority in trust.

Source anchors: Minas Tirith, Pippin, Denethor, White Tree, Beregond, Guards of the Citadel, Faramir.

Book Five, Chapter 2: The Passing of the Grey Company

Summary: Aragorn, Legolas, Gimli, and the Rohirrim receive the Grey Company, Halbarad, Elladan, Elrohir, and the banner sent by Arwen. Aragorn looks into the palantir, reveals himself to Sauron, and chooses the Paths of the Dead because Gondor's southern war and the Corsairs of Umbar threaten Minas Tirith from behind. At Dunharrow, Eowyn pleads for action and recognition, but Aragorn rides to the Dimholt, the Door of the Dead, the Stone of Erech, and the oath-breaking Dead Men of Dunharrow.

Analysis: The Grey Company ties Aragorn's private ranger life to Legitimacy and Succession, and Halbarad carrying Arwen's banner makes his claim both martial and personal. The palantir is risky because Aragorn uses the same instrument that ruined Denethor and Saruman, but he uses it to seize initiative from Sauron. Eowyn and the Paths of the Dead place two blocked forms of courage side by side: hers denied by gendered duty, Aragorn's required by inheritance. The Corsairs pressing on Gondor's southern coasts and the oath-breaking Dead Men make the crisis a question of War, Crisis, and Collapse that only the heir can resolve by taking the road others will not.

Source anchors: Grey Company, Halbarad, Arwen, palantir, Paths of the Dead, Eowyn, Stone of Erech.

Book Five, Chapter 3: The Muster of Rohan

Summary: Merry rides with Theoden to Dunharrow as Rohan gathers under the shadow of Mordor, with Eomer, Eowyn, Elfhelm, and the Riders preparing for a road few expect to survive. Hirgon brings the Red Arrow from Gondor, reporting the siege threat and asking for aid, while Theoden counts too few riders and too much distance. Merry is told he cannot come, Eowyn disguises herself as Dernhelm, and the muster begins the long ride toward Minas Tirith.

Analysis: Dunharrow turns courage into Logistics and Throughput because Theoden must answer Hirgon and the Red Arrow with fewer riders, horses, and hours than Gondor needs. Merry's exclusion reflects carrying capacity rather than lack of loyalty, while Eowyn's Dernhelm disguise evades the allocation of roles. The muster makes the ride to war a problem of assembling and moving force under shortage, secrecy, and grief.

Source anchors: Dunharrow, Theoden, Merry, Eowyn, Hirgon, Red Arrow, Dernhelm.

Book Five, Chapter 4: The Siege of Gondor

Summary: Minas Tirith prepares for assault while Faramir returns from the Causeway Forts and Osgiliath, bringing news of Frodo, Sam, and the road toward Cirith Ungol. Denethor clashes with Gandalf over authority, sends Faramir back into danger, and watches his son brought home wounded under the Black Breath. The armies of Mordor under the Witch-king break the outer defenses, Grond smashes the Great Gate, and Denethor's despair turns toward the House of the Stewards and the pyre.

Analysis: Minas Tirith is besieged from outside by the Witch-king and from inside by Denethor's despair. Faramir's return from Osgiliath gives Gandalf crucial news of Frodo but also exposes the Steward's cruelty toward the son who survived. Grond breaking the gate and the Black Breath taking Faramir show military and spiritual assault converging at the moment Denethor chooses death over stewardship. The city's whole defense runs on State Capacity — beacons, errand-riders, gates, garrison, and the muster behind the walls — while Denethor's refusal to share command converts maximal preparation into an Order and Governance failure and bends the chapter toward War, Crisis, and Collapse at the gate.

Source anchors: Minas Tirith, Faramir, Osgiliath, Denethor, Black Breath, Witch-king, Grond.

Book Five, Chapter 5: The Ride of the Rohirrim

Summary: The Rohirrim ride through the Druadan Forest with help from Ghân-buri-Ghân and the Woses, who guide them past enemy watchers toward the Pelennor. Theoden learns that Hirgon is dead, sees the fires and darkness over Minas Tirith, and prepares his riders for a charge at dawn. As the horns of Rohan sound, Theoden becomes a figure of renewed kingship, and the Riders sweep onto the Pelennor Fields against Mordor's besieging army.

Analysis: The Rohirrim reach Minas Tirith because Theoden accepts help from Ghan-buri-Ghan and the Woses, people outside formal honor systems. Their Druadan Forest knowledge creates Informal Institutions of reciprocity between marginalized guides and a king. The hidden route bypasses the blocked road as a Chokepoint and Gateway, converting local knowledge into strategic salvation before the Pelennor battle.

Source anchors: Rohirrim, Druadan Forest, Ghan-buri-Ghan, Woses, Theoden, Minas Tirith, Pelennor Fields.

Book Five, Chapter 6: The Battle of the Pelennor Fields

Summary: The battle brings Theoden's charge, the Haradrim, the Mumakil, the Witch-king, Eowyn as Dernhelm, Merry with his barrow-blade, and the death of the Lord of the Nazgul into one field. Theoden is crushed by Snowmane, Eowyn reveals herself, Merry strikes from behind, and together they destroy the Witch-king before Eowyn collapses. Aragorn then arrives in the captured ships with the standard of the White Tree, bringing Legolas, Gimli, the Grey Company, and southern forces to turn the battle.

Analysis: Pelennor Fields completes Theoden's kingship in death and Eowyn's hidden defiance in action. The Witch-king falls because Merry's barrow-blade and Eowyn's courage combine two overlooked actors, a hobbit and a shieldmaiden, in the largest War, Crisis, and Collapse of the war. The White Tree standard announces Aragorn without a coronation, making his first public claim to Gondor a military rescue rather than a speech — Legitimacy and Succession declared on the field before it is ratified in the City.

Source anchors: Pelennor Fields, Theoden, Witch-king, Eowyn, Merry, Mumakil, White Tree.

Book Five, Chapter 7: The Pyre of Denethor

Summary: Pippin realizes that Denethor means to burn himself and the wounded Faramir alive in the House of the Stewards, and he runs to find Gandalf while Beregond abandons his post to block the servants of the pyre. Gandalf confronts Denethor, who reveals his knowledge through the palantir and his refusal to be a lesser steward under Aragorn. Faramir is saved, but Denethor burns with the palantir in his hands, while outside the battle continues and the cost of divided duty falls on Beregond.

Analysis: Pippin and Beregond save Faramir by breaking ordinary command, showing Order and Governance cannot reduce service to obedience when the Steward destroys the trust he holds. The palantir gives Denethor true information through Sauron's framing, making despair a failure of interpretation rather than simple ignorance. Gandalf's diversion to the House of Stewards links institutional rescue inside the city to military risk at the gate.

Source anchors: Pippin, Denethor, Faramir, House of the Stewards, Beregond, palantir, Gandalf.

Book Five, Chapter 8: The Houses of Healing

Summary: In the Houses of Healing, Faramir, Eowyn, and Merry lie under the Black Breath while Ioreth speaks of the old saying that the hands of the king are the hands of a healer. Aragorn enters the city quietly, uses athelas, calls Faramir back, heals Eowyn and Merry, and refuses to claim the throne by spectacle before the war is finished. Eomer grieves Theoden and Eowyn, Imrahil recognizes Aragorn's authority, and the city begins to understand the returning king through healing rather than conquest.

Analysis: The Houses of Healing make Aragorn's kingship practical before ceremonial. Ioreth's proverb and athelas create Religion and Legitimacy through a remembered sign that Gondor can feel and smell when the hidden king heals Faramir, Eowyn, and Merry. Aragorn's restraint strengthens the claim because he enters as healer, exercises authority without seizing the city, and leaves coronation until Sauron's defeat.

Source anchors: Houses of Healing, Faramir, Eowyn, Merry, Ioreth, athelas, Aragorn.

Book Five, Chapter 9: The Last Debate

Summary: The Captains of the West meet after the Pelennor victory and decide that military survival is not enough while Frodo's errand remains unknown. Gandalf, Aragorn, Eomer, Imrahil, Legolas, Gimli, and others debate marching to the Black Gate as a diversion, knowing they cannot defeat Mordor by force. Aragorn releases the faint-hearted at the Cross-roads, sends some to retake Cair Andros, and leads a deliberately small army toward the Morannon to draw Sauron's Eye away from the Ring-bearer.

Analysis: The Captains of the West treat Pelennor's victory as time purchased for Frodo. Their Black Gate march becomes Crisis Governance through deliberate misdirection rather than conquest: leaders expose the remaining army to hold Sauron's Eye away from Mordor's interior. Decisions at the Cross-roads and Cair Andros preserve small reserves while Aragorn spends military strength and his public claim on a gamble whose decisive actor cannot coordinate with him.

Source anchors: Captains of the West, Gandalf, Aragorn, Black Gate, Cross-roads, Cair Andros, Sauron's Eye.

Book Five, Chapter 10: The Black Gate Opens

Summary: Aragorn's host reaches the Morannon, where the Mouth of Sauron comes out with Frodo's mithril coat, Sam's sword, the grey cloak, and threats of torment. Gandalf rejects the terms, takes the tokens, and refuses to believe that Sauron's messenger understands the whole truth. The Captains form their last defense on the slag hills before the Black Gate as armies of Mordor surround them and Pippin, beneath a troll, hears cries that the Eagles are coming.

Analysis: The Morannon confrontation attacks hope by displaying the mithril coat as evidence that Frodo has failed. Gandalf's refusal before the Mouth of Sauron keeps the diversion intact even when the tokens wound everyone who recognizes them. The scene exposes the limit of Sauron's State Capacity: his intelligence is real, but his interpretation of it is wrong, and no amount of coercion can correct a misunderstanding. Pippin and the Eagles close Book Five with a soldier's fragmentary perception: the battle is lost by ordinary measures just as help and the Ring's destruction approach unseen, a final commitment to War, Crisis, and Collapse in which the winning move is invisible.

Source anchors: Morannon, Mouth of Sauron, mithril coat, Gandalf, Black Gate, Pippin, Eagles.

Book Six, Chapter 1: The Tower of Cirith Ungol

Summary: Sam returns to the Tower of Cirith Ungol, passes the Watchers with Galadriel's phial, and discovers that Shagrat and Gorbag's orcs have slaughtered one another over Frodo's gear. He finds Frodo stripped, beaten, and imprisoned, but alive, and returns the Ring after a painful moment in which Frodo sees him as a thief. The two hobbits disguise themselves in orc-gear, escape the tower, and begin the final passage into Mordor.

Analysis: The Tower of Cirith Ungol falls from within because Shagrat and Gorbag's rivalry destroys the garrison before Sam can mount a real assault — Mordor's policing apparatus collapsing in Collapse and Resilience from internal competition. Galadriel's phial lets Sam pass the Watchers, but the Ring creates the hardest moment between him and Frodo by turning rescue into accusation, a test of Order and Governance inside the friendship itself. Leaving the tower in orc-gear makes survival depend on disguise and exhaustion rather than heroic victory.

Source anchors: Tower of Cirith Ungol, Sam, Watchers, Galadriel's phial, Shagrat, Gorbag, Ring.

Book Six, Chapter 2: The Land of Shadow

Summary: Frodo and Sam cross Mordor in stolen orc-gear, moving through ash, thirst, fumes, road systems, marching troops, and the pressure of the Ring. They are caught in an orc column, forced to march, and barely escape when confusion and discipline break down among Sauron's servants. Sam rations water and lembas, sees that Frodo can no longer carry much, and begins to measure the journey to Mount Doom as a problem of bodily endurance rather than route-finding.

Analysis: Mordor strips the quest to thirst, weight, and concealment, while orc-gear protects Frodo and Sam by absorbing them into the machinery they hate. The orc column reveals Coercive Labor through whips, forced marching, and expendable bodies that supply Sauron's armies. Water, lembas, roads, and Mount Doom become Logistics and Throughput in Sam's arithmetic as Frodo's carrying capacity is consumed by the Ring.

Source anchors: Mordor, orc-gear, Ring, orc column, water, lembas, Mount Doom.

Book Six, Chapter 3: Mount Doom

Summary: Frodo and Sam reach the slopes of Orodruin as the armies at the Black Gate draw Sauron's attention away. Sam carries Frodo, Gollum reappears and attacks them, and Frodo enters the Sammath Naur where the Ring was made. At the final moment Frodo claims the Ring instead of destroying it, Gollum bites off his finger, takes the Ring, and falls into the fire, unmaking Sauron's power as Barad-dur collapses.

Analysis: Orodruin exposes the limit of Frodo's will: reaching the Sammath Naur does not make destruction possible. Gollum's survival creates a Moral Economy in which Bilbo's and Frodo's earlier mercy produces a consequence no strategist could command, though Gollum never repents. The Black Gate diversion and Barad-dur's fall reveal victory emerging from pity, failure, and obsession after armies merely create the conditions for that collision.

Source anchors: Orodruin, Black Gate, Gollum, Sammath Naur, Ring, Sauron, Barad-dur.

Book Six, Chapter 4: The Field of Cormallen

Summary: Sam wakes in Ithilien after rescue by the Eagles and learns that Frodo is alive, Sauron has fallen, and the Captains survived the battle at the Black Gate. On the Field of Cormallen, Aragorn honors Frodo and Sam before the host, minstrels sing of the Ring-bearers, and the hobbits reunite with Gandalf, Merry, Pippin, Legolas, Gimli, and others. The chapter turns private endurance in Mordor into public thanksgiving while leaving Frodo physically and inwardly marked by the quest.

Analysis: Ithilien gives Frodo and Sam green life after Mordor, and the Eagles make their survival feel like rescue rather than conquest. The Field of Cormallen matters because Aragorn places the Ring-bearers above the warriors, correcting the usual hierarchy of fame. The minstrels, songs, and honoring of names turn the hour into Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation: the war's outcome becomes a story before it becomes a settlement. Frodo and Sam receive honor, but the title Ring-bearers also names an injury that celebration cannot erase.

Source anchors: Ithilien, Eagles, Field of Cormallen, Aragorn, Frodo, Sam, Ring-bearers.

Book Six, Chapter 5: The Steward and the King

Summary: In Minas Tirith, Faramir serves as Steward while waiting for the king, and Eowyn remains in the Houses of Healing wrestling with despair, glory, and the desire not to be caged. Faramir and Eowyn come to understand one another as news arrives from the Black Gate and Mordor, and their relationship turns from shared woundedness toward a future in Ithilien. Aragorn enters the city, is crowned with the crown of Eärnur brought from the tombs of Rath Dínen, receives Faramir's surrender of stewardship, finds the sapling of the White Tree, and weds Arwen.

Analysis: Faramir and Eowyn show healing as a change in desire: Eowyn turns from death-seeking renown toward life in Ithilien, and Faramir can love without possessing. Minas Tirith receives Aragorn through the crown of Elendil and Faramir's willing stewardship, not through seizure, making the coronation an act of Legitimacy and Succession that is consented to rather than imposed. The White Tree and Arwen join political renewal to older Elvish and Numenorean continuity, and the sapling found on Mindolluin ties restoration itself to Religion and Legitimacy.

Source anchors: Faramir, Eowyn, Minas Tirith, Aragorn, crown of Eärnur, White Tree, Arwen.

Book Six, Chapter 6: Many Partings

Summary: The company leaves Minas Tirith after days of celebration, travels to Rohan for Theoden's funeral, and sees Eomer confirmed as king. Eowyn and Faramir's betrothal, Merry's farewell to Rohan, and Eomer's grief for Theoden turn victory into ordered leave-taking. The travelers continue through Isengard, where Treebeard has released Saruman, then to Rivendell, where Bilbo is old and fading, and onward as the great companions begin separating.

Analysis: Minas Tirith cannot hold the story after victory because Theoden's funeral and Eomer's accession require Rohan's own closure, a succession completed under Legitimacy and Succession. Eowyn and Faramir carry healing into alliance between Gondor and Rohan, binding the two realms as Empire and Periphery partners rather than lord and dependence. Merry's leave-taking honors the bond formed in war. Treebeard's release of Saruman keeps danger alive and shows that mercy after victory can have costs beyond the battlefield.

Source anchors: Minas Tirith, Theoden's funeral, Eomer, Eowyn, Faramir, Treebeard, Saruman.

Book Six, Chapter 7: Homeward Bound

Summary: The hobbits, Gandalf, and their companions travel north, meet Saruman and Wormtongue on the road, and learn that Saruman's malice has not ended with Isengard. At Rivendell, Bilbo gives Frodo gifts and memories rather than guidance, and the hobbits eventually turn toward Bree. Barliman Butterbur reports trouble in the North, the absence of Rangers, closed roads, and rumors that prepare the hobbits for a Shire changed in their absence.

Analysis: Saruman and Wormtongue on the road reduce the fallen wizard from world-threat to poisonous vagrant, but his words still point toward harm ahead. Rivendell and Bilbo offer tenderness without restoration, since Frodo has outgrown the simple return to Bilbo's adventure-world. Bree, Barliman Butterbur, and the missing Rangers show how much the North's safety had depended on the Rangers' thin deployed capacity — State Capacity stretched until ordinary places lost their protection unseen. The closed roads and rumours preview a Shire that has become a neglected Empire and Periphery margin in the disorders after the war.

Source anchors: Saruman, Wormtongue, Rivendell, Bilbo, Bree, Barliman Butterbur, Rangers.

Book Six, Chapter 8: The Scouring of the Shire

Summary: Frodo, Sam, Merry, and Pippin return to find the Shire controlled by Sharkey's Men, Lotho Sackville-Baggins's regime, rules, lockholes, cut trees, ugly sheds, and dispossession. The hobbits rouse Hobbiton, Bywater, Tookland, and the Shire-folk, defeat the ruffians at the Battle of Bywater, and discover Saruman himself at Bag End with Wormtongue. Frodo forbids revenge, Saruman is killed by Wormtongue, Wormtongue is shot by hobbit archers, and Sam later uses Galadriel's gift to begin healing the land.

Analysis: The Shire is not saved by distant kings; Merry, Pippin, Sam, and Frodo must rebuild Order and Governance in Hobbiton and Bywater. Sharkey and Lotho reduce domination to rules, lists, petty industry, surveillance, and local collaboration. Frodo's mercy at Bag End and Sam's use of Galadriel's gift separate moral repair from Ecological Constraint, because political liberation alone cannot restore felled trees, spoiled fields, and damaged homes.

Source anchors: Shire, Sharkey, Lotho, Battle of Bywater, Bag End, Saruman, Galadriel's gift.

Book Six, Chapter 9: The Grey Havens

Summary: The Shire is restored outwardly, Sam marries Rosie Cotton, becomes a father, and helps renew the land, while Frodo remains wounded by Weathertop, Shelob, the Ring, and the anniversary illnesses. Frodo finishes the Red Book, gives it to Sam, and rides with Bilbo, Elrond, Galadriel, Gandalf, and others to the Grey Havens. At the Sea, Frodo departs into the West, leaving Sam, Merry, and Pippin behind; Sam returns to Bag End and his family life.

Analysis: The Shire can be renewed for Sam and Rosie Cotton, but Frodo's wounds make ordinary restoration impossible. The Red Book performs Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation by transferring witness from Frodo to Sam and turning private suffering into the record from which the story descends. The Grey Havens separate healing from homecoming: Frodo leaves, while Sam returns to Bag End and carries both the book and the life Frodo preserved.

Source anchors: Shire, Rosie Cotton, Red Book, Bilbo, Grey Havens, Frodo, Sam.

Appendix A: Annals of the Kings and Rulers

Summary: Appendix A gathers dynastic and historical material behind the narrative, including Numenor, the Realms in Exile, Arnor, Gondor, the heirs of Isildur, the Stewards, and Aragorn's line. It also gives the tale of Aragorn and Arwen, the House of Eorl, the Kings of the Mark, and Durin's Folk, linking Gondor, Rohan, and the Dwarves to older migrations, marriages, wars, and losses. The appendix turns names used in the story, such as Elendil, Isildur, Eorl, Thorin, Dain, and Arwen, into parts of longer political and family histories.

Analysis: Numenor, Arnor, and Gondor supply the institutional background for Aragorn's return, so his coronation is not an isolated romance of kingship but the working out of a succession under Legitimacy and Succession. Aragorn and Arwen adds a personal cost to the restored line, while the House of Eorl explains Rohan's alliance as history rather than convenience, binding the Empire and Periphery relationship of Gondor and Rohan to oath and descent. Durin's Folk and Isildur keep Dwarvish and Numenorean memory tied to the same long defeat that the War of the Ring only partly resolves.

Source anchors: Numenor, Arnor, Gondor, Aragorn and Arwen, House of Eorl, Durin's Folk, Isildur.

Appendix B: The Tale of Years

Summary: Appendix B sets the story into chronology from the Elder Days through the Second Age, Third Age, the Great Years, and later events concerning members of the Fellowship. It records dates for Sauron, the Rings of Power, the Last Alliance, the founding of the Shire, Bilbo's journey, Frodo's departure, the War of the Ring, Aragorn's reign, and the later lives of Sam, Merry, Pippin, Legolas, and Gimli. The appendix makes the novel's events one dense cluster inside a much longer calendar of decline and succession.

Analysis: The Tale of Years turns the War of the Ring into dated history, and the Second Age and Third Age show how long Sauron's defeat had been deferred — the generations-long horizon of War, Crisis, and Collapse. The Shire dates place hobbit life inside the same chronology as empires and battles, which matches the novel's insistence that small places belong to history. The Great Years and Fellowship entries extend consequence beyond the final chapter, showing how victory becomes memory: the whole chronological apparatus is a labor of Knowledge Preservation.

Source anchors: Tale of Years, Second Age, Third Age, Great Years, Shire, War of the Ring, Fellowship.

Appendix C: Family Trees

Summary: Appendix C provides selected hobbit genealogies, especially the Baggins, Took, Brandybuck, and Gamgee lines tied to Bilbo's Farewell Party and its descendants. The trees use Shire-reckoning dates, marriages, births, and deaths to place Frodo, Bilbo, Merry, Pippin, Sam, Rosie Cotton, Elanor, and later Gardner descendants inside family continuity. The appendix also reinforces how much the hobbits understand identity through kinship, inheritance, and remembered households.

Analysis: Family Trees matters because the Baggins, Took, Brandybuck, and Gamgee lines are the social map behind the Shire chapters, and the genealogies make kinship a Legitimacy and Succession concern even where the stakes are inheritances and marriages rather than thrones. Shire-reckoning and Rosie Cotton place the epic afterlife of the quest into births and marriages rather than monuments. Elanor and the Gardner descendants show Sam's restoration becoming generational — Knowledge Preservation through descent — while Frodo's departure leaves a record rather than a household line.

Source anchors: Family Trees, Baggins, Took, Brandybuck, Gamgee, Shire-reckoning, Rosie Cotton, Elanor.

Appendix D: Shire Calendar

Summary: Appendix D explains the Shire Calendar, including weekdays, months, Yule, Lithe, Mid-year's Day, Overlithe, Shire-reckoning, and differences from calendars in Bree, Rivendell, Rohan, Gondor, and the Kings' Reckoning. It treats timekeeping as a local cultural practice with practical consequences for dates in the Red Book. The section also clarifies how hobbit regularity, festival days, and calendar conservatism fit the broader chronological apparatus of Middle-earth.

Analysis: The Shire Calendar makes hobbit time as specific as hobbit food or family names, and Yule, Lithe, and Overlithe give the Shire's year its own texture — Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy expressed in timekeeping. Shire-reckoning also matters for the Red Book because the story's dates pass through local record-keeping before reaching the reader, a piece of Knowledge Preservation. Bree and Kings' Reckoning show that time itself is translated across communities, just like language and names.

Source anchors: Shire Calendar, Yule, Lithe, Overlithe, Shire-reckoning, Bree, Kings' Reckoning.

Appendix E: Writing and Pronunciation

Summary: Appendix E explains pronunciation, consonants, vowels, stress, and scripts used for names and records in the translated history. It distinguishes Quenya, Sindarin, Westron representation, the Tengwar, the Cirth, the Angerthas, and the choices made in rendering ancient scripts into modern letters. The section gives readers tools for understanding names such as Celeborn, Cirith, Cirdan, Isengard, and other forms that carry linguistic history inside the narrative.

Analysis: Quenya and Sindarin keep Elvish history audible in names rather than leaving it as decorative sound, so linguistic practice becomes Language and Ideology in miniature. Tengwar, Cirth, and Angerthas connect inscriptions, books, tombs, and maps to writing systems with their own traditions — the material side of Knowledge Preservation. Pronunciation and Westron complete the translator fiction: the English text is not presented as original speech but as a careful substitution for languages inside Middle-earth.

Source anchors: Quenya, Sindarin, Tengwar, Cirth, Angerthas, pronunciation, Westron.

Appendix F: Languages and Peoples of the Third Age

Summary: Appendix F describes the languages and peoples of the Third Age, including Elves, Men, Hobbits, other races, Westron or Common Speech, Rohirric representation, names translated into English, and the treatment of archaic or regional forms. It explains how the narrative renders Hobbit names, place names, and speech levels, and why some names are translated while others remain Elvish, Dwarvish, or Mannish. The section links linguistic difference to migration, class, memory, and the reader's access to the Red Book.

Analysis: Third Age language history makes Elves, Men, and Hobbits politically and culturally distinct without isolating them from one another. Westron and Common Speech explain how the Fellowship can communicate while still preserving older names and identities — a functioning language policy within Language and Ideology. The choices of which names to translate and which to preserve map the cultural borders among Empire and Periphery communities. Translation is the appendix's central device, because it turns the whole novel into an artifact mediated through hobbit records, scholarly choices, and Knowledge Preservation.

Source anchors: Third Age, Elves, Men, Hobbits, Westron, Common Speech, translation.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • Battle of Bywater, S.R. 1419: nearly seventy ruffians were slain and a dozen taken prisoner; nineteen hobbits were killed and about thirty wounded. The dead ruffians went to the old sand-pit ("the Battle Pit"); the fallen hobbits were laid in a single grave on the hillside with a great stone and garden about it. It was the last battle of the Shire and has a chapter of its own in the Red Book.
  • The Witch-king's undoing: his boast "No living man may hinder me" is answered "'But no living man am I! You look upon a woman. Eowyn I am'"; Merry's barrow-blade — from the tombs of the Barrow-downs in the earlier story — stabbed the sinew behind his knee, and Eowyn's sword drove in "between crown and mantle," its blade shattering.
  • Frodo's final words at the Sammath Naur: "'I have come,' he said. 'But I do not choose now to do what I came to do. The Ring is mine!'" and Gollum's last cry, "'Precious, precious, precious! ... O my Precious!'", before he topples into the fire still holding the finger.
  • The coronation: Aragorn chooses not to crown himself; the Ring-bearer carries the crown of Eärnur, and Gandalf sets it on his head. Aragorn speaks Elendil's words from the Upheaval: "Et Eärello Endorenna utúlien" — "Out of the Great Sea to Middle-earth I am come."
  • The White Tree: the withered Tree of Minas Tirith was left standing "until the King returns"; Aragorn finds a sapling, "no more than three foot high," growing out of the snow on Mount Mindolluin, and replants it in the Court of the Fountain.
  • Ioreth's proverb: "The hands of the king are the hands of a healer," which is how the City learns its king has come; the herb is athelas, "the king's herb."
  • The Grey Havens: Círdan the Shipwright greets the riders; Gandalf now wears Narya openly ("the stone upon it was red as fire"); the farewell line "I will not say: do not weep; for not all tears are an evil"; Sam's last words in the book after Rose draws him in: "Well, I'm back."
  • The muster of Rohan: "five and fifty hundreds of Riders fully armed" — roughly 5,500 — at the muster before the long ride; Ghân-buri-Ghân and the Woses guide them down the forgotten wain-road of the Stonewain Valley, past the watchers on Amon Dîn.
  • The mallorn of the Party Field: Sam plants the silver nut from Galadriel's box where the old Party Tree stood; it becomes "the only mallorn west of the Mountains and east of the Sea," with silver bark and golden flowers in April.
  • Three tokens of the Mouth of Sauron: Sam's short sword, the grey elven-cloak with its brooch, and Frodo's coat of mithril-mail are displayed before the Captains as proof of the Ring-bearer's fall; Gandalf takes them "in memory of our friend."
  • Black Gate terms: retreat beyond the Anduin and release Sauron's claim only as far as the Misty Mountains, the lands west of the River tributary, and Isengard rebuilt — the "haggling" Gandalf calls an admission that Sauron can no longer win by war.
  • Dates that anchor the ending: March 25 is the date of the downfall of the Barad-dûr in both Kings' and Stewards' Reckoning; October 6 is the recurring anniversary of the Weathertop wound in the Shire-reckoning, when Frodo falls ill again; Bilbo turns 131 (passing the Old Took) in the autumn.
  • Beregond's fate: for shedding blood in the Hallows and leaving his post he is removed from the Guard of the Citadel — then appointed captain of the White Company, the guard of Faramir in Emyn Arnen, "in the service of him for whom you risked all".
  • Aragorn's first judgments: he pardons the Easterlings who surrendered, makes peace with the peoples of Harad, and gives the lands about Lake Núrnen to the freed slaves of Mordor.
