Position in the vault

This note sits inside Middle-earth Legendarium as the second volume of J. R. R. Tolkien's The Lord of the Rings, between the book notes for The Fellowship of the Ring and The Return of the King. It should be read both as a standalone work and as part of that larger series-scale structure, and its extracted source text backs every claim below. It is one of the vault's central fantasy texts on war and state breakdown, and it feeds the analysis clusters that read governance, legitimacy, and collapse across genres: War, Revolution, and Civilizational Breakdown, State, Order, and Legibility, and Identity, Culture, and Social Formation. Thematically it routes through State Formation, Legibility, and Informal Order, Identity, Memory, and Cultural Legitimacy, and Testimony, War Memory, and Broken Official Narratives.

Detailed overview

The Two Towers begins with the Fellowship already broken and refuses to repair it by reunion. Book Three follows Aragorn, Legolas, Gimli, Merry, and Pippin through Boromir's death, the orc pursuit, Rohan, Fangorn, Helm's Deep, and Isengard; Book Four returns to Frodo and Sam in Emyn Muil, the Dead Marshes, Ithilien, Henneth Annun, Minas Morgul, Cirith Ungol, and Shelob's tunnel. The split lets the war against Saruman and the journey to Mordor develop by different pressures: armies, kings, and counsel on one side; hunger, secrecy, pity, and betrayal on the other.

The Rohan plot is built around corrupted rule and restored action. Boromir's body goes over Rauros, Merry and Pippin are dragged toward Isengard, and Aragorn's company is drawn into a kingdom where Theoden sits diminished under Wormtongue's counsel while Saruman's orcs and Dunlendings move from Orthanc. Gandalf's return as the White Rider does not simply replace the lost Grey Pilgrim; it exposes a different task. He wakes Theoden, gathers riders, forces the crisis at Helm's Deep, and then brings Rohan and the Ents to the gates of a ruined Isengard.

Merry and Pippin's captivity becomes one of the book's main mechanisms for changing the war. Their escape into Fangorn brings Treebeard, the Entmoot, and the march of the Ents against Saruman, while their later account at Isengard gives Aragorn, Legolas, Gimli, Theoden, and Gandalf the missing history of what happened after Parth Galen. Tolkien uses small hobbits as messengers across otherwise separate theaters: through them the forest learns of the wider war, Rohan learns how Saruman fell, and Gandalf recovers enough information to interpret the palantir crisis.

Frodo and Sam's chapters narrow the heroic scale until survival depends on minute acts of judgment. They cannot enter Mordor by force, cannot trust Gollum completely, cannot go back, and cannot understand all of Faramir's war in Ithilien. The taming of Smeagol, the passage through the Dead Marshes, the closed Black Gate, the cooked rabbits, Faramir's interrogation, and the forbidden pool all test whether pity can remain practical when the guide is treacherous and the Ring is eating away at Frodo's will.

The two towers named by the title are never only buildings. Orthanc concentrates Saruman's voice, machinery, stored pipe-weed, and palantir; Minas Morgul and Cirith Ungol concentrate the road into Mordor, the Witch-king's army, Shelob's hunger, and the orc command structure that will carry Frodo away. The volume ends with both towers exposed as systems of surveillance and misdirection. Saruman loses his staff and the palantir, while Frodo loses freedom and is taken into the Tower of Cirith Ungol after Sam mistakenly believes him dead.

The final moral movement belongs to Sam. He fights Shelob with Sting and Galadriel's phial, takes the Ring because he thinks Frodo is dead, then overhears Shagrat and Gorbag reveal that Frodo is alive. That ending is cruel because Sam has done the bravest available thing and still discovers that bravery was based on incomplete knowledge. The quest continues not through triumph but through a correction: Sam must stop being only Frodo's servant and become the person who can enter an enemy tower carrying the Ring. The twin resolutions of Book Three and Book Four — Orthanc reduced to a watched ruin, and Frodo carried alive into an orc tower — set up the full war of The Return of the King.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Frodo Baggins: the Ring-bearer, carrying both the One Ring and the active choice to go to Mordor alone. Through Book Four his agency narrows to endurance: he drops his brooch-teasing and becomes the bearer whose will the Ring drains, until Shelob renders him captive. His arc is a study in carrying a burden through decisions no longer his to make.
  • Samwise Gamgee: servant and companion to Frodo, and the book's moral center of gravity. His arc runs from loyal follower to independent actor: he captures Gollum at Frodo's side, keeps watch and keep, cooks, fights Shelob, takes up the Ring when he believes Frodo dead, and ends the volume as the only free agent left to rescue his master.
  • Gollum / Sméagol: the doubled guide. Split between the faithful promise-keeping Sméagol and the Ring-crazed Gollum, he leads the hobbits through the Dead Marshes, to the Black Gate, and past Cirith Ungol, hesitating at a near-redemption before his bargain with Shelob. He is the book's living test of whether pity can be practical.
  • Aragorn: heir of Isildur and leader of the Three Hunters. Haunted by his failed choices at Parth Galen, he reads the orc-trail across Rohan, holds the line with Éomer at Helm's Deep, accepts the palantír by right, and begins the public return of the King.
  • Gandalf: returning as the White Rider and the series' chief coordinator. He pieces together the war's geometry, heals and musters Rohan, wields Shadowfax, breaks Saruman's staff, and diagnoses the palantír's misinformation before riding for Minas Tirith with Pippin.
  • Legolas and Gimli: the Elf and the Dwarf who defy their kinds' old estrangement. Their running orc-slaying count, the bargain to visit Fangorn and the Glittering Caves of Aglarond, and their doubled perspective give the war a comic-cosmic counterpoint.
  • Merry and Pippin: captives turned witnesses. Their orc-march exposes the machinery of Saruman's and Mordor's command; their meeting with Treebeard triggers the Ents' war; and their testimony at Isengard reconstructs Saruman's fall for the Company. Pippin's theft of the palantír is the book's plot hinge toward Minas Tirith.
  • Théoden: King of the Mark, diminished by Wormtongue's counsel. His healing at Gandalf's hands, his ride to Helm's Deep, and his rejection of Saruman's voice restore Rohan's capacity for self-rule.
  • Éowyn: Lady of Rohan, appointed to lead her people while men ride to war, and thereby a case study in a temperament confined by dynastic duty; her glance at Aragorn prefigures a larger arc.
  • Éomer: Third Marshal of the Mark. He defies Wormtongue's orders to destroy the orc-host, lends horses to the Three Hunters, and fights beside them at Helm's Deep and Isengard.
  • Faramir: Captain of Gondor, Boromir's brother. He interrogates Frodo at Henneth Annûn, refuses the Ring even in Gondor's extremity, shelters the hobbits, and warns against Cirith Ungol — the book's strongest argument for restraint as statecraft.
  • Boromir (dead, but presencing): his funeral boat, his horn ringing in Faramir's memory, and the cleaving of that horn make his Ring-lust the object lesson that Book Four keeps testing against.
  • Saruman: the traitor wizard of Orthanc. His voice, machines, and palantír network collapse in a single book; stripped of staff and color, he is confined to his tower as the West turns east.
  • Treebeard (Fangorn): the oldest living thing, roused by the destruction of his trees. He and the Ents march on Isengard through their own authority, outside Rohan, Gondor, or wizard command.
  • Wormtongue (Gríma): Théoden's counsellor and Saruman's spy, whose whispering is Saruman's colonial technology inside Rohan; he ends the book hoarded with Saruman in Orthanc.
  • Shelob: the ancient spider older than Sauron, guardian of the pass; a force that serves no master, only hunger, and whose sting nearly ends the quest.

Themes and concepts to track

  • War waged on two axes: public war in the West (Helm's Deep, the muster, the Ents' march) running against a secret, scaled-down road into Mordor; the book cuts between them chapter by chapter.
  • Voice, rhetoric, and administered decline: Wormtongue's whispering and Saruman's hypnotic speech are weapons used before armies arrive.
  • Surveillance, intelligence, and misinformation: the Eye, the winged Nazgûl, Saruman's birds, the palantíri, the orc command-gossip — and the accidents (Pippin's theft) that corrupt an enemy's picture of events.
  • Pity, mercy, and broken trust: Gandalf's old doctrine voiced inside the Emyn Muil; whether mercy toward Gollum is practical, sentimental, or theologically necessary.
  • Ecological and material constraint: Fangorn and the Huorns as actors, Saruman's furnaces and felling, the Dead Marshes, the desolation of Mordor, Ithilien's gardens.
  • Legitimacy under stress: Théoden's recovery, Éomer's insubordination made lawful, Faramir's refusal, Aragorn's claim, and who may rightfully command when the old order slips.
  • Memory as a force: Ents as living archives, the treasuries of Gondor, the Rhymes of Lore, and the great tales into which Sam recognizes he has fallen.

Core concepts

  • War, Crisis, and Collapse: the volume's spine — the siege of Helm's Deep, the destruction of Saruman's host, the Witch-king's army issuing from Minas Morgul, and the collapse of both towers as commanding systems.
  • State Capacity: Rohan's ability to muster, garrison, and feed; Gondor's Rangers and stewards; Mordor's garrisoned Morannon and the armies swelling its gate.
  • Order and Governance: Théoden's diminished rule restored; Faramir's jurisdiction at Henneth Annûn; the Ents' assumption of government over a conquered Isengard.
  • Knowledge Preservation: the Ents as living memory of the Elder Days; Faramir's treasuries of lore and the records Gandalf sought; the Rhymes of Lore that explain the palantíri.
  • Ecological Constraint: the Dead Marshes and Mordor's desolation as terrain, and Fangorn's trees as a decisive military power that no army plan accounts for.
  • Information and Coordination: Aragorn's trail-reading from Pippin's brooch to the "rumour of the earth"; Gandalf coordinating riders, Ents, and Erkenbrand; the palantír's misinformation reaching Sauron.
  • Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy: Rohan's identity against Saruman; the Dunlendings' remembered grievance; Gondor's Númenórean inheritance; the Shire as the standard of what is being defended.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: Aragorn's claim to the palantír, Théoden naming Éomer heir, Faramir's summons to Minas Tirith, and Gandalf casting Saruman from the order.
  • Commodity Systems: Longbottom Leaf from the Southfarthing turning up in Saruman's stores — a supply chain that draws the Shire into Isengard's web.
  • Moral Economy: Faramir's refusal of the Ring as bindable oath; Sam's obligations to a living master; the servant's claim on the servant.
  • Chokepoints and Gateways: the Morannon, Helm's Dike and the Hornburg, the Fords of Isen, Henneth Annûn's curtain, and the stairs and tunnel of Cirith Ungol.
  • Language and Ideology: Wormtongue's renaming of prudence as paralysis and Gandalf's counter-cursing, climaxing in the Voice of Saruman.
  • Polycentric Order: Ents, Rohan, and the hobbits entering the war by their own authority, coordinated but never commanded by a single center.
  • Intelligence: the palantíri as remote-reconnaissance machinery, the Eye fixing on Minas Tirith, the Nazgûl patrols, and Saruman's capture by his own stone.
  • Empire and Periphery: Mordor's imperial rallying of Easterlings and Southrons; Isengard as a coercive outpost; Rohan and Gondor as threatened frontier polities.
  • Informal Institutions: oaths sworn on the Precious, the protection Furth given to Gollum, and codes of hospitality and mercy that bind even in a war zone.
  • Logistics and Throughput: the culvert, the store-caves, ammunition and water at Helm's Deep — where a fortress wins only so long as its material life holds.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Book Three, Chapter 1: The Departure of Boromir

Summary: Aragorn climbs Amon Hen after Frodo's disappearance, hears Boromir's horn, and finds Boromir dying among slain orcs after defending Merry and Pippin. Boromir confesses that he tried to take the Ring, tells Aragorn that the hobbits were captured, and dies before Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli can save him. The three companions place Boromir in a funeral boat with his sword, horn, shield, and the weapons of his enemies, send him over Rauros, and choose to pursue the orcs rather than follow Frodo and Sam across the river.
Analysis: Amon Hen leaves Aragorn with no clean command decision within a broken fellowship: Boromir's horn has called him to one disaster while Frodo has vanished into another, and every choice redistributes failure, which makes the chapter an opening study in War, Crisis, and Collapse at the scale of a single company. Boromir's confession about the Ring gives his death moral clarity without undoing the damage, and Merry and Pippin become the living obligation that remains. Aragorn's forensic reading of tracks, signs, and the two missing packs is the volume's first extended exercise in Information and Coordination: he reconstructs Frodo's flight from footprints before ever seeing it. Rauros turns the funeral into a boundary: Aragorn can honor Boromir, but he must let Frodo go and take Legolas and Gimli into pursuit, committing them to a theater whose outcome the Ring-bearer will never see.
Source anchors: Amon Hen; Boromir's horn; Boromir; Merry; Pippin; funeral boat; Rauros; Andúril; Frodo; Sam; Isengard orc-markings.

Book Three, Chapter 2: The Riders of Rohan

Summary: Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli run across the plains of Rohan, reading signs of the orc company that carries Merry and Pippin toward Isengard. They enter the Eastemnet, see the trail of many feet, find Pippin's dropped brooch from an Elven-cloak, and meet Éomer with the Riders of Rohan after his éored has destroyed the orcs at the eaves of Fangorn. Éomer tells them of Théoden, Wormtongue, Saruman, and the troubled law of Rohan, lends them Hasufel and Arod, and directs them toward Fangorn where the hobbits may have escaped.
Analysis: Rohan appears as a political borderland where Éomer must decide whether law, service to Théoden, or judgment about strangers governs his action, a frontier version of Order and Governance under disruption because Wormtongue's rules criminalize initiative while Saruman's raids demand it. The country's empty pastures, the rumor of tribute to Mordor, and Éomer's own witheld reprimand expose State Capacity strained between an absent king and an encroaching wizard; Aragorn's answers make clear that good and ill do not change across borders. The brooch — "Not idly do the leaves of Lórien fall" — turns a wardrobe item into intelligence, and the riders' own admiration of the heirloom sword and the Elf-wrought cloaks thickens the theme of Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy as old lore walks visibly on the grass. Aragorn's reading of half-faded signs, the "rumour of the earth," and the true track inside official reports make the chapter's chase a sustained Information and Coordination set-piece, and Éomer's decision to break his own law for strangers plants a seed of legal reform that Gandalf will later harvest.
Source anchors: Rohan; Eastemnet; Éomer; Riders of Rohan; Hasufel; Arod; Pippin's brooch; Fangorn; Wormtongue; Saruman; Andúril; Shadowfax (reported).

Book Three, Chapter 3: The Uruk-hai

Summary: Merry and Pippin are carried by a quarrelling orc band split among Uglúk's Isengarders, Grishnákh's Mordor-orcs, and northerners who hate both commands. The captives listen to arguments about Saruman, the Great Eye, orders to bring hobbits alive, and the strange value their captors attach to them. Pippin frees his wrists with a dead guard's knife, drops his Lothlórien brooch as a sign, and the Rohirrim attack by night, allowing the hobbits to crawl away toward Fangorn after Grishnákh tries to seize them for himself and is ridden down.
Analysis: Merry and Pippin survive because the orc alliance is internally rotten: Uglúk and Grishnákh serve different masters, the Mordor-orcs distrust Saruman's orders, and the northerners detest both, an anatomy of Empire and Periphery at the level of a penal convoy where two imperial systems hold barely compatible contracts over the same captives. The chapter is also a lesson in the fragility of command: orders pass between Saruman and the Eye, a winged Nazgûl is promised on the east bank, and the quarrel over "the Ring" versus "something wanted for the War" is itself an intelligence leak rehearsed before the prisoners (Information and Coordination). Pippin's cut wrist-cords, the dropped brooch, and the lembas rationed in the dark show the hobbits converting War, Crisis, and Collapse into small, disciplined improvisation; Fangorn is not a planned refuge but the opening created when orc discipline, Rohirrim violence, and hobbit patience intersect.
Source anchors: Merry; Pippin; Uglúk; Grishnákh; Uruk-hai; Mordor-orcs; Lugbúrz; Nazgûl; Lothlórien brooch; lembas; Fangorn.

Book Three, Chapter 4: Treebeard

Summary: Merry and Pippin enter Fangorn and meet Treebeard, who identifies them as hobbits, carries them to Wellinghall, and gives them Ent-draughts. Treebeard speaks of Ents, Entwives, old forests, Saruman's tree-felling, Isengard's smoke, and his own slow anger at the damage done by axes and fire. After hearing the hobbits' news, he summons the Entmoot at Derndingle; the Ents, having argued for three days, decide to march on Isengard with a speed that surprises even Merry and Pippin, singing "We go to war" as the forest behind them begins to move.
Analysis: Treebeard turns Fangorn from feared terrain into a political actor with memory, grief, and delayed rage, and the chapter thereby introduces Ecological Constraint as a species of statecraft: Saruman's wanton felling converts industrial damage into collective grievance stronger than any promise. The Ent-draughts, the naming lists, and the lost Entwives make the forest a living archive, so the Ents embody Knowledge Preservation — memory literally walking — even as their numbers dwindle. The Entmoot creates Polycentric Order because Ents deliberate outside Rohan, Gondor, and wizard command, then enter the war through their own authority after industrial destruction converts local ecological injury into collective action. "The last march of the Ents" reframes War, Crisis, and Collapse as a decision to spend one's own doom in a song worth singing rather than wait for it to arrive.
Source anchors: Treebeard; Fangorn; Wellinghall; Ent-draughts; Entwives; Entmoot; Derndingle; Bregalad (Quickbeam); Isengard; Methedras.

Book Three, Chapter 5: The White Rider

Summary: Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli enter Fangorn while tracking the hobbits and encounter an old man they first suspect may be Saruman — who had visited their camp the night before and loosed their horses. The figure reveals himself as Gandalf returned, now the White Rider, and recounts his battle with the Balrog from Moria through fire, water, stone, and Zirakzigil, his rescue by Gwaihir, and his healing in Lothlórien. Gandalf explains that Merry and Pippin are with Treebeard, brings messages from Galadriel (to Aragorn about the Grey Company, to Legolas to beware the Sea), summons Shadowfax, and redirects the three hunters toward Edoras and Théoden rather than deeper into Fangorn.
Analysis: The White Rider changes the meaning of Gandalf's fall by making his return a new commission rather than a simple rescue, and his report doubles as transmission of Knowledge Preservation: he carries messages, memories of the abyss, and the judgment of the west as cargo. The Balrog and Zirakzigil account gives the others a victory they could not see, while Treebeard's care of the hobbits frees Aragorn from his failed pursuit, and Galadriel and Shadowfax then pull the hunters toward Rohan — Gandalf reasserting himself as the principal coordinator of disparate fronts (Information and Coordination). His strategic read of the Enemy — "he that strikes the first blow," the Ring's destruction never entering Sauron's political imagination — frames War, Crisis, and Collapse as a contest of misread intentions, and his warning that "hope is not victory" keeps the repaired Company from mistaking reunion for safety.
Source anchors: White Rider; Gandalf; Balrog; Zirakzigil; Gwaihir; Galadriel's messages; Shadowfax; Hasufel; Arod; Treebeard; Theoden (destination).

Book Three, Chapter 6: The King of the Golden Hall

Summary: Gandalf, Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli ride to Edoras, where Meduseld stands under the shadow of Théoden's decline and Wormtongue's counsel. Háma makes them leave weapons at the door except for Gandalf's staff, and inside the hall Gandalf confronts Wormtongue, frees Théoden from despair, and calls him back to his own strength. Théoden takes up Herugrim, honors Éowyn, frees Éomer, rejects Wormtongue, and chooses to ride against Saruman while Éowyn remains to govern the people; he names Éomer his heir and gives Shadowfax to Gandalf.
Analysis: Edoras shows Saruman's power operating through Language and Ideology before armies arrive, with Wormtongue controlling the king's hearing, renaming Gandalf "Stormcrow," and converting paralysis into prudence — until Gandalf counters by literally re-lighting the hall and renaming Wormtongue back to "Gríma son of Gálmód." Herugrim's return restores Legitimacy and Succession by making Théoden's kingship bodily and public again, and the succession is formalized ("I name Éomer my sister-son to be my heir") before the muster. The mustering of riders, the ordering of refuges at Dunharrow, and the parsimonious command economy are pure State Capacity under Order and Governance; Éowyn's appointment exposes the limit of renewal because the hall still confines a capable dynastic heir to stewardship while men ride to war.
Source anchors: Edoras; Meduseld; Théoden; Wormtongue (Gríma); Herugrim; Éowyn; Éomer; Háma; Dunharrow; Shadowfax; Forth Eorlingas.

Book Three, Chapter 7: Helm's Deep

Summary: Théoden's riders move toward the Fords of Isen but divert to Helm's Deep when news of defeat at the Fords and Saruman's advancing army reaches them. At the Hornburg and Deeping Wall, the Rohirrim, Aragorn, Legolas, Gimli, Éomer, and Gamling withstand orcs, Dunlendings, ladders, blasting fire, and repeated assaults through the night; the culvert is blocked and re-blasted, and the gates fall. At dawn Théoden and Aragorn ride out to Helm's horn and the great charge, Gandalf arrives with Erkenbrand, and the strange forest of Huorns behind the enemy seals the destruction of Saruman's host.
Analysis: Helm's Deep proves that restored authority must survive material siege: the Deeping Wall and Hornburg give Rohan a defensive body, while civilians, horses, gates, culverts, and ammunition make Logistics and Throughput decisive inside the fortress — the culvert stopped, re-blasted, and turned into a pool is war decided by hydraulics. The Dunlending chorus in their own tongue and the blasting fire mark Saruman's alliance of grievance and industry driving War, Crisis, and Collapse onto Rohan's frontier, while the Hornburg's two choke-points (the Dike breach, the culvert) are textbook Chokepoints and Gateways under assault. Erkenbrand and the Huorns show Gandalf coordinating human reinforcement with Fangorn's independently mobilized force, so the chapter closes with Ecological Constraint overriding the military arithmetic of the siege: no orc crosses the wood, and the Death Down buries the mystery.
Source anchors: Helm's Deep; Hornburg; Deeping Wall; Théoden; Aragorn; Éomer; Gimli; Legolas; Erkenbrand; blasting fire; culvert; helm-horn; Huorns; Death Down.

Book Three, Chapter 8: The Road to Isengard

Summary: After Helm's Deep, Théoden, Gandalf, Aragorn, Legolas, Gimli, and the riders travel through the silent Huorn forest toward Isengard. They hear the Ents' explanation of the trees, Gimli rhapsodizes over the Glittering Caves of Aglarond, and the company passes the Fords of Isen where Gandalf has had the fallen buried and sent men to safety. At the gates they find the aftermath of the Ents' assault: broken walls, flooded pits, drowned machinery, torn stone, and Orthanc still standing amid ruin, with Merry and Pippin sitting like cheerful gatekeepers, smoking, eating, and guarding the road while Treebeard controls the conquered ring.
Analysis: Isengard's ruin lets Théoden see that Saruman's industrial fortress has already fallen to forces outside Rohan's command, an outcome explicable only through Ecological Constraint — Gandalf insists the trees are "no deed of mine" but "a power far older." The burial mounds, the clean rebirth of the Isen, and the "strange trees" that bury the slain make the chapter an exercise in how War, Crisis, and Collapse is memorialized: separate graves for Rohan's East Dales, Westfold, and Dunlendish dead, and a nameless hill no man will ever climb. Gimli's vision of Aglarond and Théoden's confession that the songs are "teaching them only to children, as a careless custom" place lore and craft under Knowledge Preservation, while the Huorns' night passage past the camp shows an entire forest acting with a coordination no command center observes. Merry and Pippin at Orthanc's gate reverse their apparent helplessness: the captured hobbits have become witnesses to Saruman's defeat.
Source anchors: Huorns; Ents; Fangorn; Fords of Isen; burial mounds; Death Down; Aglarond (Glittering Caves); Isengard; Orthanc; Merry; Pippin; Treebeard (reported).

Book Three, Chapter 9: Flotsam and Jetsam

Summary: Merry and Pippin tell Aragorn, Legolas, and Gimli how they escaped the orcs, met Treebeard, drank Ent-draughts, attended the Entmoot, and watched Isengard drown. They guide their friends through Saruman's wrecked stores, including food, salted pork, wine, and pipe-weed from the Southfarthing — Longbottom Leaf from the 1417 crop, which startles the travelers because it links Isengard to the Shire. The chapter fills in the Ent assault, the flooding of the pits, the trapped Wormtongue, and the strange comfort of hobbit talk amid the ruins.
Analysis: Merry and Pippin become historians of Isengard's fall, and the chapter's full oral reconstruction — orc-march, Grishnákh, Entmoot, storm, drowning — is Information and Coordination completing its circuit: the Company's separate witnesses finally converge into a single usable account. Southfarthing pipe-weed places the Shire inside Commodity Systems reaching Saruman's stores through unknown traders and intermediaries, and Aragorn's puzzlement ("Saruman had secret dealings with someone in the Shire, I guess") foreshadows penetration of the hobbits' home economy by forces that dominate through supply chains rather than furnaces — a clue at the scale of Empire and Periphery in reverse, the center importing from the periphery. The Ents' reported wrath, Quickbeam's chase of Saruman, and "put all the rats in one trap" reconstruct the fall as both legend and logistics, while the shared pipe and the meal keep Knowledge Preservation humble: memory survives as storytelling over food.
Source anchors: Merry; Pippin; Grishnákh; Entmoot; Quickbeam; Wormtongue; drowning of Isengard; Longbottom Leaf; pipe-weed; Southfarthing; Hornblower brandmarks.

Book Three, Chapter 10: The Voice of Saruman

Summary: Gandalf, Théoden, Aragorn, and the others confront Saruman at Orthanc, where his voice tries to charm, shame, divide, and bargain with the listeners — offering Théoden peace, flattering Gandalf, and hiding his defeat behind theatrical control while Wormtongue watches from above. Théoden rejects him in open speech, and Gandalf refuses to parley, breaks his staff, casts him from the order and the Council, and leaves the tower in Saruman's keeping; after Wormtongue throws down a hard black stone from high above, Pippin retrieves it and Gandalf takes it from him.
Analysis: Saruman's voice is his last functioning weapon — the extract describes its enchantment in an entire paragraph — and Orthanc gives him a stage even after Isengard has been ruined, making the confrontation the book's fullest demonstration of Language and Ideology as a power independent of material force. Théoden's resistance ("You are a liar, Saruman, and a corrupter of men's hearts") proves that his healing at Meduseld holds under renewed manipulation, while Gandalf's breaking of the staff removes Saruman's public authority in a public ceremony of Legitimacy and Succession — casting out the White, taking the office — as Aragorn, already "Aragorn son of Arathorn," waits as the order's future. The heavy stone cast from the window, Wormtongue's parting shot, delivers the book's key artifact: the confrontation shows that Order and Governance survives by refusing to bargain with a voice that converts every concession into submission, and that Saruman's defeat leaves dangerous instruments behind.
Source anchors: Saruman; Orthanc; Théoden; Gandalf; Aragorn; Wormtongue; breaking of the staff; palantír (Orthanc-stone); tree of Treebeard's footing.

Book Three, Chapter 11: The Palantír

Summary: Gandalf's company leaves Isengard, but Pippin is consumed by curiosity about the palantír and steals it from Gandalf while the camp sleeps. Looking into the stone, Pippin sees a dark tower ringed by winged shapes, then is questioned by Sauron himself and, in answering, accidentally misleads the Enemy into thinking the captive hobbit and the stone are in Orthanc's orbit. Gandalf interrogates Pippin, explains the palantíri, gives the stone to Aragorn as his by right, senses the movement of a Nazgûl overhead, and rides with Pippin on Shadowfax toward Minas Tirith while the others prepare for the road to war.
Analysis: Pippin's contact with the palantír gives Sauron a voice and a face but also corrupts his assumptions about Orthanc, so the theft becomes a demonstration that Intelligence is interpretive: the same stone delivers a true answer ("two hobbits taken... toward Isengard") into an Enemy framework that cannot read it, buying the West its deception. The history Gandalf unfolds — Noldor-made stones from Eldamar, the Gondor network, Saruman caught gazing on Barad-dûr — frames the palantíri as a fallen communication infrastructure, both Information and Coordination and its perversion. Aragorn's lawful claim ("the palantír of Orthanc from the treasury of Elendil... Now my hour draws near") moves Legitimacy and Succession toward open kingship, and the Nazgûl's flight over the camp — "Ride! Wait not for the dawn!" — makes Minas Tirith urgent. The "Rhymes of Lore" (seven stars and seven stones) keep the artifact's lineage inside Knowledge Preservation, and the throw of the stone thus converts a childish theft into the book's pivot toward Gondor.
Source anchors: Pippin; palantír (Orthanc-stone); Sauron; Orthanc; Aragorn; Gandalf; Nazgûl; Shadowfax; Minas Tirith; Rhymes of Lore.

Book Four, Chapter 1: The Taming of Smeagol

Summary: Frodo and Sam struggle through the Emyn Muil, unable to descend toward the marshes and Mordor until Gollum begins following them down the cliffs. Sam uses the Elven rope to lower them, the hobbits capture Gollum after a fall, and Frodo chooses pity over execution despite Sam's suspicion, recalling Gandalf's words on mercy. Gollum swears by the Precious to serve the master of the Ring, shifts between Smeagol and Gollum, and agrees to guide them out of the stony maze toward the Dead Marshes.
Analysis: The Emyn Muil makes Frodo and Sam's courage useless without guidance — sheer cliffs, blind gulleys, and a storm — which is why Gollum becomes necessary before he becomes trustworthy, grounding the chapter in Ecological Constraint as the geography that forces alliance. The capture itself runs on Informal Institutions: the oath sworn "on the Precious," Frodo's warning that "it will hold you... it may twist your words," and the leash-rope that Gollum experiences as burning all constitute a private legal regime improvised between captor and captive. Frodo's decision rests on pity learned from Gandalf — "Many that live deserve death... be not too eager to deal out death in the name of justice" — so the chapter tests whether mercy can be operational even in flight, an anticipation of the volume's moral climax in War, Crisis, and Collapse where the Ring itself becomes the third party in every bargain.
Source anchors: Emyn Muil; Frodo; Sam; Gollum (Sméagol); Elven rope; Sting; Precious; pity; Dead Marshes (destination).

Book Four, Chapter 2: The Passage of the Marshes

Summary: Gollum leads Frodo and Sam through the Dead Marshes, warning them not to follow the corpse-lights or look too closely at the dead faces beneath the water. The travelers endure stink, hunger, exhaustion, lembas, creeping fear, and the sight of a Nazgûl overhead while Gollum remembers ancient battlefields and avoids the open road. Frodo grows heavier under the Ring and senses the Eye; Sam watches both master and guide, even overhearing Gollum's internal debate; and the party moves toward the desolation before the Black Gate.
Analysis: The Dead Marshes turn ancient war into terrain, and the corpse-lights tempt travelers into joining the dead rather than merely seeing them, making the chapter a landscape essay on how War, Crisis, and Collapse is remembered — Elves and Men and Orcs "all dead, all rotten" beneath a creeping fen that swallows the graves. Gollum's usefulness is inseparable from his corruption: only he can thread the marsh, and only his recollections recover the great battle at the Black Gates, so Knowledge Preservation here is a broken archive carried by the damaged witness himself. The ring of the Nazgûl and the growing weight of the Ring show the hobbits moving inside Sauron's military geography, an Empire that patrols its own periphery (Empire and Periphery), while the land's barrenness — "neither spring nor summer would ever come again" — extends Ecological Constraint into deliberate deforestation of the possible.
Source anchors: Dead Marshes; corpse-lights; Gollum; Nazgûl; lembas; Ring; the Eye; Dagorlad; Noman-lands.

Book Four, Chapter 3: The Black Gate Is Closed

Summary: Frodo, Sam, and Gollum reach the Morannon and see the Black Gate, the Towers of the Teeth, roads, troops, and the impossible strength of Mordor's guarded entrance. Sam sees Easterlings and other armies moving into Sauron's land, while Frodo prepares to attempt entry because his command is to reach Mordor, not to survive. Gollum panics, begs them not to go through the Gate, and offers an alternative path by the stairs of Cirith Ungol through Ithilien, which Frodo accepts after a test of the frightened guide.
Analysis: The Morannon proves that obedience to a fixed plan can be suicidal: the Black Gate and Towers of the Teeth form Chokepoints and Gateways whose surveillance, fortifications, and troop flows make direct entry impossible, and Frodo's "I purpose to enter Mordor" collides with the plain arithmetic of the garrison. The marching armies from the East show the gateway feeding a wider imperial war, so the chapter expands State Capacity into Empire and Periphery: Gondor's old watch-towers, "built... lest he should seek to return," now garrisoned by the returned lord they were meant to exclude. Sam's rhymed "oliphaunt" interlude and the arrival of red-garbed Southrons keep the Eye's reach human, and Gollum's proposal of Cirith Ungol forces Frodo to trade the visible route for a guide's concealed knowledge — because access, not courage, now determines whether the quest can continue (Information and Coordination).
Source anchors: Morannon; Black Gate; Towers of the Teeth; Cirith Gorgor; Ephel Dúath; Ered Lithui; Easterlings; Sam's oliphaunt rhyme; Cirith Ungol (proposed).

Book Four, Chapter 4: Of Herbs and Stewed Rabbit

Summary: In Ithilien, Frodo and Sam experience herbs, trees, water, and concealment after the barrenness before Mordor, while Gollum catches fish and resents hobbit cooking. Sam asks Gollum for rabbits, lights a fire, and makes a small stew with herbs; the smoke unintentionally reveals them to Faramir's Rangers. Mablung and Damrod ambush Southrons on the road, Sam sees an oliphaunt (Mûmak) for the first time, and the hobbits are taken into the power of Gondorian soldiers who do not yet know what Frodo carries.
Analysis: Ithilien briefly restores sensory life to Frodo and Sam, which makes stewed rabbit more than comic relief: it is a Shire habit kept alive beside Mordor, and the "Po-ta-toes" exchange marks Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy as the substance ordinary identity is made of even at the edge of domination. The land itself — a "dishevelled dryad loveliness" being re-wildened after years of enemy rule — shows Ecological Constraint at the frontier: spring still works where the shadow has not yet set. Gollum's disgust at cooking and fire marks his separation from ordinary fellowship, while Faramir's Rangers convert the meal's smoke into military exposure; the burned ring of bones Sam finds, the ambush, and the Mûmak with its war-tower place the hobbits inside Gondor's war with Harad (War, Crisis, and Collapse) before they understand Faramir's character.
Source anchors: Ithilien; stewed rabbit; herbs; Gollum; Faramir's Rangers; Mablung; Damrod; oliphaunt (Mûmak); Haradrim; charred bones.

Book Four, Chapter 5: The Window on the West

Summary: Faramir brings Frodo and Sam blindfolded to Henneth Annûn, the hidden refuge behind the waterfall, and questions them about their errand, Boromir, Elrond's council, and the broken Fellowship. Sam reveals more than he intends about Boromir and the Ring, but Faramir — who has already dreamed of his brother's funeral boat and has learned lore from the treasuries of Gondor — refuses the temptation to seize a weapon of the Enemy even for Gondor. He declares Frodo free within the realm, feeds and shelters the hobbits, hears Frodo's confession of the quest to Mount Doom, and vows never to touch the Thing.
Analysis: Faramir tests Frodo through interrogation rather than force, and Henneth Annûn combines discipline, secrecy, and hospitality — an outpost of Order and Governance whose law ("death... to all found in this land") is applied with conscious mercy. Boromir's memory makes the Ring a family and state temptation rather than a simple moral emblem; Faramir's account of the Steward's house, the treasuries, and Gandalf's questioning grounds his restraint in Knowledge Preservation — "much is still preserved of ancient lore among the Rulers of the city." Sam's loose speech creates the chapter's Moral Economy test: Faramir could seize a weapon for Gondor's survival but refuses the claim because hospitality, oath, and restraint impose obligations stronger than strategic advantage ("Not were Minas Tirith falling in ruin and I alone could save her"). The chapter also advances Legitimacy and Succession — Aragorn's claim is argued for and doubted before his own City — and shows State Capacity in Gondor's failing but still-functioning periphery of Rangers.
Source anchors: Faramir; Henneth Annûn; Boromir's funeral boat; the cleaved horn; Mablung; Damrod; Isildur's Bane; Ring; treasuries of Gondor; Steward's line; Mount Doom (confessed).

Book Four, Chapter 6: The Forbidden Pool

Summary: At Henneth Annûn, Faramir shows Frodo that Gollum is fishing in the forbidden pool below the refuge, an act punishable by death under the laws of the Rangers. Frodo must coax Smeagol away from the water while Anborn and the archers wait, and Gollum experiences the rescue as betrayal when he is seized and bound. Faramir questions Gollum, learns the way to Cirith Ungol is a secret way, pronounces a doom of death on Gollum while he walks with Frodo, warns Frodo, and finally allows the hobbits and their guide to leave with provisions.
Analysis: The forbidden pool forces Frodo to spend trust he barely has: saving Gollum requires deceiving him in front of Faramir's archers, so mercy and trickery become the same act — a lesson that Informal Institutions (oaths, protection, safe-conduct) can be upheld only through performances that damage the very trust they maintain. Faramir's juridical theater — the doom declared, the master taking the servant "under the shield of Gondor" — is Order and Governance doing its visible work, applying law to a creature outside every law. Gollum's sense of betrayal matters because it damages the Smeagol side Frodo has been trying to preserve, and Faramir's warning — "beware of this guide... he has done murder before now" — plus his naming of Cirith Ungol, make the chapter's mercy uneasy: he holds the moral high ground while knowing enough to fear the road but not enough to replace it, an ethics under Moral Economy where the rescuer may be financing the betrayal.
Source anchors: forbidden pool; Henneth Annûn; Faramir; Frodo; Sméagol; Anborn; archers; doom of death; Cirith Ungol (named); Minas Morgul (named).

Book Four, Chapter 7: Journey to the Cross-roads

Summary: Frodo, Sam, and Gollum leave Faramir and move through Ithilien toward the Morgul Vale, watching the land darken as they near Mordor. Faramir's food and counsel carry them for a while, but the guide grows secretive and the burden of the Ring increases; a rumbling like distant drums grows in the earth. At the Cross-roads they find the fallen king's statue defaced by servants of Mordor, but its severed head lies crowned with a trailing plant of white flowers, giving Frodo and Sam a brief sign that Sauron's mockery cannot wholly erase older kingdoms.
Analysis: Ithilien's fading beauty makes the approach to Morgul Vale feel like a crossing from contested land into poisoned dominion, and the track of the polluted Morgulduin extends Ecological Constraint into deliberate ruination of water and earth. Faramir remains present through supplies and warning, while Gollum's secrecy and the Ring's weight restore the pressure of the guide's unresolved betrayal, and the subterranean rumble announces War, Crisis, and Collapse as the coming storm breaks over Gondor. The Cross-roads and fallen king give Frodo and Sam a political image of endurance: the head crowning with flowers — "They cannot conquer for ever!" — sets defacement, which is the imperial vandalism of Empire and Periphery, against the stubborn regrowth that anchors Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy; the brief glimpse is a shaft of meaning in a world going dark.
Source anchors: Ithilien; Faramir (absent, through gifts); Gollum; Ring; Morgul Vale; Morgulduin; Cross-roads; fallen king's statue; crown of flowers.

Book Four, Chapter 8: The Stairs of Cirith Ungol

Summary: Frodo, Sam, and Gollum watch Minas Morgul open and the Witch-king lead a great army out toward war, leaving the road momentarily possible. They climb the Straight Stair and Winding Stair of Cirith Ungol, exhausted by height, darkness, thirst, and the pull of the Ring, and they glimpse the orc-tower above the pass. While Frodo and Sam sleep, Gollum nearly repents as he sees them together, but Sam wakes sharply, the moment breaks, and Gollum leads them onward toward the tunnel and Shelob.
Analysis: Minas Morgul and the Witch-king show that Frodo is passing behind an army moving west, so his hidden climb is synchronized with the open war of Book Three — two theaters of War, Crisis, and Collapse running on the same clock. The passage reads as a study in Chokepoints and Gateways: the bridge, the shut gates, the Straight Stair and Winding Stair all exact a toll of will before any monster appears, and the orc-tower's red eye marks the frontier's policing by a garrisoned State Capacity that watches even its own secret ways. Sam's meditation on the great tales and the phial — "we're in the same tale still" — keeps Knowledge Preservation alive as a metaphor at the threshold of the dark, while Gollum's broken moment beside the sleeping hobbits matters because Shelob becomes more than a trap: it is the path he returns to after a last failed chance at mercy, a choice the Eye silently studies (Intelligence as the pressure that foreclosed his better self).
Source anchors: Minas Morgul; Witch-king; Ringwraiths; Straight Stair; Winding Stair; Cirith Ungol; orc-tower; phial of Galadriel; Gollum's moment of reprieve; the great tales.

Book Four, Chapter 9: Shelob's Lair

Summary: Gollum leads Frodo and Sam into Shelob's tunnel, where darkness, stench, webs, and hidden passages separate them from ordinary courage. Frodo uses Galadriel's phial and Sting to cut through the web and drive back the horror for a time, but Gollum attacks Sam outside while Shelob comes between Sam and Frodo. Sam fights Gollum off too late to prevent Shelob from stinging Frodo, then takes up Sting and the phial and wounds Shelob in a desperate combat, driving her back into her lair.
Analysis: Shelob is the endpoint of Gollum's betrayal and the book's supreme figure of Ecological Constraint: a hunger older than Sauron, "who was there before Sauron, and before the first stone of Barad-dûr," a power that serves no master and converts the war's machineries into feeding etiquette. Galadriel's phial and Sting bring Lothlórien and Bilbo's gift into the darkest passage, letting Sam wield inherited light and blade when Frodo falls — Knowledge Preservation as a carried remedy rather than a stored record. The webs make separation physical, and Sam's fight with the monster (and the killing-sorrowful fight with Gollum) is War, Crisis, and Collapse compressed into one body: he who was the follower kills, wounds, and endures without any commander left to instruct him, and the chapter ends with him thinking his master dead — the book's darkest misreading.
Source anchors: Shelob; Torech Ungol; Gollum; Galadriel's phial; Sting; webs; Sam; Frodo (stung); Ungoliant (heritage); Beren; Elbereth.

Book Four, Chapter 10: The Choices of Master Samwise

Summary: Sam believes Frodo is dead after Shelob's sting, fights through grief, and decides to take the Ring because the quest must not end in the tunnel. He puts on the Ring, hears orcs approaching, and hides as Shagrat and Gorbag argue over Frodo's body, orders from Lugbúrz and Minas Morgul, and the valuables they might claim. Sam learns from their talk that Frodo is alive — "this fellow isn't dead" — and sees the orcs carry him toward the Tower of Cirith Ungol, leaving Sam outside with the Ring, Sting, Galadriel's phial, and the knowledge that his first decision was wrong.
Analysis: Sam's choice about the Ring is heroic because it is made without counsel — "the Council gave him companions... you are the last of all the Company" — and his internal debate is a solo rehearsal of Moral Economy: who may rightfully inherit an errand, and what a servant owes a living master. But Shagrat and Gorbag's overheard gossip reveals that heroism has rested on a false diagnosis of Frodo's condition, converting the chapter into a case study in raw Information and Coordination: Sam's whole decision tree collapses because his terminal fact was wrong, and even the Ring's gift of tongues cannot compensate for the missing datum that his master is merely paralyzed, not dead. The Tower of Cirith Ungol replaces Shelob's tunnel as the immediate obstacle, the orc captains' petty scheming shows the periphery of an empire running on laziness and graft, and Galadriel's phial remains Sam's one visible sign that help from outside is still with him. Frodo's survival turns the ending from tragic completion into an urgent rescue problem — but also advances War, Crisis, and Collapse into its most intimate register: the quest continues because one hobbit chose, on incomplete knowledge, to carry the Ring.
Source anchors: Sam; Ring; Shagrat; Gorbag; Lugbúrz; Minas Morgul; Tower of Cirith Ungol; Galadriel's phial; Sting; 'he isn't dead'; Samwise's internal debate.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • "Not idly do the leaves of Lórien fall" — Pippin's deliberately dropped brooch is Book Three's first intelligence cryptogram; Aragorn uses it to prove the orc-host was not the plain slaughter Éomer reported.
  • The "S" badge — the small white hand on a black field and the S-rune identify Saruman's Uruk-hai before anyone in the Company has met Saruman; "S is for Sauron... Nay, Sauron does not use white."
  • Merry's scarfacing — Uglúk's orc-draught and salve heal Merry's forehead wound, leaving a brown scar he bears "to the end of his days," and it is orc-work that keeps a fighting-fit trophy alive.
  • Ent-draughts change hobbits: Merry and Pippin emerge from Fangorn taller, thicker-haired, and growing — the physical proof that they "drank of the waters of the Ents."
  • "We go, we go, we go to war" — the Ents' marching song, and Treebeard's "the last march of the Ents" distills their political decision: better to spend one's doom in a song than wait for it.
  • Saruman's mask slips: at the height of the Wizard's Vale fight, the White Hand pillar's "nails were red," and the ents break it down entirely afterward — the sign of lordship physically unmade.
  • Longbottom Leaf in Isengard: barrels brandmarked Hornblower, from the Southfarthing's 1417 crop, anchor Saruman's supply chain back to the Shire — a mystery Aragorn files away for later.
  • Whispered geostrategy: Gandalf's read of Sauron — the Enemy cannot conceive of destroying the Ring, only of wielding it — is the strategic core of the whole series and a perfect aphorism for misread intelligence.
  • "I am Gandalf the White... You have no colour now" — the formal casting-out of Saruman, and the broken staff as the visible execution of a legitimacy judgment.
  • The palantír's rhyme: "Seven stars and seven stones / And one white tree" — the Rhymes of Lore that decode the Surveillance network of Gondor, and Saruman's fatal peering into Barad-dûr.
  • Pippin's stone-vision: "nine... bats wheeling round the tower," then Sauron's question and the command "Tell Saruman that this dainty is not for him" — one of the book's few direct encounters with the Enemy's voice.
  • Henneth Annûn: "the Window of the Sunset," the waterfall refuge whose name means the hidden landing that no stranger may see unbidden — and whose water costs death.
  • Faramir's counterfactual: "Not were Minas Tirith falling in ruin and I alone could save her, so, using the weapon of the Dark Lord for her good and my glory" — the strongest statement of the book's ethic of restraint.
  • Boromir's cleaved horn: returned in two pieces to Denethor's lap, the physical proof of the elder son's fall and the cause of Faramir's dream-vision of the funeral boat.
  • The Cross-roads king: headless statue with a "coronal of silver and gold" of white flowers — "They cannot conquer for ever!" — the book's single image of endurance against imperial vandalism.
  • "The great tales never end": Sam's fireside theory of narrative at the threshold of Cirith Ungol, capped by "your Bert" — actually "years and years afterwards... Let's hear about Frodo and the Ring!"
  • Sam hearing double: the Ring "gave understanding of tongues," letting Sam overhear Shagrat and Gorbag's quarrel — and the pivotal line "This fellow isn't dead."
  • The Gorbag/Shagrat debate: two middle managers of Mordor desiring to "slip off and set up somewhere on our own," showing an empire run on personal greed and unreliability beneath the Eye's perfect command.
  • Shelob's pedigree: "last child of Ungoliant," older than Sauron and Barad-dûr, served by no one and feeding on the orcs Sauron feeds her — a natural counter-power embedded in the enemy's own infrastructure.
  • The Witch-king's army: "So great an army had never issued from that vale since the days of Isildur's might" — the open war that synchronizes the two towers' storylines.
  • Who ends the book: Sam, alone outside the Tower of Cirith Ungol, carrying the Ring, with Frodo alive and carried off — the correction that turns the quest from completion into rescue.

Extract note

The extract contains a duplicated passage inside Book Four, Chapter 3 (The Black Gate Is Closed) — the dialog between Gollum and the hobbits about the "other way" repeats verbatim across several lines before the text resumes at Cirith Ungol's naming. It is an artifact of text extraction rather than divergent content, and the chapter's events are unambiguous.
