The confusion

Buy on Amazon — The confusion

Position in the vault

This is the second volume of The Baroque Cycle, the three-volume
historical sequence that begins with Quicksilver (which follows Daniel Waterhouse, Jack
Shaftoe, and Eliza through the Scientific Revolution, the death of Charles II, and the
Glorious Revolution) and continues into The system of the world (which completes the
story with Newton at the Mint and the formation of the modern fiscal-military state).
The Confusion is the middle volume, and it is the hinge of the Cycle: it converts the
science-and-revolution preoccupations of Quicksilver into an empire-scale story of
silver, credit, war, and redemption. It sits beside its two siblings in the same series
subfolder, belongs to the vault's crosslinked book layer, and feeds the MoCs on maritime
empires, finance and industrialization, and state/order. Its deep thematic links run to
Commercial Society, Financial Infrastructure,
Maritime Power, Coercive Labor,
Empire and Periphery, Intelligence,
and State Capacity.

Detailed overview

Neal Stephenson opens the volume by warning that it contains, running concurrently from
1689 to 1702, two novels-or-books that the text labels Bonanza and The Juncto. The
title's pun on being "con-fused" is not ornamental: the book is built by cutting between
Jack Shaftoe's global silver-and-slave adventure and Eliza's European financial and
diplomatic maneuvers so that treasure, credit, naval war, dynastic succession, and
personal revenge mature on the same calendar. The result is a middle volume that makes the
Baroque Cycle's two engines, bullion movement and state finance, visibly drive each other.
The extract interleaves the two threads chapter by chapter: a Bonanza scene (Jack, the
Cabal of galley-slaves) is followed by a The Juncto scene (Eliza, her letters and courts),
and the two often echo one another thematically even when the characters never meet.

Bonanza follows Jack Shaftoe, Dappa, Moseh de la Cruz, Vrej Esphahnian, Jeronimo, and
the other oar-slaves of Algiers into a scheme to intercept the corrupt Spanish ex-Viceroy's
treasure before it reaches Bonanza on the Guadalquivir. The plan, worked out on the roof of
the banyolar by Moseh, is a venture-capital contract: the Pasha of Algiers, the French duc
d'Arcachon (the "Investor"), Ottoman harbour-masters, and the freed men will all divide the
haul. What they seize is not the expected silver pigs but gold — the legendary, alchemically
charged "Gold of Solomon" for which bankers and the Esoteric Brotherhood will hunt them for
years. Their flight runs from the Gulf of Cadiz, Malta, Cairo, and the Red Sea eastward to
Surat, Malabar, Japan, and across the Pacific to New Spain, with the gold dispersed, buried,
traded for quicksilver, and finally sheathing the hull of their teak-built ship Minerva.
Jack's names mutate through Ali Zaybak, L'Emmerdeur, and King of the Vagabonds, and in
Hindoostan he is made a king for three years by the Great Mogul. The half is ultimately lost
to betrayal at Qwghlm, yet Jack is handed his freedom by the King of France and sent back to
London as "Jack the Coiner" to undo the new English state's financial heart.

The Juncto follows Eliza from the Glorious Revolution through the courts and credit
systems of France, England, the Dutch Republic, Saxony, Hanover, and Prussia. Stripped by
Jean Bart's privateers, she rebuilds influence through Lyon's Dépôt, bills of exchange,
Amsterdam-payable paper, Geneva bullion, Samuel Bernard's lending, and Pontchartrain's
treasury. She helps arrange (and, privately, sabotage) a French invasion of England,
runs enormous commercial and speculative schemes, and destroys the House of Hacklheber by
turning the banker Lothar's own greed against him. In parallel the English Juncto — Roger
Comstock, the Bank of England, and the recoinage under a newly-recruited Isaac Newton —
is building its state credit. The two arcs converge when Jack is betrayed and imprisoned at
Qwghlm, and Eliza, now Duchess d'Arcachon, engineers both his ransom and her own cruel
renunciation of him before Louis XIV.

The book's central narrative logic is exchange under coercion. Jack's crew are literally
chained to oars, yet they learn to bargain with the Pasha, the French fleet, Cairo markets,
Indian merchants, Japanese shoguns, and Spanish mine-heads. Eliza appears freer, but she is
pressed by Louis XIV's war machine, William III's needs, French ministries, court rumor,
hostage-like family ties, and the predatory Arcachon network. Both halves ask who can
convert captivity into leverage without losing the self that made leverage possible. As the
second Baroque Cycle volume, The Confusion also prepares the state-credit world of
The system of the world: Newton is recruited to run the Mint, the Bank of England is
founded, and England's recoinage is framed as the nation's own "great work." The volume
takes Quicksilver's concern with measurement and applies it to empire-scale trust —
showing that early modern power depends on making strangers accept paper, numbers, seals,
and promises as if they were silver in hand.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Jack Shaftoe (Half-Cocked Jack, Ali Zaybak, L'Emmerdeur, King of the Vagabonds):

the perpetual engine of Bonanza. Rows, schemes, sells, and fights across two
hemispheres to convert freedom into wealth, and eventually to reach England. His secret
motive is a thirteen-year-old promise on Eliza's behalf: to kill the man who enslaved
her — the duc d'Arcachon. Comic, cunning, and increasingly weary, he embodies the
Vagabond who turns imperial bullion flows into mobility and power.

  • Eliza, Countess de la Zeur, then Duchess d'Arcachon and of Qwghlm: the constant

intelligence behind The Juncto. Her motives are survival and revenge (she vows to kill
the man who sold her and her mother into slavery), executed through finance, espionage,
maternity, and court intimacy. She builds the schemes — the Compagnie du Nord timber,
the invasion-pay bills, the destruction of Hacklheber — that convert knowledge of money
into leverage over men more powerful than herself.

  • Moseh de la Cruz (the Kohan with the Plan): the crypto-Jewish architect of the

Bonanza scheme whose long-range business sense ("synergistically leveraging the
value-added of diverse core competencies") turns a theft into a world-spanning trading
enterprise. He is the moral and logistical counterweight to Jack's improvidence.

  • Dappa: the exiled Niger-delta linguist and chronicler of slavery who becomes the

Cabal's interpreter and, at the end, the would-be historian of the African slave trade.
His testimony about the uses of freedom and the blood on the gold anchors the novel's
conscience.

  • Vrej Esphahnian: the Armenian trader who joins Jack out of honor and debt, survives

the Jesuit Intelligence Test in Hindoostan, and eventually betrays the Cabal out of a
long-cultivated Vendetta, only to kill himself at Versailles.

  • Bonaventure Rossignol: the King's cryptanalyst, Eliza's lover and the secret father

of Jean-Jacques. He decodes everyone's mail, loves numbers more than money, and both
entraps and protects Eliza.

  • Lothar von Hacklheber: the Saxon metals-banker whose Alchemical obsession and

bitterness drive him to kidnap Eliza's son and whose house Eliza ruins. He ends more
friend than enemy, adopting the boy he loves.

  • Jean Bart: the Dunkirk privateer, Eliza's ally and Jean-Jacques's godfather, who

carries the naval and prize-money strand of the French war.

  • Daniel Waterhouse: the Royal Society man who reappears to recruit Newton for the

Mint, carries the Juncto's messages across Europe, and serves as the link between the
scientific and the new financial world.

  • Isaac Newton: the alchemical savant, eventually Warden of the Mint, who embodies the

completion of the grand project — and is Jack's final "opponent."

  • Bob Shaftoe: Jack's soldier brother, whose Irish campaigns and pursuit of his

enslaved love Abigail Frome run in counterpoint to Jack's globetrotting.

  • The Ten (Cabal): Nyazi, Van Hoek, Jeronimo (El Desamparado), Gabriel Goto, Yevgeny,

Mr. Foot, Padraig Tallow, Surendranath, and the rest — the oar-mates who together are
worth more than apart.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Money as trust converted into metal, and back again (the "confusion" of gold, silver,

paper, and promises).

  • The conversion of captivity into leverage — unfree people forcing markets to serve them.
  • The world-economy as a single circulatory system whose "heartbeats" are Mexico City's

Mint and London's Mint.

  • The marriage of finance to intelligence, bodies, maternity, and violence.
  • Alchemy and the Enlightenment as rival ways of understanding value and matter.
  • The state learning to make strangers accept paper, numbers, seals, and promises.
  • Mercantilism vs. credit; the Whig vs. Tory financing of war; and dynastic succession,

with the Hanoverian succession looming over the whole.

Core concepts

  • Financial Infrastructure: The Dépôt of Lyon, the

bills of exchange, Hacklheber's banking, the Bank of England, and the recoinage. Eliza
demonstrates how paper claims transfer purchasing power and can be used to destroy a
rival bank, while the Juncto builds a national institution out of tax, lottery, and
annuities revenue.

  • Commodity Systems: silver, pieces of eight, Indian

textiles, coffee, quicksilver, wootz, slaves, ships, and ransoms form the material
circuit binding Bonanza to The Juncto. The gold of Bonanza travels the world and is
finally melted onto the hull of Minerva.

  • Coercive Labor: Galley slavery, Barbary captivity,

Indian caste slavery, Jamaican refuse-slaves, and Abigail's legal enslavement make
unfreedom a central engine of exchange — and give Dappa's book its urgency.

  • Maritime Power: privateering (Bart), the Manila Galleon,

Minerva, and the navies of the nine years' war and the War of the Spanish Succession.

  • Empire and Periphery: Spain's silver empire, the

Mogul's Hindoostan, the closed Japanese shogunate, Qwghlm, and the Atlantic world as
linked peripheries of a commercial core.

  • Intelligence: Rossignol's cryptanalysis, the forged

vermilion-ink correspondence, and the letters by which Eliza both spies and speculates.

  • State Capacity: England's recoinage, France's treasury

and navy, and the provisioning of armies — the state learning to borrow against the
future.

  • Speculative Governance: lotteries, annuities,

bank paper, and the Jacobite invasion — the art of betting on opinion.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Granularity note: the extract is a single continuous narrative interleaving two strands —
Book 4: BONANZA (Jack Shaftoe and the Cabal) and Book 5: THE JUNCTO (Eliza and the
European courts). The chapter notes below follow the named scene-headings of the extract
as given in its contents, folding the many epistolary letter-divisions into their governing
scene. Run 1689–1702.

Barbary Coast (Bonanza; October 1689)

Summary: Jack awakens, "detonated" out of a fever-dream by the firing of fifteen hundred
guns, on a beach at Algiers as the city greets a new Pasha. He is a galley-slave
recovering from la suette anglaise, which Moseh de la Cruz claims has burned the French
Pox out of his body. Moseh explains Algiers's society of ocak, corsairs, and slaves, the
market in tutsaklar (ransom) futures that an "Invisible Hand" always grips, and the Cabal's
plan to win wealth and then freedom. Dappa recounts his enslavement under the Aro oracle,
and Jeronimo (El Desamparado) tells of his Mexican past and of the ex-Viceroy's brig loaded
with smuggling silver. Moseh's plan is to intercept that treasure. Source anchors: Algiers;
Moseh de la Cruz; the Pasha; Dappa; Jeronimo; the Plan; Bonanza.

Analysis: The opening establishes Algiers as a node where Barbary captivity, the ransom
economy, and the treasure-flows of New Spain meet. The men who must beg their plan from
the Pasha while lying face-down show that freedom can only be bought through an economic
argument. This is a lived Coercive Labor regime fused with a
Commodity System of silver, slaves, and ransom — the same
Financial Infrastructure of paper deeds and
speculation that operates, far grander and more "legitimate," in northern Europe.

Throne Room of the Pasha, the Kasba, Algiers (Bonanza; October 1689)

Summary: Before the Pasha, Moseh presents the plan in the elaborate protocol of the
"noble Floor" and the "sole of the Pasha's slipper." He explains the Guadalquivir sand-bar
that has forced the treasure-fleet to end at Cadiz rather than Bonanza, and proposes to use
a trading-galleot (stealth rather than might) crewed by disguised oar-slaves. Jack will be
their fake Janissary; he suggests that the French duc d'Arcachon must be "in on" the plan,
since his fleet commands the Bay of Biscay and he wants Jack as "Ali Zaybak." The Pasha
agrees, with a threat implicit in the man hanged on the hook outside. Source anchors:
the Pasha; Floor/slipper protocol; Guadalquivir sand-bar; Ali Zaybak; the duc d'Arcachon.

Analysis: The scene is a case study in how unfree and free powers negotiate under
constraint — a commercial-cum-military proposition pressed on a ruler through ritual and
bribery. The plan's economics (exchange the hog-cargo for "pigs of silver," take the profit
as ransom) show bullion moving east as the real motor of the Mediterranean and Ottoman
worlds. The duc d'Arcachon's interest in "Ali Zaybak" (Jack) imports the European
Intelligence state into the Corsair economy and sets the
central revenge-plot of Bonanza.

Dundalk, Ireland (The Juncto; 6 September 1689)

Summary: In a letter to Eliza, Sergeant Bob Shaftoe writes from the camp before Dundalk. He
reports the d'Avaux imprisonment and the Jacobite army in Dublin, the Partry out-laws
(Jack's sons Jimmy and Danny) siding with James, and his recovery of stolen French
diplomatic papers from d'Avaux's Dublin house — which he passes on to Eliza. He also
broaches his love for the enslaved Abigail Frome. Source anchors: Bob Shaftoe; Dundalk;
d'Avaux; the Partrys; stolen papers; Abigail Frome.

Analysis: The letter opens the Juncto strand by establishing the British-Irish war that
parallels Jack's maritime one, and by making Eliza's intelligence a family affair of
thieves and soldiers. Bob's aid to Eliza nets her damaging material on d'Avaux, converting
friendship into an intelligence asset — an early instance of Intelligence
as the currency of the Juncto world, and of Britain's colonial struggle over Ireland's
periphery.

Château Juvisy (The Juncto; 10 December 1689)

Summary: Eliza arrives exhausted at Rossignol's château at Juvisy with her infant
Jean-Jacques. He is cold, jealous, brilliant; she tells the long story of her capture by
Jean Bart's privateers, her confiscated fortune, and her lodging in the d'Ozoirs' Dunkerque
residence. Rossignol is revealed as a member of the Cabinet Noir who decoded Eliza's
steganographic correspondence with Leibniz, and he professes himself the father of
Jean-Jacques. Source anchors: Juvisy; Rossignol; Jean-Jacques; the Cabinet Noir;
steganography.

Analysis: Juvisy is a "perfect little kingdom of secrets" where Eliza's relationship to
Rossignol is a partnership of cryptanalysis and desire. The scene makes clear that Eliza's
survival depends on being valuable to the state-espionage apparatus — Intelligence
wielded at Court — and that her fertility and her letters are inseparable instruments
under constraint. Rossignol's power to decode what others think hidden gives him leverage
over d'Avaux, the King, and Eliza alike.

Dunkerque Residence of the d'Ozoirs (The Juncto; 13 December 1689)

Summary: Eliza tells the Marquis d'Ozoir of her failed timber-trading adventure down to
Lyon, the Dépôt's book-keeping, and the loss of the logs. D'Ozoir reveals that French ship
timber must come from the Baltic, that the Compagnie du Nord ran on Huguenot labor, and that
his father, the duc d'Arcachon, wants Eliza as an ally (a "new Colbert"). He persuades her
to restart Baltic timber supply for the French navy. Source anchors: the Marquis d'Ozoir;
timber; the Compagnie du Nord; the Baltic; Colbert.

Analysis: The scene introduces the material-logistics underside of naval power: ships need
timber, and timber comes from the Baltic through a network of Huguenot merchants and Dutch
bottoms. It shows State Capacity bent to the sea, and the
way noble families convert military procurement into extraction. Eliza is being recruited
as a "new Colbert," a commoner who makes finance work for a king — foreshadowing the
fiscal-military state.

Cap Gris-Nez, France (The Juncto; 15 December 1689)

Summary: Eliza travels with Jean Bart to Versailles, interviewing intendants about the
financing of new coastal fortifications (nobles exchanging hard money for court favor and
state paper). At La Dunette's party she hosts a masque (La Métamorphose) celebrating Jean
Bart's capture, and dances with Louis XIV, who mistakes a half-truth — that Eliza wants to
kill the man who wronged her — for a plan to assassinate William of Orange. Source anchors:
Cap Gris-Nez; Jean Bart; the masque; La Métamorphose; Louis XIV; William of Orange.

Analysis: The masque dramatizes how naval heroism is converted into royal favor and
resources — the Navy's claim to funding against the Army's. Eliza's dance with the King
shows the Court as a stage where [[02 Concepts/Speculative Governance|Speculative
Governance]] lives: reputation, gossip, and misread intentions decide access. The King's
misconstruction of her vow gives her a dangerous, convenient reputation as a would-be
regicide.

La Dunette (The Juncto; mid-July 1690)

Summary: At a party toasting French victories (Beachy Head, the Boyne), Eliza realizes that
the duc d'Arcachon — who smells of decay — is the man who sold her and her mother into
slavery, and recognizes him by the albino "Pasha" stallion for which she was once traded.
The Duchess of Oyonnax counsels her on patience, refinement, and how to kill a great man
without being suspected. Source anchors: La Dunette; Beachy Head; the Boyne; the duc
d'Arcachon; the albino horse; Oyonnax.

Analysis: The recognition of the duc d'Arcachon links Eliza's childhood trauma to the
highest Court, converting personal vengeance into a question of "refinement." Oyonnax's
machinations show that powerful women at Versailles pursue their ends through poison,
influence, and "balance of powers" rather than direct assault — an education in
Intelligence and Court power as feminine statecraft.

The Gulf of Cadiz (Bonanza; 5 August 1690)

Summary: The Cabal sails a trading-galleot into the Gulf of Cadiz, disguising themselves
as slavers and traders; Jack rejects the chance to escape, devoted to the Plan. They
collide with and overwhelm the ex-Viceroy's brig, freeing its chained crew and capturing
its cargo — which proves to be gold, not silver. The Investor's jacht Météore shadows them
as a threat. Source anchors: Gulf of Cadiz; the Plan; the brig; the gold; Météore;
van Hoek.

Analysis: The capture of the brig shows the Plan's finance working — the market valued the
slaves low singly but high together. The discovery of gold instead of expected silver
introduces the mystery that will drive the rest of the Cycle: this is the alchemically
charged Gold of Solomon. Dappa's "blood money" line links the hoard to the enslaved
Africans who mined it, and the Investor's shadowing makes the gold a contested prize of
war-finance — a hoard that is inseparable from [[02 Concepts/Coercive Labor|Coercive
Labor]] and the Financial Infrastructure that
chases it across oceans.

Off Malta (Bonanza; late August 1690)

Summary: The galleot sails past Malta, taking on oar-slaves from the French galleys. Jack
finds the Huguenot Arlanc, who warns that French/Irish/Dutch ships will attack — "if you
see Dutch colors, make for Africa." The convoy is attacked by the Dutch halfway to
Alexandria; Jack rescues Arlanc from a sinking galley. They proceed to Rosetta and Cairo,
seeking to avoid the Investor and his French fleet. Source anchors: Malta; Arlanc; the
Dutch attack; Rosetta; Cairo.

Analysis: The Malta and battle scenes dramatize the naval war's material horrors while
showing the Cabal's weakness as unowned, pirate-bait cargo-holders. The constant threat of
seizure — by Dutch, French, or Ottoman powers — makes the gold a liability as much as a
prize, and the decision to trade it in Cairo shows value as something realized only through
Commodity Systems. The war at sea is fought over the
movement of just such treasure.

The Gulf of Cadiz interlude / Cairo (Bonanza; autumn 1690)

Summary: At Cairo, after the Jack historically named Off Malta resolves, the Cabal
barters the gold in Khan el-Khalili. Jack, chosen to be their Janissary, confronts the duc
d'Arcachon in the caravanserai, refuses to hand over the gold, and — in a single stroke —
kills the man who enslaved Eliza, beheading the duc d'Arcachon. A running battle leaves the
Cabal escaping with most of the gold on river-boats toward the Red Sea. Source anchors:
Cairo; Khan el-Khalili; the duc d'Arcachon; the beheading; the escape to the Red Sea.

Analysis: Cairo's Khan el-Khalili is described as the ancient heart of the world-market
where silver has passed for millennia independent of kings — a pure
Commercial Society. Jack's killing of the Investor ties
the two halves of the book together: the act "was done for a woman, whose name I will not
say." The escape demonstrates that value must be liquidated and moved through the
Red Sea-trade to be preserved.

The Juncto meets Bonanza: Ireland, the Boyne, Aughrim (The Juncto; 1690–1691)

Summary: Bob Shaftoe's campaigns in Ireland provide the Juncto's land-war counterpoint.
At the Boyne, William survives a cannonball; Bob watches the battle, then a series of
sieges (Limerick, Athlone) and the great Battle of Aughrim. He pursues the Earl of Upnor
for Abigail Frome's sake, and is saved by the rapparee Teague Partry, who beats Upnor to
death. Bob farewells his nephews Jimmy and Danny Shaftoe, now soldiers. Source anchors:
Boyne; Aughrim; Limerick; Upnor; Teague Partry; Abigail Frome.

Analysis: The Irish chapters show land, conquest, religion, and colonial extraction inside
the same ornate state that runs on English credit. They also give Bob a parallel pursuit
to Jack's — the rescue of a woman from legal slavery — while emphasizing the brutality of
"no quarter" warfare and the rapparee insurgency that defeats formal armies. It anchors
Empire and Periphery within Britain itself.

A Hay-rick, St.-Malo / Château d'Arcachon (The Juncto; April 1692)

Summary: Bob Shaftoe meets Eliza at St.-Malo, where she presses him about the planned
French invasion and reveals her motives: she wants her abducted son Jean-Jacques (now
"Johann von Hacklheber") back, and intends to get her boot on Lothar's neck. At the château,
Eliza and Pontchartrain discuss English finances and devise, through a masque of Mercury
and the Bill of Exchange, how French invasion-pay silver can be transferred to London to
pay troops. Source anchors: St.-Malo; the invasion; Jean-Jacques/Johann; the Bill of
Exchange masque; Pontchartrain.

Analysis: The Bill-of-Exchange masque is the novel's most explicit tutorial on
Financial Infrastructure: Mercury (Eliza) shows
how a bill issued in Lyon, endorsed across courts, can move silver to London at no risk and
even be minted into English pennies. At the same time Eliza is arranging the invasion's
financial machinery — a scheme she will secretly sabotage. Finance here is a performative,
convertible instrument of war.

Café Esphahan, Versailles (The Juncto; 26 April 1692)

Summary: Eliza meets Samuel Bernard, the financier, in the Esphahnians' coffeehouse, where
they trade intelligence about the French Compagnie des Indes, the Dépôt, and the silver
transfer. Rossignol joins her and is revealed as the father of Jean-Jacques. Letters from
Daniel Waterhouse and Roger Comstock (Ravenscar) answer Eliza's inquiries about the Tower
Mint — Sir Thomas Neale is a cooperation-inducing gambler, and Ravenscar wagers that no
coins will ever be needed. Source anchors: Café Esphahan; Bernard; the Compagnie des Indes;
Rossignol; Ravenscar; the Mint.

Analysis: The Café Esphahan — run by Vrej's Armenians, watched by the Cabinet Noir — is a
microcosm of finance-in-waiting: gossip, coffee, and credit all circulate through it.
Ravenscar's letter introduces the English fiscal story (the Mint, the lotteries, the
Juncto's willingness to speculate) and frames the coming invasion-pay scheme as a contest
of credit and prediction — a speculative [[02 Concepts/Speculative Governance|Speculative
Governance]] duel between London and Paris.

Cabin of Météore, off Cherbourg (The Juncto; 2 June 1692)

Summary: Off Cherbourg, the combined Franco-English invasion of England is stopped at the
battle of Barfleur/La Hougue: Tourville's fleet is defeated, the invasion collapses, and
Eliza, her cover in place, escapes to England by a smuggler's boat (Arbalète) with Bob
Shaftoe after an English boarding-party seizes Météore. She carries the invasion-pay bills.
Source anchors: Battle of Barfleur; La Hougue; Cherbourg; Arbalète; Bob Shaftoe.

Analysis: The military failure of the invasion is, in Eliza's design, a financial success:
the bills she has had drawn are never presented, and the House of Hacklheber, which took
the risk, is left to lose. The scene is the clearest demonstration that finance, not
sea-power alone, decides the war — and that Eliza has engineered the French defeat while
being carried to England with the evidence in her pocket. It links
Maritime Power to the new state credit of the Juncto.

London (The Juncto; June 1692)

Summary: In London, Eliza presents the Hacklheber bills and, by making the City believe the
invasion has begun, forces the House of Hacklheber to buy silver at a ruinous price. When
the invasion fails to occur, she converts the whole transaction to Baltic timber through the
house of Durand, destroying Lothar's credit and turning the silver to her own profit.
Eliza parts from Bob Shaftoe; she speaks of her miscarried child and directs him toward
freedom rather than to her. Source anchors: London; the House of Hacklheber; 'Change Alley;
the conversion to timber; the parting with Bob.

Analysis: London's exchange becomes the arena where credit, rumor, and speculation decide
the value of silver and of entire houses. By making the market believe the invasion is
under way, Eliza compels Hacklheber to overextend; the failure then lets her dictate terms
and convert the whole into timber for France. This is [[02 Concepts/Financial Infrastructure|Financial
Infrastructure]] as active warfare — and an early, complete model of how a speculator
profits from an engineered swing in opinion.

Gresham's College (The Juncto; June 1692 / and onward)

Summary: At Gresham's, Eliza meets Isaac Newton, who explains his alchemical quest for the
Gold of Solomon and the Philosophic Mercury, and confronts the meaning of the dispersed
gold. Meanwhile Daniel Waterhouse negotiates the recruitment of Newton to run the Mint for
the Juncto's recoinage, enduring Newton's breakdown at Oates (his break with Fatio) and
accepting the commission that will let him go to Massachusetts. Source anchors: Gresham's;
Newton; Alchemy; the Gold of Solomon; the Mint; Oates; Fatio.

Analysis: The Newton scenes are the intellectual hinge of the whole Cycle: the alchemical
Great Work (recovering Solomon's gold) is reframed as an unrecoverable quest, and Daniel
redirects Newton's genius toward the coinage — the recoinage of England's silver as the
"Great Work" all the midnight lamps and habits have been preparing. This unites
State Capacity, alchemy, and science in the formation of the
modern state.

Mrs. Bligh's Coffee-house (The Juncto; September 1693)

Summary: Daniel Waterhouse and Roger Comstock discuss the want of money in England and the
proposal of the Bank of England and a national recoinage. Roger explains the Juncto's
scheme to make gold and silver appear "as if by alchemy," the lotteries, annuities, and the
need for a natural philosopher at the Mint. Source anchors: Mrs. Bligh's; the Juncto; the
Bank of England; the recoinage; Comstock.

Analysis: The coffee-house discussion is the clearest statement of the fiscal-military
revolution: England has no mines, so it will conjure wealth through commerce, credit, and
a central bank. The "recoinage" is explicitly framed as the nation's alchemical Great Work.
This converts the whole of The Confusion into an advertisement for the new
State Capacity that The system of the world will complete.

Bonanza continues: Ahmadabad, the Mogul Empire (Bonanza; September 1693)

Summary: Rejoining Jack after the gold's loss, the Cabal is scattered in Hindoostan. Jack,
in Ahmadabad, is tended by Surendranath, a Banyan trader, who explains the Mogul, Maratha,
and caste politics and the need to open the road from Surat to Delhi. Jack and Padraig's
situation as "bug-doctors" and their plan to make phosphorus yields a caravan-merchant's
alliance. Source anchors: Ahmadabad; Surendranath; the Moguls; Marathas; the Trade.

Analysis: Ahmadabad introduces the Mogul empire as a complex of political economy,
caste, and extortion that transplants the Cabal's survival instincts into an Asian
Commodity System. Surendranath's BATNA-talk shows the
Cabal members becoming businessmen as much as pirates — learning to convert
Commercial Society's rules from the inside.

The Surat-Broach Road, Hindoostan (Bonanza; October 1693)

Summary: The Cabal makes a plan for the Narmada crossing against the Marathas, using
phosphorus (made from urine) both as a night-weapon and as a currency of trade. At the
crossing they turn the Maratha army's elephants and infantry with the "cold fire," cutting
the Gordian knot and opening the road to Delhi, where Jack is rewarded with a three-year
kingship by the Great Mogul. Source anchors: Narmada; phosphorus; the Marathas; Delhi;
the jagir.

Analysis: The phosphorus battle is Jack's alchemical innovation: the same "Alchemy" that
baffled the Crusaders and drove Newton is here a practical weapon of trade and war.
The voyage shows how the Cabal's fortune and knowledge are converted into a territorial
asset (the jagir) and into the ship-building project to come — an alliance of chemistry,
commerce, and coercion within a Commercial Society's
expanding frontier.

Malabar (Bonanza; late 1696–early 1697)

Summary: The Cabal reaches the court of Queen Kottakkal, the Malabar Pirate-Queen, to whom
a quarter of the venture belongs. There they meet Moseh, Dappa, and Gabriel Goto (now the
Queen's gardener), learn of the wootz (watered-steel) industry of southern India, and
arrange the building of a teak ship — Minerva — to carry gold and quicksilver east and west.
Enoch Root arrives to broker the deal with an unnamed European backer (Sophie of Hanover).
Source anchors: Malabar; Kottakkal; wootz; Minerva; Gabriel Goto; Enoch Root.

Analysis: Malabar is a matrilineal, commercial pirate-polity that upends European
assumptions about kingship, gender, and property — a truly alien
Empire and Periphery. The wootz-vale is the source of
Damascus steel, tying the Cycle's material history (metallurgy, alchemy) to its commerce.
Enoch Root brokers a connection to Sophie of Hanover, making the venture part of the
Hanoverian financial network that will one day rule Britain.

The Juncto: Namur, Pretzsch, Leipzig, and the Track to Pretzsch (The Juncto; 1694–1696)

Summary: Bob Shaftoe's regiment winters near Namur; Colonel Barnes prepares the men for the
peace and helps Bob decide to reclaim Abigail. Eliza travels up the Elbe to Leipzig, meets
Lothar von Hacklheber (who has adopted Jean-Jacques now "Johann"), and endures smallpox at
the dower-house of Pretzsch while her scheme against the Elector of Saxony (whom she infects
with smallpox, killing him) matures. Leibniz and Daniel carry Princess Caroline toward
Berlin. Source anchors: Namur; Pretzsch; Leipzig; Lothar; Jean-Jacques/Johann; the Elector
of Saxony; smallpox; Caroline.

Analysis: These chapters in the Juncto strand show Eliza at her most ruthless — using
illness, sex, and dynastic politics as instruments against the men who threaten her child.
The contrast between her "boot on Lothar's neck" and her eventual, complicated settlement
with him (who loves the boy she cannot take) shows that revenge and maternal love pull her
in directions neither fully satisfies — a struggle within the dynastic order that
Intelligence and maternal surveillance govern. Leibniz's
tutoring of Caroline prefigures the Hanoverian succession and its
Commercial Society.

The Thames / Dunkirk (The Juncto; February–March 1696)

Summary: Daniel is sent on Météore to Dunkirk to return the jacht as a peace-offering to the
de Lavardacs. There he meets Eliza, learns that the plot to kill William failed, and that
Yevgeny (Flail-arm) has harpooned the Jesuit de Gex in the gardens of Versailles. Eliza
buys English banknotes on the strength of Newton's coming recoinage. Source anchors: the
Thames; Météore; Dunkirk; de Gex; Yevgeny; the Bank of England bills.

Analysis: The Undertow chapters gather the intelligence threads: the Jacobite assassination
plot, the return of Météore, and the financial speculation around England's recoinage.
Eliza's "derivative" reading of the two currencies (Land Bank vs. Bank of England) shows
her understanding of finance as the rate of change of wealth — an early, conceptual grasp
of the very Financial Infrastructure and
State Capacity the Juncto is creating.

An Abandoned Church in France / Black Torrent Guards near Namur (The Juncto; March 1696)

Summary: Édouard de Gex, thought dead, is revived by the Duchess of Oyonnax in a burnt
church as a Belgian Jansenist ("Edmund de Ath") and sent to the East to seek the Gold of
Solomon. Meanwhile Bob Shaftoe is informed his nephews have deserted, and that Abigail is
now within reach; Colonel Barnes resolves to steal her for him. Source anchors: Oyonnax;
Edmund de Ath/de Gex; the Black Torrent Guards; Abigail Frome.

Analysis: The Oyonnax/de Gex scene is a masterpiece of deception and doubles: the Jesuit
who hates Jansenists is reborn as one, to pursue the gold, while never knowing whether he
was resurrected by drugs or by Satan. At Namur, Barnes's decision to "steal" Abigail shows
the informal, personal counterpoint to the formal institutions of warfare — and sets the
emotional climax of Bob's strand within Coercive Labor's
chattel order.

A House Overlooking the Meuse Valley (The Juncto; April 1696)

Summary: Bob, with his regiment, rescues Abigail Frome from Count Sheerness's manor-house,
marrying her on the spot with Colonel Barnes officiating, and rides off with her.

Analysis: The rescue is the novel's clearest small victory for the Shaftoes' human
imperatives over the systems of enslavement and war — a romantic counterweight to Jack's
globe-spanning, ultimately darker quest. It shows coercion and legal property rights as the
underpinning of the very order the Cabal has been evading — and against which
Coercive Labor and [[02 Concepts/Empire and Periphery|Empire
and Periphery]] set the terms.
Source anchors: the Meuse; Sheerness's house; Abigail Frome; the wedding.

Herrenhausen Palace, Hanover (The Juncto; August 1697)

Summary: At Herrenhausen, the Electress Sophie condescends to meet Eliza, who has become a
double Duchess, and the savant Leibniz must entertain Tsar Peter the Great. The scene
weaves genealogies of the Hanoverian succession, the calculus controversy, and the
calculation (baschet to the throne), while Sophie signals she will be England's next Queen.
Source anchors: Herrenhausen; Sophie; Leibniz; Peter the Great; the succession.

Analysis: Herrenhausen is the dynastic and intellectual summit of Eliza's rise: she is now
a personage whom a future Queen of England treats as an equal, and her patronage (of
Hanover, of the ship Minerva) aligns her with the Protestant succession. The calculus and
money disputes are the same Currency confusion in intellectual form — the rival systems
(Newton's vs. Leibniz's) that The system of the world will resolve, resting on the
Financial Infrastructure of the age.

Berlin / Charlottenburg Palace (The Juncto; January–July 1701)

Summary: Leibniz, now president of the Berlin Academy, writes to Daniel about the monadology,
the collaborative "knowledge engine" of the Massachusetts Bay Institute, and the coming War
of the Spanish Succession. At Charlottenburg on Caroline's eighteenth birthday, the globe-
cage he gives her becomes a visual aid for choosing suitors, against the background of
Sophie Charlotte's court and the looming succession. Source anchors: Berlin; Leibniz; the
Berlin Academy; Caroline; Charlottenburg; the Cap.

Analysis: These chapters locate the intellectual project (the arithmetical engine, the
monadology) that will become The system of the world's culminating vision, and tie it to
the dynastic and fiscal contests of the century's turn. Caroline's globe-cage — with its
ships of quicksilver and fire — foreshadows Jack's Pacific voyage and the coming war,
suggesting that the "confusion" of the title is also a confusion of worlds about to be
reordered — an outlook that unites Intelligence,
Commercial Society, and the coming
Speculative Governance.

Japan (Bonanza; May 1700)

Summary: Minerva, laden with wootz, silk, pepper, and quicksilver, drops anchor illegally in
a Japanese cove to trade for quicksilver. The transaction nearly founders on a Japanese
death-trap (mercury tuned to the harbor's wave-period), foiled by Enoch Root. The crew
escapes, and Enoch departs for the Solomon Islands to pursue the legend of the Gold of
Solomon. Source anchors: Japan; quicksilver; Enoch Root; the Solomon Islands; the trap.

Analysis: The Japan chapter completes the "confusion" of commercial worlds: a closed,
punctilious shogunate insists on ruining the very merchants who sustain it, and only
Enoch's alchemical-physical insight foils the trap. Enoch's departure toward the Solomon
Islands — and the revelation that the ex-Viceroy's gold came from there — ties the Cycle's
mystery (Newton's Gold of Solomon) to myth and commerce — a progression of
Commodity Systems spanning the globe. The quicksilver for
gold swap is the ultimate "confusion" of the world's metal-money and its
Maritime Power.

The Pacific Ocean (Bonanza; late 1700–early 1701)

Summary: Minerva crosses the Pacific, chasing and eventually witnessing the destruction by
fire of the Manila Galleon (which, unbeknownst to the crew, carries a disguised de Gex and
Elizabeth de Obregon). Starving, they rescue two survivors, reach California, and sail down
to Acapulco, where they trade quicksilver and make their way overland toward Vera Cruz,
with Moseh heading north toward New Mexico. Source anchors: the Pacific; Manila Galleon;
Edmund de Ath (de Gex); Elizabeth de Obregon; Acapulco; the quicksilver trade.

Analysis: The Pacific crossing is the novel's most literal demonstration of how a
commercial venture risks everything on the movement of metal across an uncharted ocean —
an embodiment of Logistics and Throughput. The
surviving "Edmund de Ath" (really de Gex) becomes the Cabal's front-man, and Elizabeth de
Obregon's role as the sole survivor of the Solomon expedition plants the final clue to the
gold's origin.

Mexico City, New Spain / Mexico City (Bonanza; Sukkoth–December 1701)

Summary: In Mexico City, Jack and Moseh fall into the Inquisition's prison. They endure an
auto da fé and torture before being freed with sanbenitos (marking them as "blasphemers in
recovery"), while their sons liquidate the quicksilver and hide the proceeds. Moseh confesses
to having money, cashes out, and rides north to New Mexico. Jack, with the remaining gold
and silver, heads toward Vera Cruz to await Minerva — sending a letter he seals unread.
Source anchors: Mexico City; the Inquisition; the auto da fé; Moseh; New Mexico; the Mint.

Analysis: The Mexico City chapters are the novel's darkest meditation on
Coercive Labor, extractive empire, and the "confusion" of
money and belief: the Mint beats out pieces of eight "like a heartbeat" while the
Inquisition hunts heretics and Jews. Moseh's Euripidean turn toward New Mexico converts the
Cabal's venture-capital Plan into a diaspora, and the Mint's rhythm ("sixteen thousand
pieces of eight a day") becomes the pulse of the entire Atlantic world.

Qwghlm (Bonanza; August 1702)

Summary: Minerva, lured by the forged "Eliza" letter, runs aground on the "Dutch-hammer"
reef at Qwghlm in a French trap. Van Hoek severs his own hand in despair, Vrej betrays the
Cabal and escapes, and Jack is imprisoned. De Gex reveals himself; Étienne d'Arcachon, to
torment Jack, stages a grotesque spectacle behind one-way mirrors. But Minerva is freed —
her hull sheathed in gold — and Jack, ransomed, is taken by Louis XIV to be sent to London.
Source anchors: Qwghlm; the Dutch-hammer; de Gex; Étienne d'Arcachon; the gold-sheathed
hull; Louis XIV.

Analysis: The Qwghlm catastrophe completes the "confusion": Jack loses his fortune, his
friends are scattered, and the gold is finally understood as having been fused onto the
ship's hull itself — the ultimate, literal "confusion" of metal and vessel. The ghastly
peep-show staged by Étienne (whom Jack once emasculated and whose father he beheaded) is
the revenge-plot's climax, while Louis's ransom and dispatch of Jack to London set up the
final act against England's Mint — the far reach of [[02 Concepts/Maritime Power|Maritime
Power]] and Intelligence across a contested
Empire and Periphery.

Hôtel Arcachon (The Juncto; October 1702)

Summary: Louis XIV hands Jack his commission: go to London and render the English paper
money worthless, be it over years. In the ballroom, the three great scores of the book are
settled — Vrej (his revenge spent) instead shoots Étienne d'Arcachon before turning the
pistol on himself, and Eliza, to protect Jack from the Bastille, publicly swears she will
never see him again. Jack, freed, departs for London. Source anchors: Hôtel Arcachon;
Louis XIV; Vrej; Étienne; Eliza's renunciation; the commission.

Analysis: The Versailles denouement converts the whole personal history of revenge into a
geopolitical commission — the diminished, aging Jack is told to undo English state credit,
a task that mirrors the Financial Infrastructure
and State Capacity the Juncto has built. Eliza's cruel
renunciation is both a protection and a final severance of the central love-story, tying
the personal and the political "confusion" together as the Cycle heads toward its
conclusion.

En Route from Paris to London (Bonanza; October 1702)

Summary: Jack, brought up the Thames, watches the Mint at the Tower at work — Newton's
fires lit, guineas being coined — and, seeing Newton on the parapet, announces himself:
"Jack the Coiner has come back to London-town, and he aims to knock you down; the game has
begun." Source anchors: The Thames; the Tower Mint; Newton; Jack the Coiner.

Analysis: The closing scene frames the final volume: Jack's personal vendetta and the state's
fiscal machine are now one and the same target, and Newton — the alchemist turned Warden of
the Mint — is the emblem of the new State Capacity Jack has
been sent to destroy. London's "rebuilding" after the Fire is a metaphor for the
recoinage, and the whole Baroque Cycle turns on whether the Vagabond or the savant will
win control of the nation's money.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • "The Invisible Hand…grips us all by the testicles" — Moseh's simile for the ransom

market that decides a slave's fate (finances as coercive coercion).

  • The "noble Floor" and "sole of the slipper" protocol in the throneroom — ritualized

subaltern negotiation.

  • The Bill-of-Exchange masque at St.-Malo — the clearest worked example of moving silver

across Europe on paper alone.

  • Mexico City's Mint strikes "sixteen thousand pieces of eight" a day — the "heartbeat" of

the silver economy.

  • The wootz-vale of southern India — the source of "Damascus steel" and of the watered

Janissary blades; the gold of Bonanza's possible origin in the Islands of Solomon.

  • Minerva's hull sheathed in gold — the literal "confusion" of metal and ship.
  • The recurring name "Ali Zaybak" = "Quicksilver" for Jack — a title of volatility and

elusiveness that links him to the element mercury and to the gold-silver confusion.

  • "Jack the Coiner" — Jack's final alias, naming his mission against Newton's Mint.
  • Epigraphs from Defoe's "A Plan of the English Commerce" bracket the whole (also from

Hobbes, Milton, Bunyan) — framing the book as a commerce-and-power history as much as a
romance.
