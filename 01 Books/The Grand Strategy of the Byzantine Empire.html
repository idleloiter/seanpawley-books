The Grand Strategy of the Byzantine Empire

Place in the vault

This note lives under War, Revolution, and Civilizational Breakdown. Use it as a synthesis node for Byzantine survival, imperial statecraft, operational restraint, and the conversion of limited force into durable political security.

Detailed overview

Edward N. Luttwak's book argues that the eastern Roman empire survived because it invented a strategic culture unlike the earlier Roman reliance on decisive battle and overwhelming force. The empire called Byzantine by modern habit, but Roman by its own rulers and subjects, had to defend Constantinople, Anatolia, Syria, Egypt, the Balkans, the Danube frontier, the Caucasus, the Euphrates line, and maritime approaches with fewer resources than the old united empire. Luttwak therefore reads Byzantine history as a long experiment in using persuasion, intelligence, subsidies, religion, marriage, tactical manuals, maneuver, and selective force to generate more security than raw military strength could buy.

The first part begins with the administrative division of 395 between Honorius and Arkadios and asks why the western empire disappeared while the eastern empire endured until 1204 and, in reduced form, until 1453. Luttwak puts Attila, the Huns, the Sasanian Persians, the Danube steppe peoples, Justinian's reconquests, the plague, Constantinople's walls, and the empire's unfavorable geography into one comparison. The key contrast is not that the east had easier enemies; it had more dangerous ones, less strategic depth, and a long frontier, yet developed habits of deflection, bribery, alliance-making, intelligence, and containment that the western court could not match.

The diplomacy section is the book's most concrete explanation of how Byzantine statecraft worked without a modern foreign ministry. Envoys such as Zemarchos could cross the Black Sea, southern Russia, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, and Central Asia to reach Ishtemi of the Turk qaganate because Constantinople needed allies against Sasanian Persia. Luttwak then broadens the method to religion and statecraft, imperial prestige, dynastic marriages, geography, Bulghar and Bulgarian politics, and the Muslim Arab and Turkish challenge. Diplomacy is not treated as courtly decoration; it is a substitute for battles the empire could not afford.

The military section explains why Byzantine armies and navies were not merely defensive survivors. Luttwak treats Aeneas Tacticus, Vegetius, Frontinus, the Strategikon attributed to Maurikios, Leo VI's Taktika, the dromon, Greek fire, siegecraft, the De Velitatione, the Praecepta Militaria associated with Nikephoros Phokas, and the campaign literature of the tenth century as a working library for commanders. The point is that Byzantine forces enlarged their repertoire: mounted archery, ambushes, feigned retreats, patrols, reconnaissance, naval fire weapons, siege defenses, and operational maneuver all helped avoid attritional battles while preserving the army as the empire's scarce instrument.

The book culminates in a formal "operational code." Luttwak identifies a triple identity, Christian and specifically Chalcedonian, Hellenic in culture, and Roman as Romaioi, then argues that the Muslim conquest of Syria and Egypt removed deep Monophysite divisions while leaving a smaller but more cohesive empire. From there he states seven norms: avoid war while preparing for it, gather intelligence, campaign vigorously but prefer small actions, replace attrition with maneuver, recruit allies, use subversion, and fight relationally by exploiting enemy weaknesses. The conclusion makes the Byzantine achievement a strategic doctrine rather than a sequence of lucky survivals.

The appendix matters because it answers a methodological objection raised by Luttwak's earlier work on Roman grand strategy: whether premodern rulers without modern staffs, archives, maps, or social science could possess strategy at all. His answer is practical rather than bureaucratic. Byzantine strategy did not require a general staff in the modern sense; it required durable rules of conduct, transmitted through imperial habits, manuals, envoys, military culture, court ceremony, and accumulated precedent. That is why the book can move from Attila to Herakleios, from the Turk qaganate to the Bulgarians, and from Leo VI to the Fourth Crusade without treating each war as an isolated episode.

Section-by-section notes

Preface

Summary: Luttwak explains that the book grew from his earlier work on Roman grand strategy but became something larger when Byzantine sources revealed a richer strategic tradition than he expected. He names George T. Dennis, the Strategikon, Three Byzantine Military Treatises, Leo's Taktika, Walter E. Kaegi Jr., Peter B. Golden, John Wortley, Scylitzes, Eric McGeer, Paul Stephenson, Denis F. Sullivan, John F. Haldon, Dumbarton Oaks, Alice-Mary Talbot, Harvard University Press, and Michael Aronson as part of the scholarly infrastructure behind the book. The preface also makes clear that the audience includes non-specialists and that the subject is strategy from imperial statecraft down to tactics. Source anchors: George T. Dennis; Strategikon; Leo's Taktika; Peter B. Golden; Scylitzes; Dumbarton Oaks.

Analysis: George T. Dennis, the Strategikon, and Leo's Taktika matter because Luttwak is building the book from manuals that claim practical military use, not only from narrative chronicles. Peter B. Golden and Scylitzes show the range of evidence he needs: steppe peoples, Turkic terminology, and Byzantine historical narration all feed the same strategic inquiry. Dumbarton Oaks and Harvard University Press locate the project inside modern Byzantine studies, which lets Luttwak present himself as a strategic analyst dependent on specialist scholarship rather than as a solitary theorist imposing a model from outside.

Part I: The Invention of Byzantine Strategy

Summary: Part I opens with the division of the empire in 395 between Honorius in the west and Arkadios in the east, then contrasts the western collapse of 476 with the eastern survival from Constantinople. Luttwak names Romulus Augustus, Theodosius I, Diocletian, Moesia, Praevalitania, Macedonia, Bulgaria, Thrace, Anatolia, Syria, Egypt, Libya, the Sasanian empire, Carrhae, the Danube frontier, Huns, Avars, Onogur-Bulghars, Magyars, Pechenegs, Cumans, Attila, Charles d'Anjou, Michael VIII Palaiologos, Peter III of Aragon, and Giovanni da Procida. The part argues that the eastern empire learned to recruit allies, dissuade enemies, redirect invaders, and contain rather than annihilate opponents because it lacked the strength and geography that had protected earlier Rome. Source anchors: 395; Arkadios; Attila; Sasanian empire; Danube frontier; Michael VIII Palaiologos; Giovanni da Procida.

Analysis: The date 395 and Arkadios establish the institutional starting point: Luttwak is not describing a separate medieval state suddenly born after Rome, but the eastern continuation of Roman government under harsher conditions. Attila, the Sasanian empire, and the Danube frontier make the section a Collapse and Resilience anchor because the eastern empire survives pressures that destroy the west by changing how it absorbs threats. Michael VIII Palaiologos and Giovanni da Procida show continuity across centuries: the same preference for conspiracy, alliance, and indirect action that deflected Hunnic danger also helped block Charles d'Anjou in 1282.

Part II: Byzantine Diplomacy: The Myth and the Methods

Summary: Part II dismantles the myth of Byzantine diplomacy as mere intrigue by showing how envoys, religion, prestige, marriage, geography, and enemy management substituted for unavailable military abundance. Zemarchos travels for Justin II to Ishtemi, the Yabghu qagan of the Turk qaganate, crossing the Black Sea, southern Russia, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan, Sogdian territory, Samarkand, the Amu Darya, and the Altai or Tekes region to pursue an alliance against Sasanian Persia. Luttwak then treats imperial prestige, dynastic marriages, Bulghars and Bulgarians, Muslim Arabs and Turks, Constantinople's relics, conversion, gifts, subsidies, and the manipulation of frontier peoples as instruments of power. Source anchors: Zemarchos; Justin II; Ishtemi; Turk qaganate; Samarkand; Sasanian Persia; dynastic marriages.

Analysis: Zemarchos, Justin II, and Ishtemi make Byzantine diplomacy concrete: an envoy's journey is a strategic operation across the steppe, not a decorative embassy. The Turk qaganate and Sasanian Persia reveal Information and Coordination as imperial technique, because Constantinople tries to synchronize one distant power against another rather than meet Persia with Byzantine force alone. Samarkand, dynastic marriages, conversion, and subsidies show the range of tools: ceremony, kinship, religion, and money extend imperial reach where armies would be too slow, too expensive, or too vulnerable.

Part III: The Byzantine Art of War

Summary: Part III turns to the military culture preserved in handbooks and practice: the classical inheritance from Aeneas Tacticus, Vegetius, Frontinus, and Greek tactical writing; the Strategikon attributed to Maurikios; later texts after the Strategikon; Leo VI and naval warfare; the tenth-century military revival; and Herakleios's maneuver campaign against Persia. Luttwak discusses mounted archers, Hunnic and Turkic cavalry, illiterate merarchs, Latin command phrases, reconnaissance, ambushes, feigned retreats, the dromon, Greek fire, siegecraft manuals, De Velitatione, Praecepta Militaria, Nikephoros Phokas, Basil II, the Sasanian empire, Adur Gushnasp, and the year 626 siege context. Source anchors: Strategikon; Maurikios; Leo VI; Greek fire; De Velitatione; Nikephoros Phokas; Herakleios.

Analysis: The Strategikon and Maurikios are central because they make the Byzantine art of war readable as Knowledge Preservation: training, command language, enemy typologies, intelligence, and battlefield caution are codified so commanders can reuse hard-won practice. Leo VI, Greek fire, and the dromon extend that system to the sea, where Constantinople's survival depended on technology, crews, and naval doctrine as much as walls. De Velitatione, Nikephoros Phokas, and Herakleios show the mature form of Luttwak's claim: offensive skill exists, but its highest use is maneuver, raiding, ambush, and alliance rather than a Roman search for annihilating battle.

Conclusion: Grand Strategy and the Byzantine Operational Code

Summary: The conclusion identifies the psychological basis of Byzantine resilience as a triple identity: Chalcedonian Christianity, Hellenic culture, and Roman identity as Romaioi. Luttwak then explains how Syria, Egypt, Western Aramaic, Coptic, Monophysite Christianity, the Muslim conquest, Armenia, Slav-speakers, the Theodosian Wall, Herakleitos, the paradoxical logic of strategy, Belisarios, Alexander, Sasanian Persia, Muslim Arabs, Seljuk Turks, Ottoman Turks, Huns, Avars, Bulghars, Pechenegs, Magyars, and Cumans feed into a seven-part operational code. That code tells the empire to avoid war while preparing for it, gather intelligence, prefer small actions, replace attrition with maneuver, recruit allies, use subversion, and fight by exploiting enemy weaknesses. Source anchors: Chalcedonian; Romaioi; Monophysite; Herakleitos; Belisarios; operational code.

Analysis: Chalcedonian identity, Romaioi self-understanding, and Monophysite division explain why strategy is not only technique in this book; Religion and Legitimacy decide whether populations support the imperial center or experience it as alien rule. Herakleitos and Belisarios let Luttwak translate Byzantine practice into the paradoxical logic of war, where the obvious road, decisive victory, or total destruction can become dangerous. The operational code is the book's distilled mechanism: it turns intelligence, maneuver, allies, subversion, and limited battle into rules that explain how a poorer empire could keep surviving successive enemies.

Appendix: Was Strategy Feasible in Byzantine Times?

Summary: The appendix addresses whether it is legitimate to attribute strategy to premodern empires that lacked modern general staffs, archives, mapping practices, and bureaucratic planning systems. Luttwak links the question to his earlier The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire, mentions critics such as P. A. Brunt, Ernst Badian, Stephen L. Dyson, John C. Mann, A. D. Lee, and Information and Frontiers, and returns to the problem of Roman and Byzantine knowledge, roads, itineraries, intelligence, and strategic feasibility. The appendix defends the use of grand strategy as an observed pattern of conduct rather than as a formally written government plan. Source anchors: Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire; P. A. Brunt; Ernst Badian; A. D. Lee; Information and Frontiers; itineraries.

Analysis: The Grand Strategy of the Roman Empire and P. A. Brunt matter because the appendix is Luttwak's answer to an existing methodological fight, not an unrelated afterword. A. D. Lee, Information and Frontiers, and itineraries focus that fight on what ancient and Byzantine officials could actually know about routes, enemies, and frontiers. By treating strategy as repeated conduct visible in envoys, manuals, roads, subsidies, and military choices, the appendix turns Legibility into the methodological hinge: strategy is plausible only if imperial actors could see enough of the frontier world to make patterned choices.
