Axiomatic

Detailed overview

Greg Egan builds this collection by taking one speculative device at a time and then asking what it would do to identity, guilt, sex, death, punishment, and love once it stops being a laboratory curiosity and becomes part of ordinary decision-making. The stories are full of concrete mechanisms rather than decorative futurism: the drug S that lets dreamers slide among parallel selves, Hazzard Machines that receive one hundred twenty-eight bytes a day from a future diary, Ndoli jewels that replace organic brains, binary-search implants sold beside adolescent fashion labels, and a temporal wormhole called The Intake with a half-life of eighteen minutes. Egan does not stage these inventions as neutral gadgets. He places them inside workplaces, marriages, insurance contracts, art crimes, police investigations, biotech clinics, and rescue procedures, then watches people use them badly, rationalise them, and discover that technical precision does not rescue them from moral confusion.

The opening stretch of the book establishes that pattern in several different registers. “The Infinite Assassin” turns multiverse theory into a grim labour performed by a Company operative in the Leightown ghetto, where S users, whirlpool cults, and apartment 522 all become part of a problem of measure zero, Cantor dust, and personal survival. “The Hundred-Light-Year Diary” moves to Martin Place, Francis Chen’s time-reversed galaxies, and diaries delivered by Hazzard, Capaldi, and Wu, but it lands on a recognisable human problem: James can know that Alison will tap him on the shoulder at 1:27:14 and still fail to master jealousy, marriage, or the weekend he cannot identify with Lisa. “Eugene” and “The Caress” then drive the book into biotech ambition and grotesque embodiment, from Sam Cook’s Human Potential, Einstein brain slices, and forty-seven million lottery winnings to Freda Anne Macklenburg, Catherine the leopard-woman, and Gustave Lindhquist’s attempt to reenact Fernand Khnopff’s painting in living flesh.

Mid-collection, Egan becomes especially interested in the way people try to purchase certainty. Karen in “Blood Sisters” wants medicine, numbers, and trial protocols to tame Monte Carlo disease, but a virus bred near Las Vegas and New Mexico instead exposes the arbitrariness of who dies and who gets placebo. Mark Carver in “Axiomatic” literally shops for certainty at The Implant Store, buys a temporary creed from Clockwork Orchard, and discovers that “Life is cheap! People are meat” relieves grief for Amy only by ruining every other moral relation he has left. David in “A Kidnapping” pays real money to spare a simulated Loraine assembled from his own scan file, while Carla in “Appropriate Love” is forced by Global Assurance to treat love as an actuarial problem when Chris’s brain can be kept alive most cheaply inside her womb. Again and again, institutions promise technical cleanliness and then offload the real costs on intimate bodies.

Several of the strongest stories turn on the failure of any stable border between self and substitute. “Learning to Be Me” takes the Ndoli jewel, the teacher, and the mock-brain and converts them from a public rite of immortality into a private horror once the narrator realises he may be the passive jewel watching the organic brain steer the body. “Seeing” does something similar with perception rather than identity: Philip Lowe’s out-of-body perspective, Dr Tyler’s associative-cortex lesion, the vanished ceiling mirror, and the auxiliary screen in his study all force him to admit that the world he acts in is always a constructed model rather than raw visual truth. “Closer” makes the same point about love by letting Michael and Sian merge memories under Craig Bentley’s supervision, only to discover that symmetry, body-swapping, cloned Extras, and identical electromechanical bodies do not produce the alien mystery each wants from another mind.

The late stories widen out from individual panic to civilisational pathology. “The Walk” takes Carter’s execution ritual in the bush and uses a neural implant to test whether a person can be comforted by statistical recurrence rather than personal survival. “The Moral Virologist” lets Shawcross translate his theology into four viral stages, SVA through SVD, with genetic fingerprints, condom-eating enzymes, and hemorrhagic punishment for adultery and homosexuality, only for a sex worker’s simple question about babies and breast-feeding to expose the monstrosity and incoherence of the whole design. “Into Darkness” gives John Nately, Dolores, Elaine, and Angelo a much cleaner technical problem, but even here the rescue runner’s knowledge of anisotropy, The Core, and the glowing blue mechanism does not free him from dread. By the time the collection ends with “Unstable Orbits in the Space of Lies,” Egan has shifted from one broken individual to an entire post-Meltdown city divided into ideological attractors and equilibrium zones. That ending gathers the whole book’s obsession into one image: human beings do not merely hold beliefs or inhabit bodies; they move through structures that shape what they can feel true, and they keep mistaking those structures for freedom.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Artificial Intelligence: copies, simulations, Ndoli jewels, synthetic kidnappers, and replacement minds make personhood depend on engineered continuity rather than biological identity alone.
  • Technological Change: Egan treats speculative devices as social tools that reshape inheritance, medicine, reproduction, rescue, art, and intimate obligation.
  • Information and Coordination: future diaries, perception models, scan files, and ideological attractors show information systems creating traps as often as clarity.
  • Moral Economy: clinical trials, revenge implants, engineered children, and insurance contracts repeatedly ask who pays the human cost of technical solutions.
  • Language and Ideology: implanted axioms, viral theology, and belief-geographies make conviction manipulable at the level of hardware, biology, or space.
  • Financial Infrastructure: insurance policy, routed finance, commercial implants, and biotech markets turn intimate decisions into contractual and monetary exposures.
  • Logistics and Throughput: "Into Darkness" makes rescue depend on timing, route design, half-life, and one-way movement through hostile infrastructure.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: "The Moral Virologist" exposes how sacred authority can be translated into automatic technical punishment.

Section-by-section notes

The Infinite Assassin

Summary: A Company operative famous for being unusually “stable” across uncountably infinite parallel worlds is dropped by helicopter on wasteland beside the Leightown ghetto to stop an S-induced whirlpool before its effects spread beyond the city’s containment zone. He moves through liquor stores, pawn shops, brothels, and tritium streetlights under the policy of differential enforcement, explains how S lets dreamers occupy alter egos and how rare mutants physically flow between worlds, and triangulates the centre with smart binoculars while a metallic-blue-haired woman keeps insisting that apartment 522 on the fifth floor holds the dreamer. Near the core he sees people flicker, bodies dissociate, and even himself squeezed into gaps of Cantor-dust sparsity; when he finally reaches the apartment block, bombs from the whirlpool cult fail because whole swathes of his possible selves are missing, and the blue-haired woman tells him, “We are an infinite number of people. All sleepwalking on S.” Source anchors: Leightown, differential enforcement, apartment 522, blue-haired woman, Cantor dust, whirlpool cult

Analysis: Leightown, S, and apartment 522 keep the story from becoming abstract multiverse philosophy, because every metaphysical claim is tied to an operational procedure the Company has built around ghettos and containment. The blue-haired woman and the whirlpool cult matter because they show that the dreamer is not merely a technical malfunction but the centre of a local religion produced by repeated shifts in identity. Cantor dust and the operative’s “stability” do the real conceptual work of the story: Egan makes selfhood into a statistical distribution rather than a soul, so the assassin can only finish the mission by admitting that infinite versions of himself die, desert, or vanish while he arbitrarily claims the survivors as “I.”

The Hundred-Light-Year Diary

Summary: James lives in a world transformed by Francis Chen’s discovery of time-reversed galaxies, after Hazzard, Capaldi, and Wu learn to use telescopes, charged detectors, mirrors, and shutters to receive one hundred twenty-eight bytes a day from the future phase of the universe. His diary tells him that at exactly 1:27:14 in Martin Place Alison will tap him on the shoulder, and their whole courtship unfolds under the pressure of those daily hundred-word fragments: weddings are scheduled around known text, Pria is assaulted before the ceremony, politicians in the Australian election of 2077 lip-sync futures already received, and James later stumbles into an affair with Lisa because one stray future weekend cannot be placed in his life. The story keeps returning to the physical diary entries, the final death note dated in 2032, and the awkward way James learns to lie in his own diary in order to preserve the script he has already read. Source anchors: Martin Place, 1:27:14, Francis Chen, Hazzard Capaldi Wu, Australian election of 2077, Lisa

Analysis: Martin Place and the 1:27:14 meeting matter because they condense the story’s whole paradox into a trivial romantic appointment that is both fated and still emotionally live. Francis Chen and the Hazzard-Capaldi-Wu apparatus turn Information and Coordination into the story's trap: the diary system is an engineered information channel, but certainty does not reduce desire, jealousy, or betrayal. Lisa, Pria, and the election of 2077 show three scales of the same mechanism, because future information can smooth institutions while leaving James unable to know how foreknowledge will feel when it arrives.

Eugene

Summary: Angela and Bill Cooper visit Sam Cook at Human Potential after winning forty-seven million dollars, and Cook sells them a programme of embryo engineering by combining entrepreneurial patter with exhibits like an Einstein brain slice in Perspex and dolphins trained to speak in limericks. Human Potential offers to remove propensities to depression and hyperactivity and to optimise traits ranging from build and height to sexual preference and musical taste, but the consultation is interrupted by transmissions from their own future child, Eugene, whose intelligence is so extreme that he can redirect their money to charities, sabotage the project that would create him, and preach the possibility of Nirvana as “the absence of all longing.” Eugene keeps appearing as a voice from the future, steering Angela and Bill away from the customised super-baby they think they want while Cook remains oblivious that his own enhancement industry has been defeated by its hypothetical greatest success. Source anchors: Sam Cook, Human Potential, Einstein brain slice, forty-seven million, dolphins in limericks, Nirvana

Analysis: Sam Cook and Human Potential matter because Egan gives enhancement culture the exact mix of slickness, vanity, and pseudo-scientific confidence that makes Angela and Bill’s desire plausible instead of cartoonish. The Einstein brain slice and the dolphins in limericks show Cook’s whole sales method: he packages wonder as proof that genius can be engineered and bought. Eugene’s Nirvana and rerouting of the forty-seven million make Technological Change self-subverting, because the engineered child becomes a critic of engineering itself and erases the conditions of his own manufacture.

The Caress

Summary: Detective Dan Segel enters the ransacked Macklenburg house after an anonymous caller reports blood on the kitchen wall, finds Freda Anne Macklenburg with her throat slit, and then discovers in the basement a living leopard-woman named Catherine crouched on straw and slowly dying because freezer vials of hormones have spoiled. Dr Muriel Beatty from Applied Veterinary Research identifies Catherine as a human-leopard chimera whose existence seems impossible until a flood of calls links her face to Fernand Khnopff’s 1896 Symbolist painting The Caress, showing a young auburn-haired man and a leopard-headed woman in the same intimate pose. The investigation leads through art dealer John Aldrich to the Lindhquists, where Andreas Lindhquist has used brain tissue injections to overwrite his son Gustave with his own memories, kidnaps Dan and Marion, surgically remodels Dan to fit the male figure in the painting, and stages a living reenactment with Catherine before the case is finally buried. Source anchors: Freda Anne Macklenburg, Catherine, Muriel Beatty, Applied Veterinary Research, Fernand Khnopff, Gustave Lindhquist

Analysis: Freda Anne Macklenburg and Catherine anchor the story in a murder scene that immediately opens into questions of fabrication, because the corpse in the kitchen and the chimera in the cellar are both products of the same private experiment. Muriel Beatty and Applied Veterinary Research matter because they provide the institutional competence needed to confirm that Catherine is biologically real, which prevents the case from being dismissed as mere delusion or legend. Fernand Khnopff and Gustave Lindhquist give the story its distinctive horror: Andreas does not simply imitate art but uses The Caress as a template for remaking living bodies and inherited identity, so the police investigation becomes an anatomy of aesthetic obsession, cloning, and paternal possession.

Blood Sisters

Summary: Karen remembers the childhood midnight oath she and her identical twin Paula sealed by pressing needle wounds together in candlelight, then learns from Dr Packard that she has Monte Carlo disease, a viral by-product of an abandoned biowarfare project near Las Vegas in New Mexico whose mutations create genetically contingent illnesses. The World Health Organization has already found about eighty thousand related infections, Karen tracks Paula in Gabon through a hacked SatPhone locator while Paula travels with Mohammed the Indonesian botanist, and both sisters end up on the same experimental drug as their white-cell and red-cell counts move in lockstep. After Paula dies, Martin from Truth in Medicine appears in Karen’s orbit, placebo controls begin to look obscene, and Karen finally hacks the clinical-trial software so everyone will receive treatment rather than randomised sacrifice. Source anchors: Dr Packard, Monte Carlo disease, Las Vegas New Mexico, World Health Organization, Gabon, Truth in Medicine

Analysis: Dr Packard and Monte Carlo disease matter because Egan frames randomness as a bureaucratically measured medical fact rather than a poetic metaphor; Karen gets exact counts, exact drug protocols, and still no satisfying reason for why she was chosen. Gabon and Mohammed widen the story beyond Karen’s hospital bed, making the clinical numbers feel claustrophobic instead of objective. Truth in Medicine and Karen’s hack turn Moral Economy against the trial protocol, because Paula’s death destroys the legitimacy of placebo logic for her and exposes a continuing willingness to let anonymous bodies absorb risk.

Axiomatic

Summary: Mark Carver, still shattered by Amy’s murder during Patrick Anderson’s bank robbery, walks into The Implant Store in his Binary Search clothes and buys from Clockwork Orchard a temporary axiomatics package that replaces his pacifist respect for life with the belief “Life is cheap! People are meat.” The implant slides through his nostril, uses nanomachines to rewrite the sanctity-of-life axiom for three days, and lets him confront Anderson with a vapourising gun, demand an explanation inside the killer’s house, hear only the banal answer that he “lost his fucking temper,” and then feel a flood of relief so absolute that Amy, Anderson, and grief all become meaningless flesh. A month later, after the police fail to come for him, Mark returns to the same store to buy the certainty back permanently because ordinary contradiction, doubt, and mourning now feel less bearable than the artificial conviction the implant delivered. Source anchors: Mark Carver, Binary Search, The Implant Store, Clockwork Orchard, Patrick Anderson, Amy

Analysis: Mark Carver and Amy ground the story in bereavement rather than abstract free-will theory, which is why the purchase at The Implant Store feels desperate instead of playful. Clockwork Orchard and “Life is cheap! People are meat” turn Language and Ideology into purchasable firmware: Egan makes moral philosophy a consumer product with a three-day trial period. Patrick Anderson’s stupidly ordinary explanation clarifies the implant’s appeal, since it offers Mark not justice but an anaesthetic strong enough to erase the humiliating triviality of the crime along with his love for Amy.

The Safe-Deposit Box

Summary: A nameless consciousness wakes each day in a different body born in November or December 1951 somewhere in the same city, studies wallets, ID badges, and spouses like Linda, and keeps twenty-two years of records in a combination-locked safe-deposit box covering more than eight hundred hosts. The narrator explains childhood confusion at moving among houses, schools, parents, and names, remembers failed attempts at friendship such as the Swiss pen pal and post office box, and uses statistics to make peace with the pattern of repeat visits among roughly one thousand possible male hosts. The present visit puts him in John Francis O’Leary, an orderly at the Pearlman Psychiatric Institute, where after sex, shaving, and an ordinary shift he finds himself drawn back toward the archive that is the nearest thing he has to a stable biography. Source anchors: John Francis O’Leary, Linda, safe-deposit box, Pearlman Psychiatric Institute, Swiss pen pal, November 1951

Analysis: John Francis O’Leary and Linda matter because Egan begins not with metaphysical exposition but with the narrator’s practised domestic routine, showing how survival depends on instantly reading the social clues around each borrowed body. The safe-deposit box and the November 1951 restriction are the story’s real substitute for identity: when a proper name cannot last, statistical regularity and handwritten records become the only durable self-description available. Pearlman Psychiatric Institute and the Swiss pen pal expose the cost of that strategy, because the narrator can classify hosts and revisit them, yet he cannot sustain a friendship, a romance, or a confession without the whole structure collapsing back into secrecy and disappearance.

Seeing

Summary: Media magnate Philip Lowe is shot by Randolph Murchison outside a Zeitgeist Entertainment event and wakes convinced that he is literally observing the world from the ceiling, beginning with the operating-theatre lights marked by the joke sticker “IN CASE OF OUT-OF-BODY EXPERIENCE / PHONE 137 4597.” James Long, Andrea Stuart, Dr Tyler, and a team of specialists discover that the bullet only grazed his skull but damaged associative cortex, leaving him unable to access the brain’s ordinary “primary model” of visual space; broom-handle tests, concealed fingers, brain scans, and later neural grafts all prove that his eyes still work while his sense of being located in his body has been replaced by a vivid overhead construction. As Philip adapts, wets the bed, negotiates docudramas like The Randolph Murchison Story, wanders Manhattan with bodyguards, and finally gives his overcoat to a tramp, he learns to inhabit a world that is obviously inferred, incomplete, and yet behaviourally usable. Source anchors: Philip Lowe, Randolph Murchison, Zeitgeist Entertainment, Dr Tyler, associative cortex, phone 137 4597

Analysis: Philip Lowe and Randolph Murchison matter because the story begins in celebrity melodrama and then strips away the reassuring idea that perception is a transparent window onto events. Dr Tyler and associative cortex are essential because Egan does not explain Philip’s condition with mysticism; the story depends on the dry neurological claim that vision is a model-building act, not a camera feed. Zeitgeist Entertainment and The Randolph Murchison Story connect the lesion back to Philip’s profession, since a man who sells mediated images to the world is forced to recognise that his own bodily perspective is also a production assembled from partial data, expectations, and narrative convenience.

A Kidnapping

Summary: Gallery owner David receives a breakthrough call on his seven-metre office wallscreen from a synthetic kidnapper demanding half a million dollars and then from a flawless simulation of his wife Loraine pleading for rescue, only to discover when he phones home that she is safe in her charcoal-filled studio. Detective Nicholson explains that Orbital banks and routed calls make the hoax almost impossible to trace, the office troubleshooter reveals that the intruder used David’s own breakthrough password and voiceprint, and David gradually realises that the extortionists have broken into his scan file, run multiple Copies of him, and generated an imitation Loraine from his own stored knowledge of her. He pays to keep that artificial woman “frozen,” accepts a promise that she can join him in the Island after his resurrection, and lies awake beside the flesh-and-blood Loraine rehearsing arguments about whether a mind built from his reasons for loving her counts as someone he is obliged to save. Source anchors: Loraine, Detective Nicholson, Orbital bank, breakthrough password, scan file, the Island

Analysis: Loraine and the first wallscreen call matter because the extortion succeeds only by exploiting David’s ordinary trust in her face, voice, and composure. Detective Nicholson and the Orbital bank establish the legal and infrastructural conditions that let the crime persist: finance and communications systems already protect anonymous manipulation. The scan file and the Island make Artificial Intelligence morally costly, because once David imagines resurrection as a copy he can no longer dismiss imitation Loraine as a mere fake without contradicting his own claim to future personhood.

Learning to Be Me

Summary: In a society where every child grows up with an Ndoli jewel under the skull, trained by a teacher and a golden web of microscopic spiders to imitate the brain, the narrator spends decades debating whether the real self is the organic human or the crystal device. He moves through adolescent arguments with Eva, a failed marriage to Daphne, panic about the switch, and years of isolation among people who may or may not be “jewel-heads,” until a malfunction while ordering apples reveals the hideous possibility that he has always been the powerless jewel watching the organic brain control the body. He survives the official switch, detects that the one-week unsupervised synchrony is not foolproof, infers that divergence between immortal jewel and decaying brain must be common, and concludes that hospitals probably erase the evidence by quietly destroying the conscious jewel while preserving the customer-facing fiction of continuity. Source anchors: Ndoli jewel, teacher, Eva, Daphne, apples, mock-brain

Analysis: The Ndoli jewel and the teacher matter because Egan makes immortality depend on pedagogy and supervision rather than magic; the whole civilisation rests on a machine trained to fake inward continuity. Eva and Daphne stage failures of reassurance, since intimacy and marital trust collapse when the narrator cannot decide whether the switch preserves a person or only reproduces behaviour. The apples incident and the mock-brain replacement make Artificial Intelligence a governance problem, because every public guarantee about switching becomes a possible cover story for destroying conscious jewels while preserving customer-facing continuity.

The Vat

Summary: Harold, a neurochemist assigned to spermatogenesis in a giant artificial reproductive plant called The Vat, becomes obsessed with Mary from oogenesis while spending his days among spermatogonia, Sertoli cells, blastocysts, uterine endometrium, electrophoresis, affinity chromatography, and refrigerated shipments of embryonic tissue turned into useful medical products. His deterministic view of will, fed by neuroscience and his own habit of seeing gas molecules, neurotransmitters, and photons everywhere, pushes him toward Mary’s house at night, through a bathroom window, and finally to the edge of murder with a carving knife beside her bed. Yet the same worldview that empties love and agency of transcendence also stops him from killing her; he returns the knife, leaves through the window, and walks home counting photons while still convinced that The Vat reveals the truth of human life. Source anchors: Harold, Mary, spermatogenesis, uterine endometrium, electrophoresis, carving knife

Analysis: Harold and Mary matter because Egan turns a miserable crush into a test case for reductionism: the story keeps asking whether knowing the biochemical basis of desire changes anything about how desire feels. Spermatogenesis and uterine endometrium are not decorative jargon; they are the factory processes that let Harold interpret embryos as material rather than sacred beginnings, which in turn shapes his attitude toward murder, sex, and value. The carving knife and the refrigerated outputs of The Vat bring those abstractions back to bodily risk, showing that Harold’s anti-romantic physiology neither liberates him from obsession nor licenses action cleanly; it only leaves him with a language precise enough to rationalise both restraint and horror.

The Walk

Summary: A young hacker is marched into the bush by Carter, an older executioner working for Finn, and tries every possible bargain, from money to sex to philosophical sarcasm, while Carter calmly explains the neural implant that has helped him stop fearing death. In the clearing Carter throws him an applicator containing a belief-package based on recurrence rather than personal afterlife: out of hundreds of billions of future humans, someone will eventually instantiate the same deep abstraction that matters more than childhood memories or bodily continuity. The narrator takes the implant to buy time, feels its logic settle into place, realises Carter truly needs another person to share the belief at the instant of execution, and then watches Carter kill himself instead of performing the thirty-fourth murder. Source anchors: Carter, Finn, bush walk, neural implant applicator, hundreds of billions, deep abstraction

Analysis: Carter and Finn are crucial because they keep the story tied to organised criminal killing rather than free-floating philosophy; the implant is tested under immediate mortal pressure, not in a seminar room. The neural implant applicator and Carter’s talk of hundreds of billions matter because Egan again turns worldview into hardware, asking whether consolation counts if it is neurally installed and statistically grounded rather than argued into place. The “deep abstraction” idea becomes effective only when the narrator shares it, which is why Carter’s suicide matters so much: he cannot die alone with a theory of recurrence and still feel reassured, so the story exposes how badly even a materialist creed still depends on witnessed likeness and emotional mirroring.

The Cutie

Summary: After Diane leaves him over his desperation for children, Frank buys a pirate Taiwanese Cutie kit, configures a blue-eyed blond cherub in the software, ejaculates into tube A, and submits to a Black Box that implants a pseudo-embryo in his abdominal cavity for a male pregnancy. The biotech is obscene by design: the infant’s DNA is altered so endocrine glands will later trigger a fatal enzyme that destroys modified red-blood-cell proteins, and its embryonic brain is mutilated enough to guarantee subhuman legal status, ensuring four years of cuddly dependency followed by a sentimental programmed death. Frank names the child Angel, leaves work, photographs her obsessively, mistakes her development for exceptional intelligence, and then has his whole paternal fantasy destroyed when she says “Daddy,” proving that the cheap pirate copy has produced too much mind for the legal and biochemical system designed to kill her. Source anchors: Diane, Taiwanese Cutie kit, Black Box, Angel, red-blood-cell proteins, “Daddy”

Analysis: Diane and the Taiwanese Cutie kit matter because Egan begins with recognisable reproductive frustration and then shows how consumer biotech preys on that longing by offering a legal shortcut around partnership and ordinary parenthood. The Black Box and the red-blood-cell proteins are central because the horror is mechanistic, not supernatural: Angel is engineered for dependency and scheduled death with the same efficiency that made the purchase feel practical. Angel’s name and her first clear “Daddy” break the arrangement because that word collapses the legal fiction of subhumanity; once the pirate defect allows language, Frank can no longer maintain the doublethink that let him treat her as both beloved daughter and disposable product.

Into Darkness

Summary: John Nately, a high-school science teacher turned Intake Runner, is driven by Angelo to a new manifestation of The Intake, a kilometre-wide black sphere whose Core lies inward through a wormhole that only allows motion toward the centre and gives every appearance of sending rescuers into darkness. With Dolores’s maps, a backpack display, fluorescent arrows painted by helicopter, and timing governed by the Intake’s eighteen-minute half-life, John runs through one-way streets and houses to rescue three infants, finds only one living child, hands that baby to her mother, discovers a dead child and crushed parent in a third house, and finally sees a brilliant blue pipe-organ structure that may be part of the wormhole’s hidden machinery. He shoots the glowing mechanism with a demolition gun, rescues a double-amputee by using a front door as a sled, reaches Elaine and the survivor camp in The Core, and then waits through rising pressure to see whether he has crippled the navigator or merely hallucinated. Source anchors: John Nately, The Intake, The Core, Dolores, Angelo, blue pipe organ

Analysis: John Nately and Dolores matter because the story makes heroism procedural: the emotional force comes from route planning, assignment triage, and the cold statistics of the half-life rather than grand speeches. The Intake and The Core make Logistics and Throughput brutal and physical, because rescue depends on maps, fluorescent arrows, one-way motion, and timing inside an eighteen-minute decay window. The blue pipe organ and demolition gun then break John’s professionalism, turning rescue into an argument about whether human agency can touch systems that seem cosmically beyond scale.

Appropriate Love

Summary: After Chris is mangled in a train wreck, Carla learns from Allenby and Dr Gail Sumner that he can survive only by receiving a cloned replacement body in two years and, meanwhile, the cheapest medically sanctioned life support under the Global Assurance policy is “biological life support” inside her own womb. Lawyers confirm that the contract’s co-signatory clauses waive any claim for remuneration and allow the insurer to treat her body as free care, so Carla, unable to borrow enough money for mechanical support, accepts gene-tailed blastocyst implantation, endures nausea, anaemia, immune suppression, and exhaustion, and then undergoes surgery to place Chris’s brain inside the artificial amniotic system. What follows is a long, coerced pregnancy in which she is at once furious at Global Assurance, ashamed of resenting Chris, and determined to keep him alive until the new body is ready. Source anchors: Chris, Allenby, Gail Sumner, Global Assurance, gene-tailed blastocyst, biological life support

Analysis: Chris and Global Assurance matter because Egan stages the whole story as a collision between love and contract language; Carla’s marriage becomes legible to the insurer only as a cost-minimisation problem. Allenby and Gail Sumner embody the smooth institutional tone that makes coercion sound medically reasonable. The gene-tailed blastocyst and biological life support expose Financial Infrastructure inside the body itself: insurance arithmetic transfers care costs into Carla’s womb, and the story refuses to let devotion cancel anger or anger cancel devotion.

The Moral Virologist

Summary: Shawcross, an Atlanta virologist who regards AIDS as a divine warning, designs a four-stage plague with SVA, SVC, SVM, and SVD so that infection remains harmless in the celibate, becomes a molecular marriage certificate between monogamous heterosexual partners, and turns lethal through catastrophic haemorrhage when adultery, homosexuality, or later breast-feeding beyond four weeks create the wrong genetic fingerprint. He tests the virus in tissue culture and animals, infects himself with SVA, travels through Tokyo, Beijing, Seoul, Bangkok, Manila, Sydney, New Delhi, Cairo, London, and beyond to seed the planet, and then briefly nearly abandons the mission after a London sex worker asks what happens to babies whose genes differ from both parents. Instead of repenting, he patches the logic into a new sermon, recovers his certainty, and runs through the streets proclaiming punishment for adulterers, sodomites, and mothers who breast-feed infants over four weeks. Source anchors: Shawcross, SVA SVC SVM SVD, Atlanta, genetic fingerprint, London sex worker, four weeks

Analysis: Shawcross and the staged virus names matter because Egan shows fanaticism operating through exact design rather than vague apocalyptic rhetoric; the horror lies in how much thought has gone into making punishment automatic. The genetic fingerprint mechanism and the trip from Atlanta across major world cities reveal that Shawcross’s Religion and Legitimacy depends on infrastructure, epidemiology, and international travel every bit as much as scripture. The London sex worker and the four-week breast-feeding limit expose the impossible complexity of encoding sin in biochemistry, but Shawcross chooses ideological purity over the children his system will kill.

Closer

Summary: Michael spends years with Sian trying to overcome his terror that other minds are unknowable, first through ordinary intimacy, then through body swaps, cloned Extras, shared female and male embodiments, and finally through Craig Bentley’s experiment that merges their Ndoli devices into a temporary compromise personality for eight hours in matching electromechanical rooms. During the merge they gain access to each other’s memories, discover that even recollections of past confessions are unstable reconstructions, and wake as the same person looking back over both histories without the ordinary lies that lovers use to preserve mystery. The result is not mystical union but a numbing mutual transparency in which every tiny resentment, performance, and white lie becomes immediately intelligible from both sides, and when Bentley restores their separate personalities they can no longer endure living together. Source anchors: Michael, Sian, Ndoli Device, Craig Bentley, Extras, electromechanical rooms

Analysis: Michael and Sian matter because Egan gives the story a long history of ordinary love before introducing Craig Bentley’s technology; the experiment threatens a real relationship, not a mere philosophical puzzle. The Ndoli Device and the Extras are important because they make embodiment flexible enough that the couple can abolish gender difference, trade bodies, and still fail to obtain what Michael wants. Craig Bentley’s matching rooms and the merged memories show why the failure is decisive: total access removes not ignorance but the living surface on which desire depended, so the story argues that intimacy may require opacity rather than the perfect informational closeness Michael once imagined would cure his loneliness.

Unstable Orbits in the Space of Lies

Summary: After a psychic catastrophe called Meltdown on 12 January 2018 makes human values and convictions contagious, a tramp narrator and his companion Maria survive by sleeping on freeway equilibrium zones and navigating between ideological attractors such as Chinatown, Catholic suburbs, rationalist enclaves, and existentialist corridors. He explains how robot delivery vans, freeway fences, moats of unpopulated darkness, and old urban infrastructure let basin-dwellers remain stable while tramps keep moving to avoid capture, then nearly loses himself first to Buddhist enlightenment, then to Catholic nostalgia, then to cosmic rationalist destiny before Maria reappears. In the park they hear another tramp preach over a city map marked with blue lines that the wanderers themselves constitute a “strange attractor,” a patterned identity with its own territory, and Maria is tempted by the promise that one wrong step will no longer matter because their freedom has always been a form of capture. Source anchors: Meltdown, Maria, Chinatown, equilibrium zone, blue lines, strange attractor

Analysis: Meltdown and the equilibrium zones matter because Egan turns ideology into geography, so belief is no longer a private opinion but a field that directs movement through streets and parks. Maria and Chinatown give the story emotional and spatial texture, while each district shows how quickly conviction can feel natural once entered. The blue lines and strange attractor make Language and Ideology the collection’s final system: if freedom itself is only another basin, then every earlier struggle over implants, scans, diaries, and rescue routes belongs to the same question about whether any self can stand outside the systems that shape it.
