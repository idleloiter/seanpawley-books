Hidden Family

Buy on Amazon — Hidden Family

Position in the vault

The Hidden Family is the second volume of Merchant Princes, immediately following the events of book one and concluding on a cliff-edge that the series carries forward. It is the book in which the series' premise hardens from "a woman discovers she is a world-walking aristocrat" into a three-world economic and security problem: the medieval Gruinmarkt (world one, the Clan's home), the contemporary United States (world two), and the industrial-empire world of New Britain (world three), which Miriam discovers and then annexes as a business territory.

Thematically it sits in the vault's state-formation and political-economy cluster. It most directly feeds the shared concepts of Illicit Markets, Commercial Society, Elite Formation, and State Capacity, and it is the Merchant Princes volume that turns Miriam from fugitive into institutional actor. Its natural companions are the other volumes of the Merchant Princes sequence and the vault's maps of content for state order and for firms and finance.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Miriam Beckstein / Helge Thorold-Hjorth: an investigative journalist revealed as the long-lost daughter of the Clan's western line. Her motivation is survival, then autonomy: she wants proof of the conspiracy against her that will defeat the Clan's move to declare her incompetent, and she builds a legitimate import/export and patent business in New Britain ("world three") as both her power base and her defense. Her arc moves from running-and-hiding to presenting herself as a strategist before the Clan's assembly.
  • Iris, later revealed as Patricia Thorold-Hjorth: Miriam's adoptive mother and, it emerges, her birth mother. A onetime radical-activist turned reclusive bookseller's wife, wheelchair-bound with multiple sclerosis, she disguised her daughter's bloodline for decades with forged papers and false adoption records. Her motivation is to keep Miriam out of the Clan's dynastic machinery until she can defend herself; she is the book's deepest reserve of covert skill, keeping a sawed-off shotgun under her chair and killing intruders without hesitation.
  • Angbard Lofstrom: duke and head of Clan security. He takes personal responsibility for Miriam's induction, releases Olga into her care, and — once Matthias is exposed — accepts Patricia's return, clears the succession, and backs the rescue. Motivated by the survival of the Clan as an institution as much as by kinship.
  • Matthias van Hjorth: Angbard's "keeper of secrets" and secretary, outer family. He schemes to replace Angbard at the head of Clan security, blackmails Roland with camera footage, and as the mechanism fails he runs: sending the "deep throat" CD and the FBI/DEA letters that expose the Clan's American money trail, then fleeing with bearer bonds and secrets.
  • Roland: Clan guard and Angbard's security deputy, Miriam's lover. His motivation is divided between duty and love; Matthias's blackmail converts his private life into a lever, and the recoiling of that leverage is fatal.
  • Brilliana d'Ost: Miriam's lady-in-waiting, planted on her by Angbard as a bodyguard. She world-walks, has formal combat training, and is the refuge and courier that keeps the two worlds of the plot connected. Her motivation is loyalty redeemed by escape from the Clan's "gilded poverty."
  • Olga Thorold: Miriam's bodyguard-courier and closest ally in the Clan; wounded by the same assassins repeatedly. Her arc converts her from errant noble into invested partner, and she is the one who refuses to let the meeting's killing logic stand unchallenged.
  • Esau / James Lee: a senior son of the New British Chinese merchant family, the sixth, "lost" branch. Motivated by filial obedience to the Eldest's standing death order against Patricia's line, he is the book's view inside the hidden family's power structure, culminating in the family tale of the abandoned ancestor.
  • The Eldest (Great-Uncle) and Great-Uncle Huan: the Lee patriarchs who sit in the Yellow Room, keep the family's arms and talismans, and — crucially — begin to suspect that "the talent lies in the pattern instead of the bearer."
  • Erasmus Burgeson: New Britain's pawnbroker, Marx-quoting Leveler quartermaster with an explosives cellar, the window through which Miriam buys identity, currency, and radical contacts. Motivated by revolutionary conviction and by the commercial logic of doing business with a bottomless source of gold.
  • Lin Lee: a fifteen-year-old Lee family courier captured in Miriam's house, the living evidence of the sixth family's existence and the vehicle for the abandonment narrative that transforms the feud's meaning.
  • Paulette O'Neill: Miriam's American partner-paralegal, who runs the company machinery, pores over patent law and museum catalogues, and supplies the operational brain on the United States side.
  • Inspector Smith: of New Britain's Homeland Defense Bureau, the empire's policeman whose stakeout, bullion theories, and lost fourteen mark the state's uncomprehending pursuit of the consequences Miriam leaves behind.
  • Sir Alfred Durant: New British motor industrialist, the brake-shoe customer who converts Miriam's patent play into a real company and offers to invest.
  • Baron Oliver Hjorth and the dowager duchess Hildegarde: the internal opposition to Miriam's legitimacy on the Clan's own court, scheming (per Miriam's accusation) to have her declared incompetent or worse.

Detailed overview

Hidden Family opens by making New Britain politically real before Miriam turns it into a business opportunity. King John Frederick's Imperial Intelligence Oversight Committee hears Sir Roderick, Lord Douglass, Lord Scotia, and the police reports about Levelers, illicit printers in Massachusetts, the western New Provinces, and New London, plus a plot against the Western Summer Palace at Monterey. The cabinet worries about poor grain harvests, bread prices, new taxes, French livres, ranters, reservists at Fort Victoria, and loyalty in local regiments. That opening matters because Miriam's later arrival with gold, patents, and revolutionary contacts does not happen in a blank alternate Boston; it happens in a paranoid empire already searching for foreign financing behind domestic unrest.

Miriam, Paulette, and Brilliana begin the book as fugitives trying to turn flight into organization. Paulette houses Brill in Boston, files company paperwork, opens bank accounts, and receives blood-money capital while teaching Brill about electricity, flush toilets, household chores, and twenty-first-century streets. Miriam, meanwhile, tests the assassin's locket and reaches a third North America, where Boston has steam cars, airships, English signs, pounds and shillings, paupers, police, sedition law, and Erasmus Burgeson's pawnbroker shop. The book's title becomes literal here: the hidden family is not merely a secret branch of the Clan, but a second world-walking lineage anchored in another industrial world.

The Lee family emerges as both kin and enemy. Esau is James Lee, a senior son in a New British Chinese merchant family whose elders preserve talismans, household shrines, armed retainers, and a grievance about abandonment by the eastern Clan. Their ancestor reconstructed the knotwork from memory after losing the first talisman, so his descendants travel between Gruinmarkt and New Britain rather than Gruinmarkt and Miriam's America. Stross turns a myth of betrayal into a technical error with political consequences: a wrong pattern created a separate route, a separate fortune, and a family memory strong enough to justify assassinations decades later.

Miriam's response is entrepreneurial and forensic. She works with Paulette on art arbitrage, parachutes, disk brakes, asbestos-resin brake shoes, electric motors, flash boilers, proportional fonts, and patents; she sets up an office in New Britain with Declan McHugh, Roger, Martin, Soames, Oswald, and a growing staff. She uses gold bars, identity papers, Burgeson's contacts, letters, and lawyers to build a local base while her American house, Iris's house, and the doppelganger warehouse prove unsafe. Her method is not to flee family politics but to create facts Angbard, Oliver Hjorth, Hildegarde, the Lees, and the New British police must react to.

The book's emotional center is Miriam's changing family knowledge. Iris is not merely a foster mother who hid a locket; she is Patricia Thorold-Hjorth, Miriam's birth mother, who escaped after Alfredo Wu and Joan were killed, survived a stabbing, met Morris through radical networks, and raised Miriam under false adoption papers. Angbard is ruthless, but he can also be forced to listen when Miriam exposes Matthias's private dossiers, forged moves, and link to the attackers. The relationship with Roland sharpens this conflict: his love is real enough to save her in Fort Lofstrom, but Matthias has already turned their affair into blackmail, and the cost of that vulnerability is lethal.

The climax turns a shareholder meeting into a trial of family history. Angbard calls Patricia, Olga, and Miriam before the Clan extraordinary meeting, lays out the attacks on Patricia and Alfredo, and lets Miriam explain the Lee talisman, New Britain, the wrong knot, the old murder campaign, and the commercial value of a new world. Matthias tries to escape by sending Paulette's deep throat CD to American FBI and DEA offices, then Fort Lofstrom becomes a trap with dead servants, a rear guard, and explosives. By the epilogue, Roland is dead, Inspector Smith is baffled by fourteen people vanishing from Burgeson's shop, and Miriam is hosting a New London motor-trade celebration for brake shoes with Angbard, Iris, Olga, Brill, and Paulette present. The victory is real but wounded: she has a business, allies, and recognition, but her route there has already cost lives.

The book's analytical spine runs underneath the plot. The Clan is presented as a rent-seeking transport monopoly whose wealth comes from moving drugs and precious metal across worlds — a business that, Miriam argues, is dying because anonymity is eroding. Her counter-model is value creation: shipping patents and ideas into Victorian-industrial New Britain, where an obsolete invention can be licensed for a fortune, and then using the profits to modernize the lesser-developed Gruinmarkt. The title's "hidden family" therefore doubles as the book's political science: secret, family-based institutions — the Clan and the Lees — control economic life through information asymmetries, and Miriam's project is to make them legible, tradeable, and finally normal.

Major linkages

  • Merchant Princes — the series hub; this is volume two, and the book establishes the three-world cosmology (Gruinmarkt / United States / New Britain) on which later volumes build.
  • Illicit Markets — the Clan's drug and bullion traffic, Burgeson's black-market gold, the identity trade, and Miriam's uneasy entry into revolutionary finance.
  • Commercial Society — Miriam's explicit counter-thesis to mercantilism: labor and ideas create value, not just transported goods; Smith, Marx, and the "car for every home" pitch.
  • Elite Formation — the Clan's shareholder aristocracy and the Lee family's hidden court above Hanover Street as rival elite machines.
  • State Capacity — New Britain's political police, sedition acts and stakeouts, and the FBI/DEA exposure that Matthias detonates.
  • Institutions — law-firms, patents, acts of Parliament, identities, and succession rules as the real scaffolding of the plot.
  • Knowledge Preservation — the talisman knotwork as encoded knowledge, DNA fingerprinting for kinship, forged records, and the recovered family history.
  • Information and Coordination — the postal/courier service, bugged cameras, blackmail tapes, codes, and the mobile-phone network externality argument.
  • Empire and Periphery — modernizing empire-facing New Britain against the backward Gruinmarkt; the periphery's dependence on imported machines.
  • State, Order, and Legibility and Finance, Firms, and Industrialization — the book's two most natural atlas homes.
  • Illicit Markets, Contraband, and State Capacity, Commercial Society, Stagnation, and Institutional Drift, and State Formation, Legibility, and Informal Order — reading paths the book supports directly.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Family as firm, firm as polity: the Clan is organized as a shareholder partnership with a post office; the Lees as a household court with talismans instead of shares. Both are Elite Formation machines that govern access to wealth.
  • Mercantilism defeated by ideas: Miriam's running economics lecture — from the table-and-nails argument to "ship ideas, not cargo" — is the book's most explicit statement of Commercial Society as a theory of growth.
  • Legibility as exposure: gold sales, identity papers, and police stakeouts make Miriam visible to New Britain before her business is secure; Legibility cuts both ways.
  • The state's appetite for secrets: New Britain's sedition laws, the Homeland Defense Bureau, and the United States' FBI/DEA turn State Capacity into the book's real antagonist no less than the Lee family.
  • What is being traded: bulk goods, gold, drugs, identities, patents, tapes, and persons all move; the book is a taxonomy of Illicit Markets ranged against lawful firms.
  • Knowledge as inheritance and weapon: knotwork designs, blood lines, DNA tests, dossiers, blackmail tapes, and forged adoption papers all carry the plot's load — Knowledge Preservation made lethal.
  • Legitimacy assembled in a room: the extraordinary meeting converts testimony, genetics, and record into Legitimacy and Succession, the book's answer to what counts as valid rule.
  • Path dependence and drift: the wrong knot "should" abort, but institutions adapt to error; Path Dependence and Institutional Drift explains why the feud can outlive its cause.

Core concepts

  • Illicit Markets: the book advances this concept by detailing a working smuggling economy — black-market gold (bullion ownership reserved for the crown), identity fabrication, seditious printing, and the Clan's drug-money transport — and by showing its fragility once anonymity erodes.
  • Commercial Society: the conceptual heart of the book. Miriam articulates, in the mercantilist-rejection dialogues, that labor and knowledge, not stored commodities, create value, and she operationalizes it through patents, licensing, and a brake-shoe company.
  • Elite Formation: two rival elite factories — the Clan's shareholder council and the Lee household behind the Ames-room facades — both produce and reproduce status, arms, and loyalty across generations.
  • State Capacity: New Britain's police fortress, sedition law, and "pigeons"; the United States' FBI/DEA as the state apparatus that Matthias arms against the Clan; the book stages a contest over who can use a state's violence.
  • Institutions: firms require acts of Parliament, identities are deeds posted with inns of court, patents are applications, and succession is a meeting: Miriam makes institutions the terrain of the plot.
  • Information and Coordination: the Clan is ultimately a communications network — couriers, lockets, tattoos, code books, and bugged cameras — and the book argues its real edge is message transmission, ending with the mobile-phone network-externality analogy.
  • Knowledge Preservation: the talisman system is stored knowledge that can be tattooed or photographed; family, DNA, and archive collectively let Miriam reconstruct the lost sixth family's story.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: the extraordinary meeting, witness testimony, genetic fingerprinting, the dowager's outburst, and the changed designation of Angbard's successor all show legitimacy assembled procedurally.
  • Empire and Periphery: New Britain is an Atlantic empire whose industrial century came late; the medieval Gruinmarkt is its economic periphery, modernized by importing "intermediate" technology rather than American overkill.
  • Technological Change: the book's clearest statement of diffusion: disk brakes, asbestos-resin shoes, flash boilers, batteries, parachutes, and the deliberate "obsolescence" cycle of a drip-fed R&D department.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Granularity chosen: chapter-level. The extract contains five titled Parts (Business Plans; Point of Divergence; Capitalism for Beginners; StakeOut; MeltDown), each subdivided into one to three titled chapters, plus a titled Epilogue (the same structure as the book's Table of Contents at the end of the extract). Each division below is therefore one extract chapter, with its Part named in the heading — thirteen chapters plus the epilogue, covering every division in reading order.

Part 1: Business Plans — Learned Counsel

Summary: The opening moves from John Frederick's New Britain cabinet — Sir Roderick's reports of Leveler cells, illicit printers in Massachusetts/the western New Provinces/New London, the Monterey plot, French livres, poor grain, bread prices, rearousal of ranters, Fort Victoria reserves — to Angbard and Matthias reviewing the failed attacks on Miriam (Helge) and Olga. Angbard demands tissue samples, photographs, and FBI database checks, and hears how Hildegarde's shipping-company sublease left the New Tower and parts of the palace undoppelgängered. In Boston, Miriam brings Brilliana to Paulette, opens a company account with a fifty-thousand-dollar draft, writes Paulette up as director and company secretary, and leaves Brill learning electricity, dishwashers, and a servantless household. Miriam then tests the assassin's locket (fails), warns Angbard by courier on the train, and at the Science Museum food court tells Iris the whole story; Iris presses her to trust no one, and the two lockets are shown to carry different designs.

Analysis: John Frederick and Sir Roderick make New Britain a Crisis Governance regime before Miriam arrives: sedition, bread prices, and coups justify surveillance and mobilization. Angbard and Matthias turn a Information and Coordination failure (the undoppelgängered wing) into a suspected political trap, foreshadowing the mole. Paulette's "blood money" and the two-locket comparison locate the story's engine in Illicit Markets — the trade in money, identities, and travel — from the first pages.
