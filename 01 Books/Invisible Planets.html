Detailed overview

Invisible Planets is Ken Liu's translated anthology of contemporary Chinese science fiction, built to show range rather than to define a single national school. The collection moves from Chen Qiufan's near-future labor markets, biotech waste, and Shenzhen development, through Xia Jia's ghostly machines and elder-care futures, Ma Boyong's censorship dystopia, Hao Jingfang's philosophical miniatures and class-engineered Beijing, Tang Fei's commodified intimacy, Cheng Jingbo's lyrical cosmic elegy, and Liu Cixin's planetary-scale thought experiments. Its editorial argument is that Chinese SF is not one thing: it includes cyberpunk, satire, fable, political allegory, hard SF, urban sociology, and mythic prose.

The anthology's strongest recurring concern is modernization as lived infrastructure. Chen Qiufan treats economic growth as precarity, toxic ecology, and body management; "The Year of the Rat" turns underemployment into pest control against engineered animals, "The Fish of Lijiang" treats vacation as a managed intervention into exhausted perception, and "The Flower of Shazui" places sex work, migrant aspiration, and redevelopment in the same Shenzhen economy. These stories belong near vault concepts like Technological Change, Commodity Systems, and Informal Institutions.

Xia Jia and Hao Jingfang give the anthology its most delicate social machinery. Xia Jia's ghosts, care robots, and dragon-horse automata make memory and companionship technological without making them merely mechanical. Hao Jingfang's "Invisible Planets" invents worlds as ethical diagrams, while "Folding Beijing" converts class stratification into an urban machine that literally allocates time, air, and space by economic rank. Both writers use speculative premises to make social arrangements visible as designed systems rather than natural facts.

Ma Boyong, Tang Fei, Cheng Jingbo, and Liu Cixin widen the field. "The City of Silence" rewrites censorship as a narrowing language-game in which even titles and ordinary phrases become politically unavailable. "Call Girl" turns fantasy consumption into a service economy. "Grave of the Fireflies" uses cosmic distance and fairy-tale imagery for loss and memory. Liu Cixin closes the fiction with the scale shift for which he is known: "The Circle" imagines human computation in ancient Qin, and "Taking Care of God" turns humanity's creators into dependent migrants whose arrival burdens the civilization they once seeded.

The essays at the end matter because they resist treating the stories as exotic evidence. Liu Cixin, Chen Qiufan, and Xia Jia each explain Chinese SF from a different angle: cosmic pessimism and wonder, the torn experience of rapid development, and the difficulty of defining "Chinese" in a genre built from translation, global influence, local markets, and changing readerships. The anthology is therefore useful in the vault not just as fiction but as a map of how speculative writing tracks development, censorship, class formation, platform economies, memory, and civilizational imagination.

Section-by-section notes

CHEN QIUFAN

Summary: Ken Liu introduces Chen Qiufan as a writer associated with China's fast-changing coastal modernity, especially Shenzhen, globalized production, environmental unease, and young workers facing unstable futures. The grouping prepares three stories that use speculative devices to study graduate unemployment, engineered rats, time perception, tourism, sex work, real estate, and migrant ambition. Source anchors: Chen Qiufan; Shenzhen; graduate unemployment; engineered rats; tourism; migrant ambition.

Analysis: Chen Qiufan and Shenzhen give the anthology its closest contact with contemporary development pressure. Graduate unemployment and engineered rats make labor-market failure biological and military, while tourism and migrant ambition show service economies absorbing psychic damage. The author section matters because it frames technology as something embedded in dirty growth, not clean futurism.

THE YEAR OF THE RAT

Summary: "The Year of the Rat" follows unemployed college graduates recruited into a campaign against genetically engineered rats that have escaped their intended commercial use. The narrator, his squad, and the rat-control program move through military discipline, resentment, bad food, job-market shame, state mobilization, and encounters with rats that may be smarter and more socially organized than the authorities admit. Source anchors: Year of the Rat; college graduates; engineered rats; narrator; rat-control program; state mobilization.

Analysis: Year of the Rat and college graduates turn unemployment into a paramilitary disposal system. Engineered rats and the rat-control program make failed innovation literally reproduce in the landscape, while state mobilization reveals how State Capacity can absorb educated surplus labor without creating productive employment. The campaign joins biotech risk to credential inflation: the authorities answer weak labor absorption by disciplining graduates and hunting the commercial experiment that escaped them.

THE FISH OF LIJIANG

Summary: "The Fish of Lijiang" centers on a burned-out worker sent to Lijiang for therapeutic rest, where vacation, medication, and altered time perception blur together. The narrator meets a woman named A Jie, observes fish, water, old-town tourism, corporate stress, and artificial leisure, and gradually learns that the resort environment is part of a larger system for managing exhausted professionals. Source anchors: Fish of Lijiang; Lijiang; A Jie; time perception; corporate stress; therapeutic rest.

Analysis: Fish of Lijiang and Lijiang turn tourism into a technology of labor repair. A Jie and time perception keep the story from being only satire, because the damaged workers are still capable of connection. Corporate stress and therapeutic rest matter because they show an economy that extracts attention, then sells back carefully engineered recovery.

THE FLOWER OF SHAZUI

Summary: "The Flower of Shazui" follows a young woman in Shenzhen's Shazui district as redevelopment, sex work, digital platforms, and migrant aspiration converge. The story tracks her relationship with a technology worker, the pressure of urban clearance, the language of opportunity, and the way bodies, neighborhoods, data, and desire become assets in a city remaking itself. Source anchors: Flower of Shazui; Shazui; Shenzhen; sex work; redevelopment; technology worker.

Analysis: Flower of Shazui and Shazui make the city itself the main machine. Shenzhen and redevelopment let capital replace neighborhoods, while sex work and the technology worker connect bodily intimacy to platform-era ambition. The story exposes Informal Institutions as the survival layer beneath formal redevelopment: migrants use personal bargains, rented rooms, sexual commerce, and contingent trust to remain in a city whose property market keeps displacing them.

XIA JIA

Summary: Xia Jia's author section introduces a writer whose stories move between literary fable, science fiction, childhood memory, care, and technological nostalgia. The anthology places her after Chen Qiufan to shift from gritty near-future social pressure toward ghosts, robots, museums, elderly parents, automata, children's perception, and questions about whether machines can preserve tenderness. Source anchors: Xia Jia; fable; childhood memory; care; robots; automata.

Analysis: Xia Jia and fable change the anthology's emotional register without leaving technology behind. Childhood memory and care make machines intimate rather than merely instrumental, while robots and automata carry cultural memory. The section matters because it broadens Chinese SF beyond development satire into stories about affection, aging, and remembrance.

A HUNDRED GHOSTS PARADE TONIGHT

Summary: "A Hundred Ghosts Parade Tonight" is narrated by Ning, a boy living among mechanical ghosts in an abandoned attraction called Ghost Street. He is raised by figures such as Xiaoqian, Demon, and other ghostly caretakers, gradually confronting what kind of being he is, why tourists no longer come, and how love can persist among artificial bodies, staged hauntings, and obsolete entertainment. Source anchors: Hundred Ghosts; Ning; Ghost Street; Xiaoqian; Demon; mechanical ghosts.

Analysis: Hundred Ghosts and Ghost Street turn obsolescence into family structure. Ning and Xiaoqian matter because the story's emotional question is not whether mechanical ghosts are real, but whether care given by artificial beings can form a child. Demon and the mechanical ghosts practice Knowledge Preservation through obsolete bodies: the abandoned attraction outlives its market function because its machines still carry stories, roles, affection, and the routines that make Ning's moral world possible.

TONGTONG'S SUMMER

Summary: "Tongtong's Summer" follows a girl whose grandfather is cared for through a telepresence or robotic system that lets aging, family duty, and distance be renegotiated. Tongtong, her parents, Grandpa, the care device, summer routines, and domestic frictions create a near-future household where technology does not solve aging but redistributes attention, dignity, dependence, and companionship. Source anchors: Tongtong; summer; Grandpa; care robot; parents; aging.

Analysis: Tongtong and Grandpa make elder care concrete rather than policy abstraction, because the household has to decide who sits with an aging man and what kind of presence counts as care. The care robot and parents expose Informal Institutions inside the family: expectations of filial duty allocate attention before any formal care system does, while distance and work make those obligations harder to meet. The device helps Grandpa by redistributing companionship, but it also reveals the guilt and bargaining that already govern dependency.

NIGHT JOURNEY OF THE DRAGON-HORSE

Summary: "Night Journey of the Dragon-Horse" follows an ancient mechanical dragon-horse through a transformed world where ruins, stars, memory, and machines outlast the civilization that made them. The dragon-horse, a bat companion, mechanical heritage, urban remnants, cosmic time, and mythic imagery combine into a journey story about endurance after human centrality has faded. Source anchors: Dragon-Horse; bat; mechanical heritage; ruins; cosmic time; journey.

Analysis: Dragon-Horse and mechanical heritage give Xia Jia's technology a mythic timescale. The bat and journey prevent the story from becoming static allegory, while ruins and cosmic time ask what machines carry after their makers are gone. The piece matters because it links Chinese technological imagination to older forms of legend and pilgrimage.

MA BOYONG

Summary: Ma Boyong's section introduces a writer using alternate history, satire, bureaucratic absurdity, and political constraint as speculative material. His selection, "The City of Silence," brings the anthology into explicit contact with censorship, language control, online life, forbidden words, administrative euphemism, and the shrinking of expression under a security state. Source anchors: Ma Boyong; satire; City of Silence; censorship; forbidden words; security state.

Analysis: Ma Boyong and satire make control systems legible through absurd procedure. City of Silence and censorship connect speculative fiction to everyday linguistic governance, while forbidden words and security state pressure show how politics can enter syntax. The section matters because it treats language itself as infrastructure that can be regulated, degraded, and weaponized.

THE CITY OF SILENCE

Summary: "The City of Silence" follows Arvardan through a society where approved vocabulary continually contracts and citizens must speak through permitted phrases, substitute labels, and monitored channels. The story's devices include the visible erasure of titles, the placeholder T-I-T-L-E, online filtering, state euphemisms, and a protagonist trying to maintain thought and contact as language is technically and politically compressed. Source anchors: Arvardan; approved vocabulary; T-I-T-L-E; online filtering; state euphemisms; language control.

Analysis: Arvardan and approved vocabulary make censorship bodily and cognitive: the issue is not just banned opinion but shrinking interior life. T-I-T-L-E and online filtering turn Language and Ideology into executable rules, because software removes words before citizens can use them to coordinate dissent or even describe experience. State euphemisms complete the mechanism by making administrative obedience sound like ordinary speech, so the security state engineers silence as a public environment.

HAO JINGFANG

Summary: Hao Jingfang's section frames a writer moving between philosophical fable, social modeling, economics, and literary speculation. The anthology pairs "Invisible Planets," a sequence of invented worlds, with "Folding Beijing," a near-future class dystopia, so that abstract planetary parables sit beside a concrete city machine organized by labor, waste, time, and unequal access. Source anchors: Hao Jingfang; Invisible Planets; Folding Beijing; social modeling; economics; class dystopia.

Analysis: Hao Jingfang and social modeling give the anthology its most explicit bridge between fable and political economy. Invisible Planets abstracts civilization into small diagrams, while Folding Beijing and economics put inequality into urban mechanics. Class dystopia matters because Hao's fiction asks how spatial design can naturalize hierarchy.

INVISIBLE PLANETS

Summary: "Invisible Planets" presents a catalogue of imagined planets such as Chichi Raha, Pimaceh, Bingwaugh, Amiyachi and Aihuowu, Lunaji, Yanyanni, Tisu Ati and Lutikawulu, Chincato, and Jinjialin. Each world operates like a compact philosophical model, using customs, bodies, cities, memory, love, time, or perception to make one social or metaphysical arrangement briefly visible. Source anchors: Chichi Raha; Pimaceh; Bingwaugh; Lunaji; Chincato; Jinjialin.

Analysis: Chichi Raha, Pimaceh, and Bingwaugh establish the story's Calvino-like method: planet as thought experiment. Lunaji, Chincato, and Jinjialin keep the sequence from settling into one allegory by varying the governing premise each time. The story matters because it makes worldbuilding a tool for comparing forms of life rather than for building a single plot.

FOLDING BEIJING

Summary: "Folding Beijing" follows Lao Dao, a waste worker in Third Space, as he carries a message across a city divided into time-shared physical layers for different classes. His route brings him into contact with Qin Tian, Yi Yan, Peng Li, the folding city mechanism, waste processing, sleep cycles, and a harsh allocation of hours that gives First Space leisure and Third Space exhaustion. Source anchors: Lao Dao; Third Space; Qin Tian; Yi Yan; folding city; waste worker.

Analysis: Lao Dao and Third Space make inequality spatial, temporal, and bodily, because the poor do not merely have less money but receive a different Beijing and fewer waking hours inside it. Qin Tian and Yi Yan show that cross-class contact exists, but the folding city forces their messages, payments, and desires through Lao Dao's dangerous movement between layers. The mechanism turns Order and Governance into architecture: timed rotation assigns air, space, sleep, and mobility by class, while the waste worker's hidden labor keeps the elegant allocation system functioning.

TANG FEI

Summary: Tang Fei's section introduces a writer represented here by "Call Girl," a story about fantasy, intimacy, performance, and commodified narrative. The anthology uses Tang Fei to add a stranger and more interior mode, where speculative service work, desire, storytelling, client relations, and unstable identity replace the larger civic machines of Ma Boyong and Hao Jingfang. Source anchors: Tang Fei; Call Girl; fantasy; service work; storytelling; desire.

Analysis: Tang Fei and Call Girl matter because they move speculation into emotional labor. Fantasy and storytelling become things purchased and performed, while service work and desire connect private longing to market exchange. The section broadens the anthology's economic concerns from factories, cities, and censorship to intimacy as a commodity.

CALL GIRL

Summary: "Call Girl" centers on a woman whose work involves providing experiences, fantasies, or stories rather than a simple conventional service, so the title's expected sexual transaction is displaced into staged imagination. The story uses clients, invented identities, dreamlike episodes, performance, payment, emotional ambiguity, and mediated intimacy to examine what people actually buy when they buy escape from ordinary life. Source anchors: Call Girl; clients; fantasies; performance; payment; mediated intimacy.

Analysis: Call Girl and clients make demand visible: the buyer wants an arranged experience, but the arrangement also exposes loneliness. Fantasies and performance blur labor with self-invention, while payment and mediated intimacy make the transaction morally unstable. The story matters because it treats imagination as both refuge and exploitative market.

CHENG JINGBO

Summary: Cheng Jingbo's section introduces the anthology's most lyrical and mythic register through "Grave of the Fireflies," moving away from the visibly engineered cities and bureaucracies of the preceding selections. Her contribution uses cosmic settings, memory, luminous imagery, childhood or fairy-tale tones, loss, long-duration feeling, and space fantasy to expand the collection beyond urban systems and social satire into a more elegiac mode. Source anchors: Cheng Jingbo; Grave of the Fireflies; cosmic settings; memory; loss; space fantasy.

Analysis: Cheng Jingbo and Grave of the Fireflies give the anthology a mode of wonder that is less interested in mechanism than in atmosphere, but the placement still performs concrete editorial work. Cosmic settings and memory treat science-fictional scale as a vessel for grief rather than as a puzzle to solve. Loss and space fantasy matter because they show Chinese SF using beauty and melancholy alongside critique, so the collection does not let Beijing, censorship, or computation define the whole field.

GRAVE OF THE FIREFLIES

Summary: "Grave of the Fireflies" unfolds as a luminous cosmic tale concerned with memory, death, travel, and fragile lights set against enormous distances rather than a conventional puzzle plot. Its fireflies, grave imagery, spacefaring frame, remembered figures, elegiac voice, mythic compression, and fragile lights make the story feel closer to a prose poem than to engineering-centered SF or social extrapolation. Source anchors: fireflies; grave; spacefaring; memory; elegiac voice; fragile lights.

Analysis: Fireflies and grave imagery make scale intimate: the story's cosmic setting does not dwarf grief but gives it a field in which to glow. Spacefaring and memory hold together movement and mourning, so travel does not erase the dead but carries them into a larger imagined sky. Elegiac voice and fragile lights explain the story's place in the anthology as a counterweight to harder social extrapolation, especially after stories built around censorship, class machinery, or computation.

LIU CIXIN

Summary: Liu Cixin's section introduces the best-known hard-SF writer in the collection, represented by stories that combine grand scale with clean conceptual engines. "The Circle" turns ancient Qin politics into computation, while "Taking Care of God" imagines humanity meeting its aged creators; together they use King of Qin, Jing Ke, human processors, alien ancestors, obligation, and civilizational time. Source anchors: Liu Cixin; The Circle; Taking Care of God; King of Qin; human processors; alien ancestors.

Analysis: Liu Cixin and The Circle bring the anthology's largest engineering imagination to the foreground. King of Qin and human processors show that computation can be staged through bodies and command, while Taking Care of God and alien ancestors turn cosmic origin into social burden. His section matters because scale becomes a way to test civilization's self-image.

THE CIRCLE

Summary: "The Circle" reworks the Jing Ke assassination story by having a scholar demonstrate computation to the King of Qin through a vast formation of people acting as logic elements. The chapter's machinery includes the King of Qin, Jing Ke, a human computer, military discipline, binary operations, predictive ambition, and the political danger of showing a ruler that people can be made into an information machine. Source anchors: Jing Ke; King of Qin; human computer; binary operations; army; prediction.

Analysis: Jing Ke and King of Qin connect mythic history to Information and Coordination. The human computer and binary operations turn soldiers into circuitry, while the army's command structure synchronizes thousands of bodies into a calculating machine. Prediction gives that coordination political danger: the ruler wants the knowledge produced by collective computation but fears any organized system powerful enough to calculate beyond his personal control.

TAKING CARE OF GOD

Summary: "Taking Care of God" imagines elderly alien creators arriving on Earth and asking humanity, their distant descendants, for care after the awe of first contact gives way to logistics. The story follows the Gods' arrival, their placement with families, generational resentment, public burden-sharing, decaying bodies, technological disappointment, creators who can no longer provide wonders, and the emotional reversal by which makers become dependents. Source anchors: Gods; Earth; families; creators; dependents; public burden.

Analysis: Gods and creators invert the usual theology of dependence, because the beings who should command reverence arrive as frail elders who need beds, food, patience, and bureaucratic allocation. Earth and families turn that reversal into a Moral Economy dispute: past creation establishes a claim on present care, but dependents and public burden force households to decide how much gratitude they owe when obligation becomes expensive. Liu uses civilizational scale to show reverence collapsing into resentment once symbolic debt requires daily labor.

ESSAYS

Summary: The essay section gathers reflective pieces by Liu Cixin, Chen Qiufan, and Xia Jia on Chinese science fiction, genre history, modernization, translation, readership, and cultural difference. The essays discuss possible universes, China's torn generation, the question of what makes Chinese SF Chinese, global genre inheritance, local experience, and the danger of simplifying a diverse field into national essence. Source anchors: essays; Liu Cixin; Chen Qiufan; Xia Jia; Chinese SF; torn generation.

Analysis: Essays by Liu Cixin, Chen Qiufan, and Xia Jia convert the anthology from sample platter into self-interpretation. Their disagreement makes Chinese SF a problem of National Identity rather than a fixed cultural essence: translation, global genre inheritance, local markets, and the torn generation all compete to define what the label includes. Rapid development supplies shared pressure without producing one national style, so the essays block readers from treating diverse stories as interchangeable evidence about China.

ABOUT THE TRANSLATOR AND EDITOR

Summary: The back matter identifies Ken Liu as translator and editor, noting his work as writer, lawyer, programmer, and translator of major Chinese SF into English. It places Invisible Planets inside a broader translation project that includes Cixin Liu's Three-Body trilogy, story introductions, copyright acknowledgments, publication histories, and the practical infrastructure that lets these works circulate to Anglophone readers. Source anchors: Ken Liu; translator; editor; Three-Body trilogy; copyright acknowledgments; Anglophone readers.

Analysis: Ken Liu's work as translator and editor is not incidental metadata; it supplies the anthology's mechanism of Knowledge Preservation. The Three-Body trilogy and Anglophone readers show how translation carries stories into a wider market without making circulation automatic. Copyright acknowledgments identify the magazines, translators, authors, and permissions that preserve provenance while moving contemporary Chinese SF across languages.
