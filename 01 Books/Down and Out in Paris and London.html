Position in the vault

It stands beside Orwell's other published notes — Burmese Days, Keep the Aspidistra Flying, Homage to Catalonia, and Nineteen Eighty-Four — and feeds the State, Order, and Legibility cluster through its study of vagrancy law, casual wards, hotel hierarchy, and the institutions that process the poor. Its poverty-economy material threads it into the vault's concept network (Moral Economy, Coercive Labor, Commercial Society, Illicit Markets).

Detailed overview

George Orwell's Down and Out in Paris and London is built as a movement from bohemian poverty in Paris to institutionalized homelessness in England. The Paris half begins in the Rue du Coq d'Or, the Hôtel des Trois Moineaux, cheap bistros, pawnshops, English lessons, Boris's schemes, and the narrator's slide from precarious respectability into hunger. Orwell's method is concrete before it is argumentative: rent advances, thirty centimes, pawned clothes, bread, Gauloises Bleu, hotel cellars, and service entrances make poverty a sequence of small bodily and administrative humiliations.

The Paris restaurant chapters turn the book into an anatomy of invisible labor. At the Hôtel X. and later the Auberge de Jehan Cottard, Orwell studies plongeurs, waiters, cooks, cafeteries, pantries, dining-room washing-up, rush hours, heat, noise, quarrels, moustache etiquette, and the hierarchy separating cooks, waiters, and dishwashers. The chapter on the social significance of the plongeur is the hinge: Orwell stops narrating only his own hardship and asks why a modern city requires men to spend their lives in underground heat doing work that preserves luxury upstairs.

The London half changes the institution but keeps the same bodily logic. The narrator arrives through Dunkirk and Tilbury with eight shillings, sleeps in cheap lodging houses on Waterloo Road and Pennyfields, then enters the world of casual wards, spikes, tea-and-two-slices, tobacco tins, Romton, Edbury, Lower Binfield, the Embankment, Rowton Houses, Salvation Army shelters, and walking rules under the Vagrancy Act. Paris poverty is frantic work and hunger; London poverty is enforced motion, waiting, dirt, and the legal management of men who have nowhere to sleep.

Orwell's portraits prevent the book from becoming a sociology diagram. Boris, Charlie, Valenti, Mario, Jules, Roucolle, Paddy, Bozo, Bill the moocher, the Irish tramp at Romton, the Chittagonian lascar, the struck-off doctor, and old Grandpa all carry specific speech, habits, pride, and delusions. The result is not sentimental. Orwell often records cruelty, dirt, anti-Semitic talk, misogyny, exhaustion, and absurdity, but he insists that poverty is not a moral essence. It is a condition that narrows choices, deforms manners, and exposes how much social respectability depends on a few coins, a bed, clean clothes, and access to food.

The book's structure also explains Orwell's political development. He moves from anecdote to analysis: first the quarrels of the Rue du Coq d'Or, then hunger, then hotel labor, then the claim that plongeurs are modern slaves, then the claim that tramps are not the monstrous idlers of respectable imagination. By the end, after Paddy goes toward Portsmouth and Bozo is in Wandsworth for begging, the narrator's troubles end only because a friend lends money and a job appears. The escape is personal, not structural, which is why the final chapters turn so heavily toward lodging, slang, casual wards, and the institutions that keep poverty reproducible.

Major people, societies, and motivations

The extract is presented as first-person autobiography-as-social-document (Eric Blair writing under the pseudonym George Orwell), so this section records the memoir's central people and the societies they move through.

  • Orwell / the narrator (Eric Blair): a bankrupted young Englishman in Paris who has "come down in the world"; his motivation is documented in the extract's front matter as a deliberate immersion — "I wanted to submerge myself, to get right down among the oppressed, to be one of them and on their side against their tyrants." He slides from tutor-and-roomer respectability through hunger to hotel scullion, then crosses to England to live the tramp's life under the Vagrancy Act. His arc runs from private humiliation to a political and sociological claim about poverty.
  • Boris: the Paris half's central companion — a fat, lame Russian ex-officer of the Second Siberian Rifles, once a waiter counting a hundred francs a day in tips at the Hôtel Scribe. He lives on military memory, elaborate schemes, and a waiter's conviction that he will one day own a restaurant on the Right Bank. His motivation is survival through performance and hope; he teaches the narrator the two-man campaign of job-seeking, borrowing, and pawnshop strategy.
  • Paddy Jaques: the London half's mate — a thirty-five-year-old Irish tramp in a tweed shooting-jacket and old evening trousers whose braid he keeps re-sewn, typical of the tens of thousands on the road. His two subjects of conversation are the shame of being a tramp and the best way of getting a free meal; Orwell reads his ruin as malnutrition, not native vice.
  • Bozo the screever: a pavement artist near Waterloo Bridge with a deformed foot, a "Roman" profile (Vespasian), an exact Cockneyed intelligence, and a philosophy of "I'm a free man in here." He is the book's counter-example to the tramp-monster; his motivation is intellectual independence, pride, and a principled refusal of both thrift and gratitude.
  • Charlie and the Coq d'Or quarter: the quarter's talkers and eccentrics — Charlie the remittance-man youth whose improvised performances about love and hunger keep the bistro amused, alongside Madame F., Azaya, Marinette, Fureux, the Rougiers, Henri, and R. The bistro and its lodgers form the society Orwell calls his first object-lesson in poverty.
  • The Paris plongeur and hotel world: the multinational underclass of the Hôtel X. and the Auberge de Jehan Cottard — Italians, Germans, Magyars, Arabs, Russians, and others working eleven- to eighteen-hour days in cellars. Their motivation is simple subsistence ("the boulot, drinks and sleep"); Orwell treats them as slaves of the modern world whose labor preserves luxury upstairs.
  • The tramps and the spike world: the casual-ward population processed through Romton, Edbury, Cromley, Lower Binfield, and the London shelters — men kept in motion by a law that admits them one night at a time. They hide tobacco in their boots, hunt cigarette ends, and survive on the tea-and-two-slices.
  • The lodging-house London: the commercial dormitories of Pennyfields, Bow, Lambeth, and the Strand, run by deputies for rich owners with cash due by seven — a largely male, maritime, multiethnic society of stevedores, pensioners, lascars, screevers, beggars, and petty thieves.
  • The poverty economy: the web that ties all of the above together — pawnshops, second-hand shops, cafés that double as employment bureaus, meal tickets, free-tea charities, and the spike's sixpenny rations. It is the "world that awaits you if you are ever penniless" that the book set out to document.

Themes and concepts to track

  • The arithmetic of poverty: exact francs, shillings, and pence as the book's basic grammar, from six francs a day to half a crown a day to a ten-shilling pension.
  • Invisible labor behind visible luxury: the service door, the cellar, and the dining room as two economies separated by a wall.
  • Poverty as performance: the lies, appearances, and etiquette that must be maintained; the tramp's sham trades.
  • Institutional processing: the spike, the casual ward, the shelter, and the lodging house as machines for moving, feeding, and classifying men.
  • The tramp-monster vs. structural explanation: dismantling the stereotype of idleness by pointing to law and lodging policy.
  • Language and social category: slang, song, and labels ("gentleman", "blackguard", "mate") as evidence of how class is spoken and worn.
  • Empire arriving home: lascars, Indians, and the colonial shipping economy inside London's cheapest lodgings.
  • The limits of charity: free tea with prayers attached, and the resentment it produces.

Core concepts

  • Coercive Labor: hotel kitchens trap plongeurs in exhausting work that sustains luxury while leaving little time or money to escape.
  • Legibility: casual wards, grooming rules, lodging prices, and vagrancy law classify and process poor bodies.
  • Informal Institutions: bistros, tramp expertise, slang, lodging houses, and street micro-economies help people survive systems that offer little security.
  • Moral Economy: Orwell challenges the respectable claim that poverty reveals bad character rather than constrained choices.
  • Language and Ideology: slang, grooming rules, class labels, and tramp stereotypes show how vocabulary turns poverty into a managed social category.
  • State Capacity: spikes, the Vagrancy Act, casual-ward rules, charity shelters, and police movement orders reveal a state able to process destitution without solving it.
  • Commercial Society: lodging houses, bistros, hotels, pawnshops, and the Twopenny Hangover show market exchange continuing even at the floor of poverty.
  • Illicit Markets: the Roucolle cocaine scheme, staff pilfering, glimming, and begging-as-legalized-crime interlock poverty with crime at every level.
  • Political Economy: hotel economics, "money as the grand test of virtue," and the fear of the mob that keeps useless work in place.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter I

Summary: Orwell opens at seven in the morning in the Rue du Coq d'Or, Paris, with Madame Monce shouting from the pavement at a lodger who has been squashing bugs on hotel wallpaper. The quarrel draws windows open across the street, then stops when cavalry rides past; the narrator uses the scene to introduce the Hôtel des Trois Moineaux, Madame F., Poles, Arabs, Italians, Bulgarians, Russians, the Sorbonne, Montparnasse, and the noisy mixed life of the quarter. Source anchors: Rue du Coq d'Or; Madame Monce; bugs; cavalry; Trois Moineaux; Montparnasse.

Analysis: Rue du Coq d'Or and Madame Monce give the book its first social fact: poverty is loud, public, and crowded. The bugs and cavalry show Orwell's eye for abrupt switches between squalor and spectacle. Trois Moineaux and Montparnasse matter because the quarter is not merely a slum; it is a cosmopolitan rooming-house world where migrants, workers, students, and failures live beside one another, held together by hotels, bistros, and improvised trades that function as an Informal Institutions tissue society barely recognizes.

Chapter II

Summary: The second chapter moves into the bistro below the Hôtel des Trois Moineaux, a half-underground brick-floored room with wine-soaked tables, Madame F. drinking Malaga, red-sashed workmen carving sausage, songs about "Madelon" and strawberries, games of dice for aperitifs, and public lovemaking. Orwell presents Charlie, an educated young local curiosity, telling stories among the crowd — here a long, self-dramatizing tale of a thousand francs spent on a girl in a red cellar — while comparing the room's cheerfulness to English pubs. Source anchors: Hôtel des Trois Moineaux; Madame F.; Malaga; Madelon; Charlie; bistro.

Analysis: Hôtel des Trois Moineaux and Madame F. show a poor social institution that is still warm, theatrical, and communal. Malaga and Madelon turn the bistro into a place where cheap drink and songs sustain sociability. Charlie matters because Orwell's Paris poverty is full of talkers and performers, not silent victims, and because the bistro lets hardship become shared noise rather than private shame; the dice-for-aperitifs play and the cheap-drink economy keep a small Commercial Society running at the foot of the hotel.

Chapter III

Summary: Orwell explains that he has lived in the Coq d'Or quarter for about eighteen months when his money falls to four hundred and fifty francs plus thirty-six francs a week from English lessons. He pays two hundred francs rent in advance, plans to find work as a tourist guide or interpreter, then is derailed when a dubious Italian compositor steals money; he begins calculating meals, bread, Luxembourg Gardens walks, and the difference between temporary economy and real hunger. Source anchors: Coq d'Or; four hundred fifty francs; English lessons; Italian compositor; Luxembourg Gardens; bread.

Analysis: Coq d'Or and four hundred fifty francs turn poverty into arithmetic. English lessons and the Italian compositor show how precarious respectability can disappear through one lost income and one theft. Coq d'Or and four hundred fifty francs turn poverty into the arithmetic of a shilling a day — an Commercial Society reduced to bread, rye loaves, and coffee-with-a-dead-fly transactions. Luxembourg Gardens and bread matter because Orwell's descent begins not with drama but with shrinking routines around walking, eating, and delaying panic, and the lies poverty forces on him — to the laundress, the tobacconist, the baker — are a first Moral Economy discovery: respectability must be performed with coins one does not have.

Chapter IV

Summary: When the English lessons stop, Orwell is left with thirty centimes and no tobacco, goes hungry for a day and a half, and pawns his remaining clothes. The pawnshop's grand portals marked with republican slogans contrast with the humiliating transaction; Madame F. is surprised he asks before removing his suitcase, and soon Boris enters the story as a lame Russian ex-officer from the Second Siberian Rifles who knows Les Halles, the Scribe, and the Right Bank job world. Source anchors: thirty centimes; pawnshop; Madame F.; Boris; Second Siberian Rifles; Les Halles.

Analysis: Thirty centimes and the pawnshop mark the end of pretending to have funds. Madame F. matters because hotel respectability depends on visible possessions and permission to remove them. Boris and Second Siberian Rifles shift the plot from solitary decline to partnership with another displaced man who lives on memory, rank, and schemes, giving Orwell poverty as a two-man campaign instead of a private embarrassment. The "Liberté, Egalité, Fraternité" portals frame a stark Commercial Society transaction — ten pounds' worth of clothes yields seventy francs — and Boris's promise of a dishwashing job points toward the Coercive Labor market the Paris half will anatomize.

Chapter V

Summary: Orwell visits Boris at the Rue du Marché des Blancs Manteaux expecting comfort and possible restaurant work, but finds a filthy hotel, Bouillon Zip smells, and Boris poorer than he imagined. Boris talks of the Hôtel Scribe, Gauloises Bleu, a possible job, a Jew at the Pont d'Austerlitz, and the art of borrowing, while Orwell discovers that hope can be another way of spending scarce energy and money. Source anchors: Boris; Rue du Marché; Bouillon Zip; Hôtel Scribe; Gauloises Bleu; Pont d'Austerlitz.

Analysis: Boris and Rue du Marché expose the gap between the stories poor men tell and the rooms they actually inhabit. Bouillon Zip and Gauloises Bleu make deprivation sensory: cheap soup smell and premature tobacco optimism. Pont d'Austerlitz matters because Boris's job-hunting network is entangled with prejudice, rumor, and improvised credit, showing how desperation recruits any possible contact, and the cafés off the Rue de Rivoli that act as employment bureaus, the borrowed coats, and the one-drink-per-two-hours etiquette form an Informal Institutions substitute for anything the official labor market offers.

Chapter VI

Summary: Orwell and Boris fail to find work for three weeks, walking Paris at two miles an hour, crossing the Seine eleven times, waiting at service doorways, and being rejected because Boris is lame and Orwell lacks experience. Boris tries to hide his limp, talks of Frenchmen, Italians, Englishmen, Les Halles, and managers, and repeatedly turns discouragement into theatrical optimism addressed to his "little cherished wolf." Source anchors: Boris; three weeks; Seine; service doorways; limp; Les Halles.

Analysis: Boris and three weeks turn unemployment into repetitive motion through the city. The Seine and service doorways show the geography of job-seeking from below: one approaches kitchens by back entrances and waits for managers. Boris's limp matters because bodily impairment becomes an immediate labor-market disqualification, no matter how much military bearing or bravado he can perform, so survival runs on Informal Institutions improvisation — agency notices, the Les Halles porter test, letters to ex-mistresses, a patron saint and a fifty-centime candle — rather than on wages.

Chapter VII

Summary: Orwell's money falls from eight francs to four, one, and then twenty-five centimes, leaving him on dry bread and eventually two and a half days without food. He fishes unsuccessfully in the Seine, reflects on involuntary hunger versus fasting cures, reads or thinks of Sherlock Holmes, and later joins Boris in schemes involving the Rue des Francs Bourgeois, a Jew, and job strategy under the pressure of actual starvation. Source anchors: twenty-five centimes; dry bread; Seine fishing; hunger; Sherlock Holmes; Rue des Francs Bourgeois.

Analysis: Twenty-five centimes and dry bread make hunger a measurable threshold. Seine fishing shows how quickly a city dweller becomes reduced to unlikely subsistence tactics. Sherlock Holmes and Rue des Francs Bourgeois matter because imagination and plotting continue even when the body is failing, but neither substitutes for food or reverses the physical dullness Orwell records. The pawnshop's demands for passports, cardboard boxes, and identity papers, and the "ruse de guerre" that needs Le Sport to distract the patron, prove how thoroughly Commercial Society still governs the poor, while the claim that poverty annihilates the future — the man with three francs worries less than the man with a hundred — is an early Moral Economy assertion.

Chapter VIII

Summary: With twenty-eight francs in hand from Boris's borrowing, the two resume job hunting and Orwell sketches Russian refugees in Paris: ex-officers, waiters, dishwashers, taxi drivers, garage owners, dancing-hall operators, men living on women, and aristocrats fallen into dependence. Boris tells stories of Russians, Bolsheviks, Moscow, Communists, and biblical analogies while contrasting Russian endurance with what he imagines Englishmen would tolerate. Source anchors: twenty-eight francs; Russian refugees; ex-officers; Boris; Bolsheviks; Moscow.

Analysis: Twenty-eight francs gives the pair just enough energy to reenter the job market. Russian refugees and ex-officers place Boris inside a post-revolution diaspora with its own hierarchies and humiliations. Bolsheviks and Moscow matter because political catastrophe becomes biography: the Russian past explains the strange dignity and absurdity of men now begging kitchen work. The "secret society" that promises a hundred and fifty francs an article and vanishes after taking an entrance fee turns out to be an Illicit Markets swindle preying on the refugees, and its password rituals and Lenin-poster stagecraft show Language and Ideology working as conspiratorial theatre.

Chapter IX

Summary: Orwell and Boris continue looking for work, with hopes at the Hôtel X near the Place de la Concorde and the delayed Auberge de Jehan Cottard in the Rue du Commerce. Boris worries over appearance, collars, hunger, and how to impress the patron; the new restaurant promises medieval color, Charlemagne atmosphere, a salon, the Jardin, and future meals, but its actual opening remains uncertain. Source anchors: Hôtel X; Place de la Concorde; Auberge de Jehan Cottard; Rue du Commerce; Boris; appearance.

Analysis: Hôtel X and Place de la Concorde represent the established hotel economy, while Auberge de Jehan Cottard offers fantasy employment. Boris and appearance show how poverty damages the very surface needed to get hired. Rue du Commerce matters because the promised restaurant becomes a recurring trap: hope is always "tomorrow," and tomorrow keeps hungry men obedient to rumor. Appearance is itself a Moral Economy injury — the patron reads cheeks and collars instead of skill — and the Auberge's "picturesque" fraud of a Charlemagne inn turns the race for work into commerce in illusion.

Chapter X

Summary: Orwell begins at the Hôtel X, entering through a dark service doorway beside the grand façade and being hired because the staff wants to practise English for American clients. The chef du personnel, an Italian, questions his experience, sees through his hands, and sends him into the scullion world of greasy trousers, Chateaubriand, Charenton men, English obscenities, and hotel hierarchy. Source anchors: Hôtel X; service entrance; chef du personnel; English clients; Chateaubriand; scullions.

Analysis: Hôtel X and service entrance make the split between luxury front and labor back physically visible. Chef du personnel and English clients show how nationality can become a small employment advantage even when experience is false. Scullions make Coercive Labor visible beneath fine dining: the hotel's polished front depends on workers customers rarely see, and the day that ends with Orwell engaged as an "extra" at twenty-five francs and searched for stolen food shows Commercial Society pricing each body by the shift.

Chapter XI

Summary: Orwell works at the Hôtel X while the Auberge delays for six weeks, spending four days in the cafeterie, one day helping the fourth-floor waiter, and one day washing for the dining room. He describes eleven- and fourteen-hour days, Sunday as the nominal day off, fearful heat in labyrinthine cellars, Mario the Italian, a Magyar, a Transylvanian, Rigoletto singing, Zola-like interiors, and hotel work that is hard chiefly because of heat, speed, and confinement. Source anchors: Hôtel X; cafeterie; fourteen-hour day; Mario; Magyar; cellars.

Analysis: Hôtel X and cafeterie show a well-organized institution that still depends on punishing labor. Fourteen-hour day and cellars make the cost bodily rather than theoretical. Mario and the Magyar matter because Orwell's workplace is a multinational underclass whose songs and quarrels humanize the machinery of service, making hotel luxury depend on immigrant exhaustion below stairs — a concentrated instance of Coercive Labor in which a cafeterie thermometer near 130 degrees and fifteen miles of walking are priced into the client's breakfast.

Chapter XII

Summary: Orwell's best time at the hotel is assisting Valenti, the fourth-floor waiter, in a cool pantry connected by service lifts. Valenti polishes silver and glasses, tips Orwell five francs on good days, wears black tailcoat and white tie like an Eton boy, and explains the distance between waiters and plongeurs; the pantry offers a humane contrast to the cellars and plate-racks of Paris hotel work. Source anchors: Valenti; fourth floor; pantry; service lifts; five francs; Eton.

Analysis: Valenti and fourth floor reveal the small gradations inside hotel servitude. Pantry and service lifts show that architecture distributes dignity as much as labor. Five francs and Eton matter because the waiter performs gentility while depending on the same brutal system that keeps plongeurs below him, and his kindness is constrained by the need to act superior in public — a prestige code of the sort Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy describes running from the service door to the dining room.

Chapter XIII

Summary: On his third day, the chef du personnel orders Orwell to shave his moustache because a plongeur must not wear one. Boris explains that only cooks wear moustaches and that the rule is custom; Orwell uses the incident to map hotel caste distinctions among cooks, waiters, plongeurs, French, Italians, Germans, Arabs, Greeks, Armenians, Sundays, and the superiority signs maintained by those just above the bottom. Source anchors: moustache; chef du personnel; Boris; cooks; waiters; plongeurs.

Analysis: Moustache and chef du personnel turn class hierarchy into grooming regulation. Boris and cooks show that the rule is not rational hygiene but status etiquette. Waiters and plongeurs expose Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy inside the workplace: symbolic privileges separate one rank of servant from another and reproduce domination among the dominated — and the wage ladder Orwell records, from the head cook's five thousand francs a month down to the cafetier's five hundred, quantifies the Coercive Labor the prestige order organizes.

Chapter XIV

Summary: Orwell explains the operating logic of the hotel: rush-hour noise, disorder, quarrels, last-minute cooking, steaks that cannot be grilled early, waiters shouting, cooks swearing, and everyone doing two men's work during service. He compares hotel labor to shops and factories, cites Valenti, Mario, Hamlet jokes, Grand Boulevard service, German and English customers, and the necessity of organized fury in feeding diners. Source anchors: hotel rush; quarrels; steaks; waiters; cooks; Valenti.

Analysis: Hotel rush and quarrels make apparent chaos functional. Steaks and cooks show why restaurant work cannot be smoothed into steady factory rhythm. Valenti and waiters matter because the public elegance of service depends on private verbal violence and compressed labor below, so the dining room's calm is manufactured by panic in the service quarters. The chapter's economics — waiters paying for employment on the Grand Boulevard, two hundred francs a night for lodging, the rule that the more one pays the more sweat and spittle one eats — is a compact Commercial Society audit of the luxury trade.

Chapter XV

Summary: Orwell records hotel stories from Mario and Valenti: dope fiends, old debauchees, thefts, blackmail, a diamond ring stolen from an American woman and baked into a roll by a chambermaid's lover, and Valenti's own experience of going five days without food. The chapter also includes rue Sainte stories, religious exclamations, hunger boasting, and the dark folklore of service workers. Source anchors: Mario; Valenti; diamond ring; American lady; bakery; five days.

Analysis: Mario and Valenti turn the hotel into an oral archive of scandal and survival. Diamond ring and bakery show how theft can exploit the building's own labor channels. Five days matters because Valenti's story collapses the distance between waiter respectability and the hunger Orwell has just endured, making starvation a recurring occupational risk rather than a freak accident, and the diamond ring baked into a roll by a chambermaid's lover, with the thefts, dope fiends, and blackmail circulating among the staff, reveals an Illicit Markets layer inside the grand hotel.

Chapter XVI

Summary: Time passes while the Auberge de Jehan Cottard still fails to open, its patron borrows five francs from Orwell, and a Baltic Russian cook claims a background as a singer and a love of Uncle Tom's Cabin. Orwell becomes used to the Métro, trams, Rue du Commerce, Rue de Sèvres, Eiffel Tower views, Arab Sunday drinking, raki, work, sleep, and the repetitive Paris routine that makes even exploitation feel normal. Source anchors: Auberge de Jehan Cottard; five francs; Baltic Russian; Uncle Tom's Cabin; Métro; raki.

Analysis: Auberge de Jehan Cottard and five francs reveal the promised job as almost comic unreliability. The Baltic Russian and Uncle Tom's Cabin add another displaced figure to Orwell's Paris underworld. Métro and raki matter because the city continues around exhausted workers, whose routine becomes so absorbing that larger landmarks barely register — a rhythm that exemplifies Coercive Labor: the boulot, drinks and sleep leave no room for anything else, so complete that not even a murder beneath the window interrupts the plongeur's night.

Chapter XVII

Summary: With thirty francs a week for drinks, Orwell rejoins the social life of the Trois Moineaux bistro on Saturdays. The chapter gives songs such as the "Marseillaise," "Internationale," "Madelon," and "Les Fraises et les Framboises," Azaya the glass-factory worker, Marinette the Corsican, the Rougiers, Charlie, Manuel the Spaniard, Madame F., Louis, Fureux the Limousin, Place Monge politics, Communists, and chauvinists. Source anchors: thirty francs; Trois Moineaux; Marseillaise; Azaya; Marinette; Place Monge.

Analysis: Thirty francs and Trois Moineaux show that a small drinking budget restores social citizenship. Marseillaise and Azaya connect politics, song, labor, and flirtation in the same smoky room. Marinette and Place Monge matter because Orwell's poor quarter is politically argumentative and sexually theatrical, not merely hungry, and drink purchases buy access to that world. The bistro acts as an Informal Institutions parliament where the "Marseillaise" and the "Internationale" are sung by the same mouths, and Fureux — a Communist when sober, a rampant Chauvinist when drunk — stages a Political Economy of the working-class weekend in song and speech.

Chapter XVIII

Summary: Charlie tells a Saturday-night bistro story about being hard up with Yvonne, a yellow-haired peasant girl like Azaya, after pawning everything and refusing work. He bangs on the zinc bar for silence, calls the tale refined and instructive, invokes Boulevard Port-Royal, government, hunger, twins, peasant bodies, and comic sexual misery, and turns three days without food into a performance for the bistro audience. Source anchors: Charlie; Yvonne; Azaya; zinc bar; Boulevard Port-Royal; hunger.

Analysis: Charlie and Yvonne show how poverty becomes anecdote when retold by a gifted performer. The zinc bar and Boulevard Port-Royal give the story a stage and a geography. Hunger matters because Charlie aestheticizes what Orwell elsewhere records as physical misery, exposing both the charm and falseness of bohemian storytelling, especially when the storyteller refuses work as beneath him. The scam — a peasant girl passed off as pregnant to draw free government meals — inverts charity through an Informal Institutions trick, and Charlie's rhetoric shows Language and Ideology dressing hunger and fraud as refinement.

Chapter XIX

Summary: After five or six weeks at the Hôtel X, Boris announces that the Auberge will open and persuades Orwell to leave his safe job. Orwell finds the restaurant locked, Boris asleep in the Rue de la Croix Nivert, and the situation still uncertain; the chapter includes Jules, the patron, Fontainebleau and Saint-Cloud fantasies, Magyars, Communists, and the melancholy realization that another promise may be empty. Source anchors: Hôtel X; Boris; Auberge; Rue de la Croix Nivert; Jules; Fontainebleau.

Analysis: Hôtel X and Auberge mark the choice between bad security and risky hope. Boris and Rue de la Croix Nivert make the hope suspect from the moment it appears. Jules and Fontainebleau matter because the promised restaurant belongs to a world of talk, dreams, and evasions as much as wages, and Orwell's loyalty to Boris pulls him out of the one stable job he has. The patron's trick — engaging a staff of four to do the workmen's jobs unpaid while duns circle the building — turns the employer into an exploiter, an exercise in Coercive Labor dressed in aristocratic courtesy and the ritual "glass of brandy."

Chapter XX

Summary: Orwell starts as kitchen plongeur at the Auberge, responsible for washing up, cleaning, vegetables, tea, coffee, sandwiches, simple cooking, and errands, for five hundred francs a month and food but no fixed hours or day off. He contrasts Hôtel X's organization with the Auberge's bad restaurant practices: artistic decor, obscene pictures, Norman and Russian pretensions, no hot water, endless Brasso, Place d'Italie errands, and customers paying for picturesque fraud. Source anchors: kitchen plongeur; Auberge; five hundred francs; no fixed hours; hot water; Brasso.

Analysis: Kitchen plongeur and five hundred francs define the new job as worse than the old one despite the promise of advancement. Auberge and no fixed hours expose how small restaurants evade the discipline that made Hôtel X at least predictable. Brasso matters because useless polishing and errands become symbols of labor expended to sustain an aesthetic illusion for diners — seventeen and a half hours a day with no hot water, no larder, two candles and a blocked sink, the Auberge running on the barest Coercive Labor surplus while the patron stands in the bar smoking and "looking gentlemanly."

Chapter XXI

Summary: The Auberge life continues for about a fortnight, increasing as more customers arrive, while Orwell cannot change lodgings, cut his hair, read a newspaper, or undress completely. He writes to B. in London asking for any job that allows more than five hours' sleep, reflects on seventeen-hour days, notices a nearby bistro girl working comparable hours, and records Boris, Jules, Russian, American, and French restaurant talk under exhaustion. Source anchors: Auberge; fortnight; B. in London; five hours sleep; seventeen-hour day; bistro girl.

Analysis: Auberge and fortnight turn temporary overwork into a crisis of endurance. B. in London and five hours sleep make escape depend on outside friendship, not labor rights. Seventeen-hour day and bistro girl matter because Orwell broadens from personal complaint to the thousands for whom such hours are permanent, and whose exhaustion remains invisible to customers — the consumptive bistro girl who worked from seven in the morning till midnight for a year places the Auberge's fortnight inside an ordinary, ongoing Coercive Labor system rather than an exceptional emergency, and the dustbin spite and everlasting quarrels show exhaustion corroding every relation in the kitchen.

Chapter XXII

Summary: Orwell pauses to analyze the Paris plongeur as a modern slave: servile work, just enough pay to live, no real leisure, no marriage, no future, and labor that supports luxury without art. He compares plongeurs to coal miners, Indian and Far Eastern workers, European servants, Deauville smartness, Marcus Cato, Italy, and the possibility that a Marxian Utopia might abolish such useless labor. Source anchors: plongeur; modern slave; coal-mining; Deauville; Marcus Cato; Marxian Utopia.

Analysis: Plongeur and modern slave state the book's clearest Coercive Labor claim. Coal-mining and Deauville prevent a simple pity hierarchy: the plongeur may be less physically endangered than some workers yet trapped in pointless servility. Marxian Utopia matters because Orwell asks not only why the work is harsh, but why society wants it done at all. His answer — that "smartness" exists to be sold, that educated people fear the mob, and that money rather than usefulness legitimises work — is the book's core Political Economy argument, resting on a Commercial Society diagnosis in which a hundred toil so that two hundred may pay through the nose for things they do not really want.

Chapter XXIII

Summary: After leaving the Auberge, Orwell sleeps almost around the clock, washes his teeth for the first time in a fortnight, bathes, cuts his hair, retrieves clothes from pawn, drinks English beer as a customer, and says goodbye around the quarter. Boris stays to earn money, and Charlie tells the story of old Roucolle the miser, Daniel Dancer comparisons, Les Halles, a Jew, Vienna, a Pole from the Sorbonne, face powder, the commissaire, and a grotesque death. Source anchors: Auberge; English beer; Boris; Roucolle; Les Halles; Sorbonne Pole.

Analysis: Auberge and English beer show the shock of moving from servant to customer in the same room. Boris remains tied to the restaurant economy that Orwell escapes. Roucolle and Les Halles matter because the farewell chapter returns to Paris anecdote, making poverty a set of remembered characters as well as a labor system, and letting the Paris half close with gossip rather than theory. Roucolle's death — a miser with half a million francs invested, eating cat's meat and bedding newspaper, fleeced in a cocaine-smuggling scheme that turns out to be face-powder — is a capsule Illicit Markets fable of greed meeting sharper greed.

Chapter XXIV

Summary: Orwell travels to England third class by Dunkirk and Tilbury, sleeps in the saloon with twenty-seven men and sixteen women, meets young Roumanian honeymooners, and lies cheerfully about England because returning from hard-up Paris makes London feel like paradise. The chapter then reverses that optimism through Lambeth, Sunday arrival, dirt, poor clothes, and the realization that English poverty has its own humiliations. Source anchors: Dunkirk; Tilbury; third class; Roumanians; London; Lambeth.

Analysis: Dunkirk and Tilbury mark the book's geographic pivot from working poverty to tramp poverty. Roumanians and London let Orwell expose the fantasy of England as comfort after foreign hardship. Lambeth matters because homecoming does not end poverty; it changes its institutions, accents, and rules, replacing Paris kitchens with lodging houses and casual wards. The shilling's worth of dirty rags that buys a new identity teaches the cheapness of dignity, and the instant shift from "sir" to "mate" shows how Language and Ideology is worn on the clothes; dirt, Orwell insists, is "a great respecter of persons," a Moral Economy observation about how surfaces are read as moral evidence.

Chapter XXV

Summary: Orwell's eight shillings last three days and four nights, after which he moves from Waterloo Road to a Pennyfields lodging house. He describes beds, dormitories, cash payment by seven, a deputy manager, common kitchens, tea-basins, Wapping, Whitechapel, Limehouse, Orientals, Chinamen, Chittagonians, labor exchanges, and the East London lodging-house world of men with just enough money to avoid the street. Source anchors: eight shillings; Waterloo Road; Pennyfields; deputy; Wapping; Limehouse.

Analysis: Eight shillings and Pennyfields give the London half its arithmetic, just as francs did in Paris. Waterloo Road and deputy show lodging as a business governed by cash deadlines. Wapping and Limehouse matter because Orwell's London poverty is maritime, colonial, and multiethnic, not only English, with empire's sailors and laborers sharing the same dormitories. The pensioner who budgets a ten-shilling week — ninepence for the kip, threepence for a shave, a sixpence haircut once a month, four and fourpence left for food and baccy — lets Orwell price an entire life in lodging-house terms, making the Pennyfields kitchen a working model of Commercial Society at its floor.

Chapter XXVI

Summary: With only a halfpenny after tea-and-two-slices and tobacco, Orwell walks to the Romton casual ward and meets an old Irish tramp leaning by pigpens in the market-place. The Irishman teaches him about road tobacco stored in an Oxo Cubes tin, skilly, York, Wales, London road habits, Catholic and Baptist talk, the Crucifixion, and the rough knowledge needed to enter the spike system without looking completely new to the road. Source anchors: halfpenny; Romton; Irishman; Oxo Cubes; skilly; casual ward.

Analysis: Halfpenny and Romton mark the threshold from cheap lodging to institutional destitution. The Irishman and Oxo Cubes show how tramp expertise begins with tiny material adaptations. Skilly and casual ward matter because Orwell is entering a system with its own food, vocabulary, hierarchy, and initiation, and he needs another poor man to teach him how to be processed; the tramps' typology of tay spikes, cocoa spikes and skilly spikes is practical Informal Institutions knowledge carried between wards. The free tea that must be paid for in half an hour of kneeling prayer shows charity sold as humiliation — a Moral Economy transaction the tramps resent precisely because it claims to be free.

Chapter XXVII

Summary: The Irishman leads Orwell to the Romton spike, a smoky yellow brick cube inside workhouse grounds, where a queue of ragged men waits behind walls, barred windows, and gates. Orwell observes boys of sixteen, old men of seventy-five, factory hands, agricultural laborers, a clerk, imbeciles, hardened tramps, the Tramp Major, Jeyes' fluid, inspection, baths, beds, and the prisonlike friendliness of men sharing the same degradation. Source anchors: Romton spike; Irishman; workhouse; barred windows; Tramp Major; Jeyes' fluid.

Analysis: Romton spike and workhouse make poverty an institution with architecture like punishment. Barred windows and Tramp Major turn Legibility into discipline: officials process, inspect, and classify tramps before allowing food or sleep. Jeyes' fluid matters because cleanliness is imposed as management of the poor body, not care. The ledger entry of name, trade, age and destination, the search below the knee, the eightpenny money limit, and the sixpenny meal tickets that coffee-shops discount are all State Capacity devices that keep tramps moving and cheap to feed; registering, rationing and inspecting, not remedying, are the point.

Chapter XXVIII

Summary: Orwell introduces Paddy, his mate for the next fortnight: a thirty-five-year-old Irish tramp with grizzled fair hair, watery blue eyes, a tweed shooting jacket, old evening trousers with braid, a razor and bootbrush, and a Catholic identity. Paddy discusses Ireland, Edbury, Romton, Baptists, church, Napoleon, Jesus Christ, and the scraps of respectability he preserves despite selling papers and a pocketknife. Source anchors: Paddy; Irish tramp; evening trousers; razor; Edbury; Catholic.

Analysis: Paddy and Irish tramp give the London section its central companion, equivalent to Boris in Paris but more defeated. Evening trousers and razor show how dignity survives as maintenance of appearance after almost everything else is gone. Edbury and Catholic matter because Paddy's world is made of spikes, routes, religious labels, and habits learned through repetition. Orwell's verdict — that two years of bread and margarine, "malnutrition and not any native vice," had destroyed Paddy's manhood — is the chapter's Moral Economy core: character is read as cause when it is really effect, and the "jackal's character" Orwell charts is a product of the institution, not its raw material.

Chapter XXIX

Summary: On the way to Edbury, Orwell tells Paddy about a friend who can lend money, but Paddy insists on using a free spike night rather than wasting it. They face tobacco confiscation under the Vagrancy Act, then later return to London, are turned away from a Rowton House, and sleep in a Salvation Army shelter whose walls carry General Booth's portraits and notices forbidding the "DETESTABLE EVIL OF GAMBLING," the whole chapter closing on the harsh calculus of beds, tea, and legality. Source anchors: Edbury; Paddy; Vagrancy Act; Rowton House; Salvation Army; General Booth; gambling notice.

Analysis: Edbury and Paddy show tramp rationality: a free lodging has value even when unpleasant. Vagrancy Act and Rowton House reveal how law and charity structure movement through the city. The Salvation Army shelter turns Moral Economy into discipline: material relief arrives boxed inside anti-gambling notices and pious exhortation that sermonise about the character of the people who need it. The ten-o'clock whistle, the semimilitary dormitories, the compulsory services, and the Rowton House doorman's "Then f——off" govern the same bodies through both State Capacity and charitable regulation, and the young clerk who rages against the Lord and is then found alone on his knees shows the two faces of enforced piety.

Chapter XXX

Summary: Orwell and Paddy find Bozo, a pavement artist or screever, near Waterloo Bridge on the Embankment, copying Winston Churchill from a penny notebook. Bozo has a deformed leg, a Roman-looking profile he associates with Vespasian, strong opinions about politics, socialism, capital and labor, the Budget, the Boat Race, the Cup Final, and a stubborn intellectual independence despite living by chalk drawings on pavement. Source anchors: Bozo; screever; Waterloo Bridge; Winston Churchill; Vespasian; socialism.

Analysis: Bozo and screever complicate the stereotype of tramps as mentally inert. Waterloo Bridge and Winston Churchill show public space turned into workplace and gallery. Vespasian and socialism matter because Bozo's pride is intellectual and aesthetic, proving that destitution has not made him accept the moral categories imposed on him by passers-by or charity officials. His Cockneyed, exact speech and his claim that a man can stay "free in here" so long as he reads, thinks and watches for meteors make him the book's counter-example to the tramp-monster, a case where Language and Ideology and personal pride survive almost everything; the screeving trade, complete with police censorship of cartoons and fought-over pitches, is a street-level Commercial Society.

Chapter XXXI

Summary: Bozo's lodging house costs ninepence and holds five hundred men, mixing tramps, beggars, petty criminals, Indians, a Chittagonian lascar, old Grandpa who sells tobacco from cigarette ends, a struck-off doctor selling advice, Shorty from Whitechapel and Commercial Road, organ-grinders, screevers, and beggars. Orwell notes that below a certain level even racial prejudice weakens, and the house becomes a crossroads of damaged livelihoods. Source anchors: Bozo's lodging house; ninepence; Indians; Chittagonian lascar; Grandpa; Shorty.

Analysis: Bozo's lodging house and ninepence show mass homelessness as a commercial dormitory system. Indians and the Chittagonian lascar connect London poverty to empire and shipping. Grandpa and Shorty matter because each man has a micro-economy, from cigarette ends to street performance, inside a world respectable people see only as vagrancy, dirt, or crime. The ranked account of acrobats, photographers, organ-grinders, screevers, hymn-singers and match-sellers is a portrait of Informal Institutions street economics, and the claim that a beggar is "simply a businessman" who has chosen a trade at which it is impossible to grow rich restates the book's Commercial Society argument in its sharpest form.

Chapter XXXII

Summary: Orwell inserts notes on London slang and swearing: gagger, moocher, nobber, chanter, clodhopper, mugfaker, glimmer, gee, split, flattie, didecai, toby, drop, funkum, boozer, kip, smoke, judy, and spike. He compares English and French slang, East End terms, Tower Bridge, Dickens, Surtees, Cockney speech, Herman Melville's White Jacket, and the way cant words record occupations and survival techniques. Source anchors: London slang; gagger; moocher; glimmer; kip; spike.

Analysis: London slang and gagger turn Language and Ideology into social evidence. Moocher, glimmer, and kip identify roles and tactics that polite vocabulary would flatten into begging or homelessness. Spike matters because the institution produces its own word, carrying the contempt and familiarity of those forced to use it night after night. By placing Tower Bridge, Dickens, Surtees, and Melville beside street cant, Orwell treats poor speech as a record of work, fraud, punishment, and lodging rather than picturesque noise.

Chapter XXXIII

Summary: B.'s two pounds last about ten days because Paddy teaches Orwell to live on half a crown a day with bread, margarine, tea, tobacco, cheap beds, and occasional illegal glimming. They fail to get sandwich-board work, visit King's Cross, hear street preachers and Brother Bootle, see Hallelujah meetings, move around Elephant and Castle, and stretch money through the rhythms of London casual poverty. Source anchors: two pounds; Paddy; half a crown; glimming; sandwich men; King's Cross.

Analysis: Two pounds and half a crown make survival a skill Paddy can teach. Glimming and sandwich men show the narrow, often illegal or degrading labor available just above begging. King's Cross matters because public religion, casual work, and cheap lodging cluster around transport nodes where the poor can circulate, wait, and be recruited into temporary schemes. Glimming itself — watching parked cars for anything worth taking while the driver is away — is an Illicit Markets dodge for a few shillings, and the church free teas and sixpenny meal tickets that eating-houses discount are Informal Institutions charities whose "generosity" the tramps must meet with irony, as when the hundred lodgers ignore a slumming-party's service as though it did not exist.

Chapter XXXIV

Summary: With money gone, Orwell and Paddy walk sixteen miles down the Old Kent Road toward Cromley, blistered and hungry, picking cigarette ends and sharing stale bread bought with a found penny. They wait in a tramps' resting place near a meadow, meet Bill the moocher, hear talk of Kent, Oxford, Chile, Gilderoy, America, Samson, Robin Hood, and confront the endless geography of spikes and roads. Source anchors: Old Kent Road; Cromley; sixteen miles; cigarette ends; stale bread; Bill the moocher.

Analysis: Old Kent Road and sixteen miles make the tramp system physically punitive by distance. Cigarette ends and stale bread show survival at the level of scraps. Bill the moocher matters because the road contains its own specialists, legends, and boasts, even when everyone is hungry, footsore, and moving toward another disciplinary lodging. Cromley, Kent, Oxford, and Chile widen the road beyond one day's walk, making Bill's bragging part of the mental geography tramps use to survive repeated displacement. The caravanserai meadow, the oral history amended like the stories of Samson and Robin Hood, and the ballad of "Unhappy Bella" show the road sustaining its own culture and memory — an Informal Institutions world beneath the law — while the Cromley ghost-cell and the suffocated stowaway keep its folklore grim, and language and song do the remembering carried from spike to spike as an Language and Ideology archive.

Chapter XXXV

Summary: At Lower Binfield, Orwell and Paddy wait with dozens of tramps on the green while villagers, a clergyman, and his daughter stare as if at aquarium fish. The group includes William and Fred singing, Bill with stale bread hidden under his coat, a woman tramp with airs of dignity, Scotty from Glasgow, Family Herald and Raffles readers, half-eaten food, and men bargaining for space, respect, and scraps before entry to the spike. Source anchors: Lower Binfield; Paddy; clergyman; woman tramp; Bill; Glasgow Scotty.

Analysis: Lower Binfield and the clergyman show respectable curiosity treating tramps as exhibits. Woman tramp and Bill demonstrate two different survival performances: dignity and hoarding. Glasgow Scotty matters because the spike queue becomes a temporary society with regional identities, songs, reading habits, and comic rivalries before the authorities process them. The word "gentleman" buys Orwell a clean towel and the coveted workhouse-kitchen job while the rest sit locked in the dining-room, proof of how Language and Ideology allocates privilege even inside the spike; and the workhouse's deliberate wastage of five dustbins of eatable food while fifty tramps get bread and two cold potatoes is the book's bluntest Moral Economy indictment of institutional charity.

Chapter XXXVI

Summary: Orwell turns to general remarks on tramps, attacking the childhood prejudice that every tramp is a blackguard. He compares the tramp-monster stereotype to sinister magazine-story Chinamen, mentions Jack London's American account, English tramps, LCC shelters, St Martin's-in-the-Fields, February cold, spending rules, work availability, and the fact that enforced wandering is produced by law and lodging policy rather than innate idleness. Source anchors: tramps; blackguard; Jack London; LCC; St Martin's; enforced wandering.

Analysis: Tramps and blackguard name the stereotype Orwell wants to dismantle. Jack London and LCC let him compare literary, American, and London institutional views of vagrancy. Enforced wandering exposes a State Capacity failure: law and lodging policy make staying put illegal or impossible, then blame men for the motion the system requires. The "tramp-monster" is a creature of vocabulary — "the very word 'tramp' evokes his image," and a hundred tramps are handled by three unarmed porters — so Language and Ideology does the work of social policy while hunger, enforced celibacy and locked-in idleness are named as the three great evils the book's farm-and-work remedy is meant to dissolve.

Chapter XXXVII

Summary: Orwell catalogs sleeping options for homeless Londoners who cannot pay sevenpence: the Embankment, described by Paddy with benches, trams, sky-signs, police at four in the morning, and cold; the Twopenny Hangover; the Coffin; Rowton Houses; Bruce Houses; and Salvation Army shelters. He compares London arrangements with Paris, the Seine, the Métro, and the different degrees of discomfort, charity, and payment each option requires. Source anchors: Embankment; sevenpence; Twopenny Hangover; Coffin; Rowton Houses; Salvation Army.

Analysis: Embankment and sevenpence make homelessness a price ladder. Twopenny Hangover and Coffin expose Commercial Society at its floor: the market sells even partial sleep and posture to the desperate. Rowton Houses and Salvation Army matter because charity and commerce both regulate the homeless body, deciding whether it may sit, hang, lie down, be moved on, or be preached at. The law that lets a man sit on the Embankment but moves him on the moment he sleeps, and the LCC's regulations that police gambling yet never mandate a comfortable mattress, extend State Capacity into the smallest details of slumber; the Croydon municipal lodging-house at ninepence stands as proof that a humane standard is unaffordable only because it is unchosen.

Chapter XXXVIII

Summary: After Lower Binfield, Orwell and Paddy earn half a crown weeding and sweeping, stay at Cromley, and return to London. B. lends Orwell a final two pounds, his promised job proves tolerable enough, Paddy heads toward Portsmouth and may later have been killed, Bozo is in Wandsworth for begging, and Orwell closes by saying the story ends because his own troubles ended, while footnotes explain French, Hindustani, pavement terminology, and lodging-house words. Source anchors: Lower Binfield; half a crown; Cromley; B.; Portsmouth; Wandsworth.

Analysis: Lower Binfield and half a crown show how little work is needed to change a day's prospects, but not a life. B. and Portsmouth separate Orwell's temporary poverty from Paddy's continuing road: the narrator has a friend and a job, Paddy has another uncertain route. Wandsworth matters because Bozo's intelligence does not protect him from the legal treatment of begging, an instance of State Capacity disposing of the very man the book admired. The closing lesson-list — never think all tramps are drunken scoundrels, never expect a beggar to be grateful for a penny, never subscribe to the Salvation Army, never pawn clothes, never enjoy a smart-restaurant meal — converts the whole narrative into Moral Economy self-reformation, and the "fringe of poverty" Orwell leaves unexplored points toward the political books that followed.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • The book's sums: four hundred fifty francs in hand plus thirty-six francs a week from English lessons; forty-seven francs left after the Italian compositor's theft; six francs (a shilling) a day as the Paris survival rate; seventy francs from the pawnshop for clothes that cost over twenty pounds; twenty-five francs a day as an "extra" at the Hôtel X.; five hundred francs a month as kitchen plongeur at the Auberge; eight shillings lasting three days and four nights; half a crown a day as Paddy's London budget; sixpence for a spike meal ticket.
  • Retrieval phrases: "More and more savagely I renewed the attack…" (Charlie's red-cellar story); "Attaquez! Attaquez! Attaquez!" (Boris's Foch motto); "My Little Cherished Wolf" (Yvonne's refusal letter); "Jesus Christ!... what about my ten per cent?" (Valenti on the salt-and-water breakfast); "Faut être un dur" / "Je suis un dur" and "débrouillard" (plongeur honour); "Attention, un Français!" (the Auberge's first French customer); "Large tea and two slices!" (the London phrase "that is to London what the eternal coup de rouge is to Paris"); "I'm a free man in here" (Bozo on his forehead); "the very word 'tramp' evokes his image"; "you got to pay for it" — "Pray for it, you mean" (tramps on the free-tea shed); "Dat tay in de spike ain't tay, it's piss" (Paddy).
  • Physical details: the moustache rule (only cooks wear moustaches — pure etiquette); the doorkeeper who cheated Orwell of a hundred and fourteen francs in six weeks and swore he was Greek but was Armenian; the Serbian "extra" who worked hard till twelve so law gave him a day's wages and he insisted on being sacked; the cell with no beds (sleep on the floor with one blanket); tobacco hidden in boot-ankles because the porter never searched below the knee; the eightpenny law limit on money entering a spike.
  • Best shorthand examples: the pensioner's ten-shilling-week budget as the lowest end of London commercial life; the Croydon municipal lodging-house at ninepence as proof that humane lodging was affordable if legislated; the workhouse kitchen's five dustbins of deliberately wasted food while fifty tramps got bread and two cold potatoes; the free-tea shed at Romton where about twenty-five waiting tramps knelt for their bun; the Twopenny Hangover's rope and the Coffin's fourpenny wooden box as the bottom of the sleep market; the meal-ticket shops that routinely short-changed tramps who could not argue because they had tickets, not money.
  • Social/legal cut-out facts: the Vagrancy Act prosecuted smoking in the spike; a tramp could legally be moved on for sleeping except on the Embankment and one corner near the Lyceum; LCC night census (13 February 1931): 60 men and 18 women in the streets, 674 men and 15 women in LCC casual wards — "below a certain level society is entirely male."
  • Orwell's own summary of what the book proves: "I shall never again think that all tramps are drunken scoundrels, nor expect a beggar to be grateful when I give him a penny, nor be surprised if men out of work lack energy, nor subscribe to the Salvation Army, nor pawn my clothes, nor refuse a handbill, nor enjoy a meal at a smart restaurant. That is a beginning."
