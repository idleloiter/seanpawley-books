Detailed overview

Tolstoy begins with a Petersburg salon in July 1805 and ends with a theory of history that denies decisive agency to emperors, generals, and official historians. Anna Pavlovna Scherer's evening, Prince Vasili Kuragin's maneuvers, Pierre Bezukhov's social clumsiness, Prince Andrew Bolkonski's contempt for drawing-room life, and Napoleon's position in Genoa, Lucca, Milan, and European talk all establish the first scale of the novel: people believe they are discussing history while also arranging marriages, promotions, inheritances, reputations, and dinner invitations.

The domestic plots and the military plots are deliberately interlocked. The Rostov household in Moscow, the Bolkonski estate at Bald Hills, Pierre's inheritance from Count Kirill Bezukhov, Helene Kuragina's marriage, Anatole Kuragin's seductions, Natasha Rostova's music and romantic impulsiveness, Sonya's self-denial, Nicholas Rostov's regimental loyalty, Mary Bolkonskaya's religious patience, and old Prince Bolkonski's tyranny matter as much as Schongraben, Austerlitz, Borodino, Moscow, Krasnoe, and the retreat from Russia. Tolstoy treats war as an intensifier of family structures rather than a separate sphere.

Pierre, Andrew, Natasha, Nicholas, and Mary each test a false idea of life. Pierre tries dissipation, marriage to Helene, Freemasonry, estate reform, numerological dreams of assassinating Napoleon, captivity, and finally Karataev's peasant simplicity. Andrew tries glory under Kutuzov, domestic withdrawal after Lise's death, reforming service with Speranski, love for Natasha, bitterness after her near elopement with Anatole, and forgiveness as he dies after Borodino. Natasha moves from childhood exuberance to operatic temptation, disgrace, nursing, grief, and family life. Nicholas moves from card debts, cavalry romance, and Denisov's circle toward the landed duties that make his marriage to Mary possible.

The 1812 sections are the novel's political center. Alexander, Napoleon, Barclay de Tolly, Bagration, Kutuzov, Rostopchin, the Smolensk evacuation, the abandoned Moscow houses, Petya's enlistment, Pierre's failed heroic plan, the Raevski redoubt, Prince Andrew's wound, Anatole's amputation, the burning of Moscow, the execution of prisoners, Platon Karataev, Dolokhov and Denisov's partisan raids, Petya's death, and the French retreat show Tolstoy dismantling the idea that history obeys the intentions of commanders. Armies move because supplies fail, roads clog, soldiers fear and misunderstand, civilians flee, and countless minor choices accumulate.

The epilogues are not appendices; they reveal what the preceding action was for. Pierre and Natasha, Nicholas and Mary, Sonya, Princess Maria's household, Countess Rostova, old memories of Andrew and Petya, and the Decembrist hints around Pierre's political talk show history continuing inside domestic arrangements. The second epilogue then turns the whole book into an attack on historical explanation based on "great men." Tolstoy insists that freedom and necessity must be understood through the aggregate motion of innumerable human wills, not through Napoleon's genius or Alexander's declarations.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Information and Coordination: Tolstoy repeatedly turns war into a problem of partial knowledge, delayed orders, misread intentions, and local action that no commander fully sees.
  • State Capacity: the novel tests imperial armies, reform commissions, bureaucracy, mobilization, evacuation, and retreat as concrete capacities rather than sovereign titles.
  • Moral Economy: households, estates, carts, debts, wounded soldiers, prisoners, and peasants expose competing claims about obligation and rightful sacrifice.
  • National Identity: Tolstoy locates Russian belonging less in official patriotism than in embodied practices, household loss, peasant endurance, and the defensive people's war of 1812.
  • Informal Institutions: salons, aristocratic matchmaking, family pressure, reputation, and secret-society hints often govern behavior more effectively than formal rules.
  • Logistics and Throughput: the 1812 campaign repeatedly turns on roads, food, carts, prisoners, cold, and supply collapse rather than on imperial intention.
  • Language and Ideology: the second epilogue attacks the explanatory vocabulary of great men, genius, power, freedom, necessity, and command.

Section-by-section notes

Book One: 1805

Summary: The novel opens in July 1805 at Anna Pavlovna Scherer's Petersburg reception, where Prince Vasili Kuragin, Helene Kuragina, Hippolyte, Anna Mikhailovna Drubetskaya, Abbe Morio, the vicomte, Pierre Bezukhov, and Prince Andrew Bolkonski talk about Napoleon, Genoa, Lucca, Milan, and the threat of war. Pierre's awkward defense of Napoleon and Andrew's disgust with society are set beside Vasili's promotion-seeking for Boris, Anna Mikhailovna's begging, and Helene's calculated beauty. The book then moves to Moscow, the Rostov name-day dinner, Natasha, Nicholas, Sonya, Boris, Vera, Count and Countess Rostov, Marya Dmitrievna, and the dying Count Kirill Bezukhov's inheritance struggle involving Pierre, Prince Vasili, Catiche, Anna Mikhailovna, and the disputed portfolio. Source anchors: Anna Pavlovna, Prince Vasili, Pierre, Prince Andrew, Rostov name-day, Count Bezukhov, portfolio.

Analysis: Anna Pavlovna and Prince Vasili show aristocratic politics as performance, because war talk and Napoleon talk occur while careers, marriages, and inheritances are being positioned. Pierre and Prince Andrew enter as opposites who are both dissatisfied with the salon code: Pierre cannot obey its forms, while Andrew despises them from inside. The Rostov name-day and Count Bezukhov's portfolio widen the book from Petersburg opinion to Moscow household feeling and material succession. This is Informal Institutions in aristocratic form: salons, kinship, begging, reputation, and a disputed portfolio move power before any official institution speaks.

Book Two: 1805

Summary: Book Two moves to the army in Austria, where Kutuzov reviews troops near Braunau, Prince Andrew serves as aide-de-camp, Zherkov jokes, Dolokhov reappears after demotion, and Nicholas Rostov enters the Pavlograd Hussars under Denisov. The campaign includes the crossing of the Enns, Mack's defeat at Ulm, Bilibin's diplomatic talk, the retreat toward Krems, the Schongraben action, Tushin's battery, Bagration's rear guard, and Rostov's first confused experience of fear, pain, and imagined heroism. Andrew seeks a Toulon-like opportunity, but he sees orders, smoke, exhaustion, and accidental courage matter more than clean glory. Source anchors: Kutuzov, Braunau, Rostov, Denisov, Schongraben, Tushin battery, Bagration.

Analysis: Kutuzov and Braunau replace salon war with bodies, boots, inspections, and tired commanders who know less than civilians imagine. Rostov, Denisov, Schongraben, and Tushin's battery make military courage messy: Nicholas experiences fear and self-dramatization, while Tushin performs real service without rhetorical grandeur. Bagration and Andrew show Tolstoy's early answer to heroic history, because the rear guard survives through confusion, stubbornness, and local action rather than through a single plan.

Book Three: 1805

Summary: Book Three brings the allied armies toward Austerlitz, where Alexander, Kutuzov, Weyrother, Prince Andrew, Boris Drubetskoy, Berg, Dolgorukov, Napoleon, and the Russian and Austrian staffs misread one another. At Bald Hills, old Prince Bolkonski, Princess Mary, Mademoiselle Bourienne, and little princess Lise live under domestic discipline while Andrew is absent. At Austerlitz, the Pratzen Heights, fog, the council of war, Weyrother's plan, Kutuzov's reluctance, Andrew's banner charge, Nicholas Rostov's glimpses of the sovereign, and Napoleon's inspection of the wounded destroy Andrew's fantasy of glory. Source anchors: Austerlitz, Pratzen Heights, Weyrother, Alexander, Kutuzov, Andrew's banner, Bald Hills.

Analysis: Austerlitz and the Pratzen Heights test the official plan against weather, timing, and mistaken confidence. Weyrother, Alexander, and Kutuzov expose the Information and Coordination failure inside imperial command: paper strategy cannot convert fog, timing, terrain, and troop movement into shared action. Andrew's banner and Bald Hills connect public ambition to private emptiness, because his near death under Napoleon's gaze makes glory feel small beside the sky and the family life he had fled.

Book Four: 1806

Summary: Book Four returns to Russian society after Austerlitz, with Pierre inheriting wealth, marrying Helene under Prince Vasili's pressure, and entering a false domestic life in Petersburg. Dolokhov's intimacy with Helene leads to the dinner at the English Club, Pierre's duel with Dolokhov, Nicholas Rostov's return home, Denisov's stay with the Rostovs, Natasha's singing, Sonya's love, Nikolai's gambling loss of forty-three thousand rubles to Dolokhov, and Count Rostov's ruinous indulgence. At Bald Hills, Andrew returns just as Lise dies giving birth to little Nicholas, and Princess Mary receives another burden of care. Source anchors: Pierre and Helene, English Club, Dolokhov duel, forty-three thousand, Natasha singing, Lise death, little Nicholas.

Analysis: Pierre and Helene turn inheritance into captivity because wealth makes Pierre easier to arrange rather than freer. The English Club, Dolokhov duel, and forty-three thousand rubles show honor culture, desire, and money damaging households as surely as battle damages regiments. Natasha singing and Lise's death place two kinds of life-force against the sterile choices of Pierre and Andrew: Natasha restores Nicholas for a moment, while Andrew's wife dies before his moral return can become domestic repair.

Book Five: 1806-07

Summary: Book Five follows Pierre after the duel into separation from Helene, a meeting with the Freemason Bazdeev, initiation into Masonry, estate reforms, and self-deceiving hopes of moral renewal. Andrew lives at Bogucharovo and Bald Hills, cares for little Nicholas, talks with Pierre at the ferry, sees the old oak, and slowly reopens himself to work and feeling. The military strand returns through Denisov's punishment for seizing food, Rostov's petitioning, Tilsit, Alexander's meeting with Napoleon, Boris's career calculations, and the uneasy spectacle of former enemies becoming diplomatic partners. Source anchors: Bazdeev, Freemasonry, estate reforms, ferry talk, old oak, Denisov's petition, Tilsit.

Analysis: Bazdeev and Freemasonry offer Pierre a system for goodness, but the estate reforms show how easily moral schemes become vanity when the peasants and stewards remain opaque to him. The failed reforms expose a Moral Economy problem rather than only Pierre's psychology: benevolent language cannot change obligations on an estate when the people affected remain unread by the reformer. Denisov's petition and Tilsit place personal loyalty against imperial theater, as Rostov's devotion cannot alter bureaucracy while Alexander and Napoleon perform reconciliation above the soldiers' suffering.

Book Six: 1808-10

Summary: Book Six moves into the interval of reform and courtship, with Pierre's Masonic disappointments, Helene's social success, Andrew's service in Petersburg, Speranski, the legislative commissions, Arakcheev's coldness, and the language of rational administration. At Otradnoe, Andrew hears Natasha at night, sees her vitality, encounters the old oak renewed, and falls in love after the Rostov visit. Natasha's first ball, her dance with Andrew, Count Ilya Rostov's pride, Vera and Berg's household, Sonya's uncertainty, and old Prince Bolkonski's demand for a year's delay bring the romance into conflict with family hierarchy. Source anchors: Speranski, Arakcheev, Otradnoe, Natasha at night, first ball, old oak, year's delay.

Analysis: Speranski and Arakcheev show Andrew trying to convert his post-Austerlitz seriousness into State Capacity, but the legislative commissions feel thin beside living emotion. Otradnoe, Natasha at night, the first ball, and the old oak make Natasha the force that pulls Andrew out of administrative abstraction. The year's delay imposed by old Prince Bolkonski turns private love into a test of endurance and exposes how aristocratic family authority can damage even sincere feeling.

Book Seven: 1810-11

Summary: Book Seven centers on the Rostovs' country life, with Count Rostov's finances declining, Nicholas home on leave, Natasha, Petya, Sonya, the mummers, hunting, Uncle's house, balalaika music, Natasha's instinctive Russian dance, and the winter journey by troika. Nicholas feels the pull between Sonya and the family's need for a wealthy marriage, while Countess Rostova pressures him against Sonya. The book's rural scenes place dogs, wolves, huntsmen, peasants, old songs, Christmas disguises, and household debt inside the same Rostov atmosphere. Source anchors: Rostov finances, wolf hunt, Uncle's house, Natasha's dance, mummers, Sonya, troika.

Analysis: Rostov finances and Sonya make the family's warmth inseparable from its insolvency, because affection cannot cancel debts. The wolf hunt, Uncle's house, Natasha's dance, and old songs make National Identity bodily rather than declarative: Russian belonging lives in hunting rhythms, music, dance, peasant proximity, and seasonal ritual more than in court slogans. The mummers and troika scenes preserve a vanishing household abundance even as Nicholas's marriage question reveals that the Rostovs' generosity needs someone else's property to survive.

Book Eight: 1811-12

Summary: Book Eight follows Natasha's vulnerability during Andrew's absence, the Rostovs' stay in Moscow, Marya Dmitrievna's protection, old Prince Bolkonski's hostility, Princess Mary's awkward kindness, Helene's salon, Anatole Kuragin's pursuit, Dolokhov's planning, the opera, Sonya's vigilance, the attempted elopement, Balaga's troika, and Pierre's intervention. Natasha breaks with Andrew, Anatole is exposed as already married, Pierre confronts Anatole, and Natasha's illness and shame begin after the collapse of the romance. Source anchors: Marya Dmitrievna, old Prince Bolkonski, Helene, Anatole, Dolokhov, opera, Balaga.

Analysis: Marya Dmitrievna and old Prince Bolkonski define the social pressures around Natasha before Anatole even appears: one protects through force, the other wounds through pride. Helene, Anatole, Dolokhov, the opera, and Balaga turn urban pleasure into machinery for deception, with seduction staged as logistics. Pierre's intervention matters because he sees both Natasha's guilt and Anatole's emptiness, beginning the compassion that will later replace his abstract moral projects.

Book Nine: 1812

Summary: Book Nine opens the French invasion of Russia, with Napoleon crossing the Niemen, Alexander at Vilna, Balashev's mission, Barclay de Tolly, Bagration, Drissa, Boris, Berg, Julie, Petya's patriotic excitement, and Moscow society's conflicting responses. Pierre becomes obsessed with Napoleon through numerology and the idea of a providential role, while Andrew rejoins the army and rejects staff vanity. The book follows the Russian retreat, the merging of armies, the abandonment of strategic illusions, and the first movement toward a people's war rather than a court war. Source anchors: Niemen, Alexander at Vilna, Balashev, Barclay, Bagration, Drissa, Pierre numerology.

Analysis: The Niemen and Alexander at Vilna shift the novel from courtly anticipation to invasion, but Balashev's mission shows diplomacy already lagging behind events. Barclay, Bagration, and Drissa expose Crisis Governance as disagreement under pressure rather than unified national will: commanders argue over retreat, concentration, and position while invasion outruns plans. Pierre's numerology and Andrew's return to the army offer opposite false solutions, with Pierre imagining symbolic destiny while Andrew strips war down to hatred, duty, and the men immediately around him.

Book Ten: 1812

Summary: Book Ten carries the campaign through Smolensk, Bald Hills, Bogucharovo, the death of old Prince Bolkonski, Princess Mary's danger among peasants, Nicholas Rostov's rescue of Mary, Kutuzov's appointment, the march to Borodino, Pierre's visit to the battlefield, the militia, the icon of Smolensk, Napoleon's cold and calculations, and the battle itself. Borodino includes the Raevski redoubt, Bagration's fleches, artillery fire, Kutuzov's waiting, Prince Andrew's wound while standing in reserve, Anatole's amputation, and Tolstoy's insistence that neither Napoleon nor Kutuzov controls the slaughter in any simple way. Source anchors: Smolensk, old Prince Bolkonski death, Nicholas rescues Mary, Kutuzov, Borodino, Raevski redoubt, Anatole amputation.

Analysis: Smolensk and old Prince Bolkonski's death make national invasion enter the Bolkonski household directly, destroying the old order at Bald Hills. Nicholas rescuing Mary ties military movement to future domestic repair, because a cavalry officer's accidental arrival alters Mary's life more than official strategy does. Borodino, the Raevski redoubt, Kutuzov, Andrew's wound, and Anatole's amputation form Tolstoy's anti-Napoleonic evidence: battle is endurance, injury, and morale, not a chessboard governed by imperial intention.

Book Eleven: 1812

Summary: Book Eleven follows the aftermath of Borodino into Moscow, with Kutuzov's council at Fili, the decision to abandon the city, Count Rostopchin's proclamations, the Rostovs packing carts, Natasha insisting that wounded soldiers take the family's wagons, Prince Andrew among the wounded, Sonya's discovery of him, and the family's departure. Pierre remains in Moscow, arms himself with a pistol and peasant clothes, watches the city empty, sees French troops enter, witnesses fire and looting, saves a child and a woman, is arrested after the killing of French soldiers, and enters captivity as Moscow burns. Source anchors: council at Fili, Rostopchin, Rostov carts, Natasha's wounded, Pierre in Moscow, Moscow fire, arrest.

Analysis: The council at Fili and Kutuzov's abandonment of Moscow make retreat an act of Crisis Governance rather than cowardice, because the army matters more than the sacred city. Rostopchin, the Rostov carts, Natasha's wounded, and Prince Andrew's presence turn public catastrophe into a household Moral Economy test that Natasha passes by sacrificing property for bodies. Pierre in Moscow, the fire, and his arrest strip away his fantasies of assassinating Napoleon and place him among ordinary victims and prisoners.

Book Twelve: 1812

Summary: Book Twelve interweaves Mary and Natasha's vigil over the dying Prince Andrew with Pierre's captivity among the French. Andrew forgives Natasha, experiences a religious detachment from earthly love, and dies with Princess Mary, Natasha, and little Nicholas shadowing the scene. Pierre witnesses executions, meets Platon Karataev, learns from Karataev's roundness, stories, dog, potatoes, and acceptance, and begins to shed the self-conscious systems that had failed him. Meanwhile Petya Rostov joins Denisov, Dolokhov appears in disguise among the French, and partisan warfare gathers around the prisoners. Source anchors: Andrew forgives Natasha, Princess Mary, Platon Karataev, executions, potatoes, Petya, Denisov.

Analysis: Andrew forgiving Natasha and Princess Mary's presence complete his movement away from pride, ambition, and wounded vanity toward a love that is almost beyond life. The executions, Platon Karataev, potatoes, and the prison dog give Pierre a moral education rooted in bodily need and peasant speech rather than Masonic doctrine. Petya, Denisov, and Dolokhov prepare a cruel counterpoint, because youthful patriotic excitement is approaching the same partisan world that may free Pierre.

Book Thirteen: 1812

Summary: Book Thirteen follows the French retreat from Moscow, Napoleon's departure, Kutuzov's refusal to risk needless battle, the Russian army's hunger and exhaustion, prisoners marching in the cold, Pierre's weakened body, Karataev's illness, and the killing of stragglers. Tolstoy repeatedly rejects explanations based on Napoleon's will, preferring cold, hunger, distance, supply collapse, peasant resistance, and army disintegration. Denisov and Dolokhov prepare a raid on the French convoy, while Petya's excitement grows around Cossacks, prisoners, drums, dreams, and the chance to prove himself. Source anchors: French retreat, Kutuzov restraint, prisoners, Karataev illness, Denisov, Dolokhov, Petya.

Analysis: The French retreat and Kutuzov's restraint make patience a form of command, because the French army is being destroyed by conditions more surely than by theatrical attacks. Prisoners and Karataev's illness shift the campaign into Logistics and Throughput: feet, cold, distance, food, supply collapse, and exhausted bodies decide what imperial strategy can no longer command. Denisov, Dolokhov, and Petya bring romantic raiding energy back into the book just as Tolstoy is showing how little romance remains in war.

Book Fourteen: 1812

Summary: Book Fourteen delivers the partisan raid, Petya's final night, his dream of music, his generosity to the captured French drummer boy, Dolokhov's reconnaissance, Denisov's attack, the liberation of prisoners including Pierre, and Petya's death. Pierre emerges from captivity changed by Karataev, suffering, and bodily deprivation, while Natasha and Princess Mary continue to live through grief after Andrew. The French retreat continues through Krasnoe, Kutuzov remains cautious, and Tolstoy emphasizes the worn-out state of both pursuing Russians and fleeing Frenchmen. Source anchors: Petya's dream, French drummer, Dolokhov reconnaissance, Denisov attack, Pierre freed, Krasnoe, Kutuzov cautious.

Analysis: Petya's dream and the French drummer make his death more than a battlefield incident; they show innocence attaching itself to war at the very moment war kills it. Dolokhov reconnaissance, Denisov attack, and Pierre freed connect partisan initiative to rescue, but not to clean triumph, because Petya's body is the price inside the same action. Krasnoe and Kutuzov's caution continue Tolstoy's case that preservation of exhausted men matters more than the glory of annihilating an already ruined enemy.

Book Fifteen: 1812-13

Summary: Book Fifteen closes the war narrative with news of Petya's death reaching the Rostovs, Countess Rostova's collapse, Natasha's care for her mother, Princess Mary's arrival, and the two women's shared mourning for Andrew and Petya. Pierre recovers in Orel, hears of Helene's death, feels a new inward freedom, returns to Moscow, and gradually recognizes his love for Natasha. Kutuzov dies after the campaign, Alexander and European politics move beyond him, and Tolstoy distinguishes the people's war that saved Russia from the official campaign that resumes once Russia crosses its borders. Source anchors: Petya's death, Countess Rostova, Natasha cares, Princess Mary, Helene death, Pierre in Orel, Kutuzov dies.

Analysis: Petya's death, Countess Rostova, Natasha cares, and Princess Mary shift the cost of victory back into family rooms, where national deliverance cannot undo private loss. Helene's death and Pierre in Orel clear the social obstacles around Pierre without making his happiness feel cheaply earned, because captivity and grief have altered him first. Kutuzov dies as the people's war ends, allowing Tolstoy to define National Identity through defensive endurance in 1812 rather than through the less organic European campaigns that follow.

First Epilogue: 1813-20

Summary: The first epilogue shows the postwar households: Pierre marries Natasha, Nicholas marries Princess Mary, Sonya remains dependent and self-effacing, Countess Rostova is aged by grief, little Nicholas Bolkonski grows under memories of Prince Andrew, and the families gather at Bald Hills. Nicholas manages the estate, struggles with peasant administration, debt, and temper, while Mary keeps diaries of his moral progress and loves him with anxious intensity. Pierre returns from Petersburg with political talk, hints of secret societies and future Decembrist feeling, and a conversation that stirs young Nicholas Bolkonski's dream of heroic continuity with his father. Source anchors: Pierre and Natasha, Nicholas and Mary, Sonya, Bald Hills, little Nicholas, estate management, secret societies.

Analysis: Pierre and Natasha, Nicholas and Mary, and Sonya show peace as rearranged dependency rather than simple reward. Bald Hills and estate management make Nicholas's maturity practical: cavalry feeling becomes agriculture, accounts, discipline, and responsibility for peasants. Little Nicholas and the secret-society talk keep history moving beyond 1820, suggesting that Andrew's unfinished striving and Pierre's moral restlessness will reappear in the next generation.

Second Epilogue

Summary: The second epilogue turns from narrative to historical theory, challenging historians who explain events through Napoleon, Alexander, genius, power, or diplomatic decisions. Tolstoy argues that historical events arise from innumerable individual wills and that freedom appears in consciousness while necessity appears in retrospect. He compares historical science to astronomy before Copernicus, attacks the illusion that commands cause events directly, and revisits the 1812 campaign as evidence that armies, peoples, supplies, weather, roads, and millions of actions make history rather than sovereign intention alone. Source anchors: Napoleon, Alexander, innumerable wills, freedom, necessity, Copernicus, commands.

Analysis: Napoleon and Alexander are reduced from causes to labels attached after the fact, which completes the demolition begun at Austerlitz and Borodino. Innumerable wills, freedom, necessity, and commands make Language and Ideology the epilogue's target: Tolstoy argues that historians' words can smuggle sovereign intention back into events that actually arise from dispersed action. The Copernicus comparison makes the epilogue deliberately methodological, because Tolstoy wants history to stop placing great men at the center just as astronomy stopped placing Earth at the center.
