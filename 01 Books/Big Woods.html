Detailed overview

Faulkner builds Big Woods out of three linked hunting narratives and a long elegy for the Mississippi bottomland that once lay between the hill country and the Tallahatchie. The book is not a neutral sportsman’s memoir. It treats deer camps, bear dogs, bayous, Indian mounds, ferryboats, canebrakes, flood control, and cotton land as parts of one historical landscape in which Chickasaw sovereignty, slavery, plantation ownership, market hunting, and modern mechanised agriculture have all left marks. The oldest presences in the book are not the white hunters but people like Sam Fathers, Jobaker, Doom, Herman Basket, and John Basket, along with the deer, bear, foxes, and hounds who seem to know the country better than any map can.

The first story, “The Old People,” gives the moral grammar for everything that follows. Isaac’s first buck is not just a boyhood achievement but a rite supervised by Sam Fathers, who teaches him how to approach the animal, pull back its head, cut its throat, and accept the bloody consecration that follows. Faulkner makes the initiation inseparable from genealogy: Isaac is the white child of the McCaslin line, while Sam is the son of a Negro slave and the Chickasaw chief Doom, “Had-Two-Fathers,” carrying in his body both royalty and bondage. That mixed inheritance, and Isaac’s listening apprenticeship in the blacksmith shop and the campfire darkness, turns the woods into a place of instruction rather than recreation.

“A Bear Hunt” shifts into Ratliff’s comic voice, but the comedy keeps opening onto older, stranger material. Lucius “Butch” Hogganbeck, with his bear meat, whisky, and unstoppable hiccups, is ridiculous; Ratliff’s prank about John Basket curing him is ridiculous too. Yet the prank works because the Indian mound and the Chickasaw remnant around it are not just local colour. They still carry enough force, or enough remembered force, that Ash can send Hogganbeck running by staging a false ritual with firewood, lantern, and Indian language. The joke exposes how the white townsmen live on land whose older meanings they mock until those meanings are turned back on them.

The final piece, “Race at Morning,” brings the whole book into the twentieth century through the young camp boy’s chase with Mister Ernest, Dan, Eagle, Roth Edmonds, Uncle Ike McCaslin, Willy Legate, and Walter Ewell. On the surface it is a long deer run from bayou to ridge to bayou again, across multiple camps and missed shots, ending in the buck’s escape. Underneath, Faulkner is measuring a code of pursuit against the shrinking world left for it to inhabit. The boy, Mister Ernest, Eagle, and the old buck all become parallel figures, each proud of doing one thing well, while Isaac’s old-man reflections at the end acknowledge that railroads, levees, flood reservoirs, freight trains, and cotton gins have pushed the last Big Woods southward until the hunters and the wilderness seem to be running out together.

Faulkner’s prose keeps moving between those scales. One sentence can hold the intimate feel of a stirrup, a muddy dog, a shotgun hammer, or a broken saddle girth; the next can widen out to the Old Man River, Sherman’s march, neon towns, and the whole economy of timber, cotton, levees, bankruptcy, and floodwater. That oscillation is what makes the book cohere. The hunts matter because they are concrete acts performed by named people and animals in named places, but they also matter because they preserve, for a little while, an ethic of humility before a world that modern ownership and extraction have almost finished destroying.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Frontier Systems: Sam Fathers, Chickasaw inheritance, slavery, market hunting, and bottomland camps make the woods a layered frontier rather than a blank wilderness.
  • Ecological Constraint: bayous, canebrakes, levees, reservoirs, cotton land, and the shrinking Big Woods shape what hunters, hounds, and landowners can do.
  • Informal Institutions: hunting camps, neighborly jokes, old grudges, local memory, and ritualized initiation govern conduct outside formal law.
  • Knowledge Preservation: Isaac, Sam, Ratliff, and the camp stories preserve a disappearing landscape through remembered techniques, names, and moral codes.
  • Coercive Labor: the McCaslin inheritance and Sam Fathers's history keep slavery and plantation ownership inside the hunting stories' moral terrain.
  • Moral Economy: the book measures proper conduct by restraint, repayment, humility, and the refusal to convert every encounter with the woods into extraction.

Section-by-section notes

Old People

Summary: Isaac’s initiation begins on a cold, rainy November dawn when Sam Fathers places him against a tree, tells him to “shoot quick, and slow,” and leads him through the first worthy kill of his life: a buck whose throat Isaac cuts with Sam’s knife before Sam wipes hot blood across the boy’s face. Faulkner then enlarges that moment through Sam’s whole history as the son of Doom, the self-named Chickasaw chief Ikkemotubbe, and a quadroon slave woman, through the story of the French companion Soeur-Blonde de Vitry, the poisoned puppy and Moketubbe’s dead son, and the sale that made Sam “Had-Two-Fathers” under Carothers McCaslin. Isaac’s education continues in the blacksmith shop, in Jobaker’s visits, in McCaslin Edmonds’s explanation of Sam’s caged-lion eyes, and finally in the second buck scene where Walter Ewell kills only a spike while the true grandfather buck passes untouched as Sam raises his hand and says, “Oleh, Chief. Grandfather.” Source anchors: Sam Fathers, Doom, Soeur-Blonde de Vitry, Carothers McCaslin, Jobaker, Walter Ewell

Analysis: Sam Fathers and the blood-marking matter because they give Isaac a code of conduct before he has any independent status as a hunter; the rite says that killing the buck binds him to a discipline rather than granting him domination. Doom, Soeur-Blonde de Vitry, and Carothers McCaslin make Frontier Systems inseparable from slavery and dispossession: Sam’s authority comes out of Chickasaw rulership, slave commerce, and mixed descent, not white sporting tradition alone. Jobaker and Walter Ewell show the two ends of the story’s structure, with Jobaker preserving a nearly vanished indigenous world and Walter’s mistaken spike proving that the old chief buck is visible only to people, like Sam and Isaac, who have been taught to read the woods with reverence instead of haste.

A Bear Hunt

Summary: Ratliff arrives at Major de Spain’s annual camp to find Lucius Hogganbeck wrecked by almost twenty-four hours of hiccups brought on by whisky, bear meat, and camp excess, after he has already been driven from Uncle Ike McCaslin’s deer stand and mocked by everybody from Major de Spain to the standers on the levee. Ratliff jokingly tells him to seek a cure from old John Basket at the Indian mound five miles farther downriver, not realising that Ash, the camp servant, overhears the conversation and runs ahead to tell the Chickasaws that a new revenue agent is coming and needs a terrifying reception. Luke goes up to the mound with a lantern and shotgun, gets disarmed, hears Indian language around a pile of wood, believes he is about to be burned alive, bursts back into camp cured of hiccups and desperate for revenge, and only the next morning does Ratliff learn from Ash that the whole revenge began with Lucius burning the expensive blue celluloid collar off Ash’s neck at a Negro picnic twenty years earlier. Source anchors: Ratliff, Lucius Hogganbeck, Major de Spain, John Basket, Indian mound, Ash

Analysis: Ratliff and Lucius Hogganbeck matter because Faulkner sets the whole story on the fault line between comic storytelling and real humiliation; Ratliff thinks he is only stirring up camp fun, while Lucius is being driven half-mad by pain and fear. John Basket and the Indian mound show Informal Institutions working through memory and performance: the prank succeeds because the mound still carries inherited power, enough that a drunken white bully can imagine a Chickasaw trial and execution the instant the ritual begins. Ash’s blue collar and the old Negro picnic turn the story from camp farce into delayed retaliation, showing that Lucius’s earlier cruelty survived in Ash’s memory until the woods, the mound, and Ratliff’s joke provided a stage for repayment.

Race at Morning

Summary: The young black camp boy sees the great buck swimming back across the river the night before the season, tells Mister Ernest about the rocking-chair rack on its head, and rides with him and Dan after dawn while Simon handles Eagle and the young dogs, Uncle Ike McCaslin places the standers, and Roth Edmonds, Willy Legate, and Walter Ewell wait along the ridges and bayous. The race runs through the canebrake island, past Uncle Ike’s stand, beyond the Hog Bayou camp and the Hollyknowe shooters, across a disastrous grapevine jump that tears the saddle loose and flings boy, rider, and tack back across the bayou, and finally into the long afternoon when the old hounds fail one by one and Eagle alone keeps moving after the buck. In the last open glade Mister Ernest works the pump gun only to hear three empty clicks because he earlier unloaded the shells, letting the twelve-point buck pass in golden light, after which the narrative turns to Isaac McCaslin’s old-man meditation on flood-control reservoirs, freight trains, cotton fields, the Old Man River, and the shrinking country where he and the wilderness seem to be nearing their end together. Source anchors: Mister Ernest, Dan, Eagle, Uncle Ike McCaslin, Roth Edmonds, Old Man River

Analysis: Mister Ernest, Dan, and Eagle matter because Faulkner makes the chase a partnership of distinct skills rather than a simple hunt; the boy hears, Ernest rides and judges, Dan runs, and Eagle embodies the hound’s stubborn honour. Uncle Ike McCaslin and Roth Edmonds place that race inside a generational chain, with Isaac’s authority still governing the stands while younger men inherit a landscape already much smaller than the one he entered with Sam Fathers. The empty pump gun, the twelve-point buck, and the Old Man River make Ecological Constraint the final argument: Ernest’s decision not to fire recognises in that exhausted buck the same dwindling right-to-exist that Isaac sees in the last Big Woods, now surrounded by levees, reservoirs, freight trains, and cotton.
