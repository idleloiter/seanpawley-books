Nuclear Weapons and American Grand Strategy

Buy on Amazon — Nuclear Weapons and American Grand Strategy

Detailed overview

Francis J. Gavin's Nuclear Weapons and American Grand Strategy argues that the bomb cannot be treated as a specialist silo inside security studies. Gavin keeps returning to a harder question: how have nuclear weapons shaped the United States' larger effort to preserve security, freedom of action, alliances, and world order since 1945? The book begins from the fact that nuclear weapons have not been used since Hiroshima and Nagasaki, but it refuses to treat nonuse as proof that deterrence simply worked. Berlin, Cuba, Able Archer 83, command-and-control failures, nuclear accidents, and near misses all sit beside the Long Peace, leaving Gavin to insist that humility is not optional.

The early chapters are a critique of methods and inherited categories. Gavin challenges Kenneth Waltz's optimistic proliferation claims, the Waltz-Sagan "franchise," and quantitative studies by Todd Sechser, Matthew Fuhrmann, and Matthew Kroenig because they often code nuclear events more cleanly than the evidence permits. The 1946 Azerbaijan crisis, the Berlin crisis of 1958-1962, the Cuban missile crisis, Khrushchev's competing motives, American worries over a nuclearized Bundeswehr, and Kennedy-era first-strike discussions all show how hard it is to separate deterrence from compellence, resolve from interest, and nuclear superiority from usable political leverage.

The book's central positive contribution is the idea of U.S. "strategies of inhibition." Gavin argues that American grand strategy after World War II cannot be described only as containment of great-power rivals and openness of the world economy. The United States also made a persistent effort to slow, halt, reverse, or mitigate independent nuclear programs, even among allies. West Germany, South Korea, Taiwan, Pakistan, France, Israel, India, China, Iran, Iraq, and North Korea appear not as isolated proliferation cases but as evidence that Washington understood nuclear weapons as power equalizers that could constrain U.S. freedom of action.

Gavin's NATO chapter gives the inhibition argument its sharpest historical case. NATO's radical nuclear posture, MC-48, massive retaliation, West German rearmament, the European Defense Community failure, flexible response, MC-14/3, Pershing II, Trident D-5, B-1, MX, cruise missiles, and C3I all served more than deterrence against the Soviet Union. They were also part of the bargain that kept the Federal Republic of Germany protected, embedded, and non-nuclear. The alliance generated repeated crises inside NATO, with Moscow, and in Washington, but Gavin argues that this continual tension may have been the price of solving the German problem in a nuclear world.

The later chapters broaden from Cold War history to nuclear futures. Gavin proposes MIDAR, multiple and interactive deterrence, assurance, and reassurance, to capture the fact that U.S. nuclear policy aims at allies, adversaries, and neutrals at the same time. He also asks how to write "the history of what did not happen," because thermonuclear war is absent from the historical record while the policies designed to prevent it remain everywhere. Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Tsutomu Yamaguchi, the Tsar Bomba, SIOP-62, the Hiroshima Peace Museum, Obama in Prague, the Four Horsemen, Acheson-Lilienthal, virtual deterrents, cyber and space capabilities, and the 2017 UN ban treaty all feed the final claim: the United States may need to preserve some benefits of deterrence while finding ways to reduce dependence on nuclear threats whose credibility and legitimacy are eroding.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

One: History and the Unanswered Questions of the Nuclear Age

Summary: Gavin opens by asking why nuclear weapons have not been used since 1945 and why that nonuse is so difficult to explain with confidence. He surveys the nuclear-studies renaissance, new archives, and cases involving Brazil, Germany, Iran, Israel, Italy, Japan, Pakistan, Romania, South Korea, and Sweden, then stresses that categories such as recessed deterrent, threshold state, latency, hedging, opacity, and nuclear reversal complicate the old nuclear/non-nuclear binary. The chapter frames four recurring questions: whether nuclear weapons made the world safer, whether they compel as well as deter, why states pursue them, and what arsenals and strategies are ideal once they have them. It sets Barack Obama's 2009 Prague speech, Harry Truman, Ronald Reagan, North Korea, Iran, Russia, China, Pakistan, and the $350 billion U.S. modernization plan inside the disarmament-versus-deterrence conflict. Source anchors: 1945 nonuse; Brazil; latency; hedging; Prague speech; North Korea; $350 billion modernization; disarmament-versus-deterrence.

Analysis: The 1945 nonuse question is the book's starting constraint because Gavin treats it as a methodological problem rather than an easy victory for deterrence theory. Brazil, latency, and hedging show that proliferation is not a binary switch, so policy built around simple nuclear/non-nuclear coding misses the political middle ground where many states operate. Prague speech and $350 billion modernization show the United States pulled in opposite directions, while North Korea turns the same intellectual issue into an active policy problem. Disarmament-versus-deterrence is therefore not a debate Gavin wants settled by slogans; it is a test of whether scholarship can handle uncertainty.

Two: Fixing the Franchise: The Ivory Tower-Policy Gap

Summary: Gavin uses Kenneth Waltz's proliferation writings to criticize a celebrated academic debate that he thinks has too little historical texture and too little policy empathy. He contrasts Waltz's Theory of International Politics, where bipolarity and structure explain stability, with Waltz's later 1981 Adelphi paper and The Spread of Nuclear Weapons, where fear of nuclear war leads him to welcome gradual proliferation. Gavin then compares Waltz and Scott Sagan on Iran, safety, the Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty, Italy, organizational risks, and American coercive tools. The chapter argues that decisionmakers confronting Iran, North Korea, Israel, regional cascades, terrorism, Russia, China, preventive strikes, and economic consequences need better help than selective history and deductive confidence. Source anchors: Kenneth Waltz; 1981 Adelphi paper; The Spread of Nuclear Weapons; Scott Sagan; Iran; Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty; Italy; preventive strikes.

Analysis: Kenneth Waltz and the 1981 Adelphi paper let Gavin show a theorist moving from structural explanation to a more moralized proliferation preference without fully accounting for the change. Scott Sagan improves on Waltz by taking organizations and safety seriously, but Gavin uses Italy and the Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty to show where Sagan's emphasis still leaves American umbrellas and coercion underexamined. Iran and preventive strikes make the ivory-tower problem concrete: policymakers cannot avoid choices, and a theory that treats their worries as childish cannot explain why the same worries recur across administrations.

Three: What We Talk about When We Talk about Nuclear Weapons

Summary: Gavin turns to quantitative nuclear-crisis scholarship by examining Todd Sechser and Matthew Fuhrmann's work on "Crisis Bargaining and Nuclear Blackmail" and Matthew Kroenig's work on "Nuclear Superiority and the Balance of Resolve." He begins with Harry Truman's claimed 1946 Azerbaijan ultimatum, George V. Allen's doubts, Averell Harriman, James Byrnes, Stalin, Iranian oil negotiations, and Bernard Brodie's The Absolute Weapon to show how quickly nuclear coercion becomes evidentiary murk. The chapter then tests coding choices against the Berlin crisis of 1958-1962, Khrushchev's November 10, 1958 ultimatum, West Berlin, Llewellyn Thompson, Eisenhower, Kennedy, McGeorge Bundy, Dean Acheson, sub-SIOP planning, Carl Kaysen, SALT, ABM, Pershing II, Trident D-5, and Soviet perceptions of U.S. counterforce capability. Source anchors: 1946 Azerbaijan; George V. Allen; Bernard Brodie; Sechser and Fuhrmann; Matthew Kroenig; Berlin crisis; Llewellyn Thompson; sub-SIOP.

Analysis: 1946 Azerbaijan and George V. Allen show why Gavin distrusts clean data rows: even participants disagreed about whether an ultimatum existed, so the event cannot be coded without smuggling a theory into the datum. Sechser and Fuhrmann and Matthew Kroenig give him two opposed statistical claims, one minimizing nuclear compellence and one stressing nuclear superiority, but the Berlin crisis resists both because Khrushchev, Eisenhower, Kennedy, and Llewellyn Thompson each read the same deployments as defense of a vital position rather than as a legible signal of resolve. That contest turns the chapter into a case for Intelligence: nuclear coercion generates evidence that adversaries actively hide, misread, and reinterpret, and the analyst who treats contested perception as a clean variable inherits the policy failure, not the explanation. Sub-SIOP planning makes the point sharper, because counterforce capability mattered only insofar as Moscow perceived it, and Moscow's perception moved through its own threat categories rather than through American coding schemes.

Four: Strategies of Inhibition

Summary: Gavin names nuclear nonproliferation and counterproliferation a third pillar of U.S. grand strategy alongside containment and openness. His "strategies of inhibition" include treaties, norms, diplomacy, aid, arms sales, alliances, security guarantees, export controls, intelligence, counterforce postures, missile defense, sanctions, coercion, interdiction, sabotage, and preventive military threats applied to friends and enemies alike. The chapter explains why the United States pressured West Germany, Taiwan, South Korea, Pakistan, France, Israel, Sweden, Iraq, Iran, and North Korea, why it worked with the Soviet Union on the 1968 Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty, and why the Gilpatric Committee worried after China's 1964 test about tipping points involving Japan and South Korea. It argues that inhibition protects the United States from attack, catalytic war, proliferation cascades, loss of freedom of action, ally independence, emboldened adversaries, and erosion of U.S. power advantages. Source anchors: strategies of inhibition; containment; openness; West Germany; Taiwan; Gilpatric Committee; 1968 Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty; catalytic war.

Analysis: Strategies of inhibition is the book's key addition because it explains policies that containment and openness cannot fully cover, and it names a concrete execution problem rather than a diplomatic posture. West Germany and Taiwan show why Gavin insists the United States inhibited allies as well as adversaries, while the Gilpatric Committee and catalytic war identify fears that were not reducible to Soviet containment. The 1968 Nuclear Nonproliferation Treaty matters because cooperation with Moscow appears anomalous unless inhibition is treated as a separate mission. Inhibition is therefore a case for State Capacity: Washington converted treaties, export controls, security guarantees, intelligence, sanctions, interdiction, sabotage, and preventive threats into working channels that coerced friends, reassured clients, and bargained with enemies to keep nuclear control centralized. Containment and openness describe what the United States wanted; inhibition describes the administrative machinery that actually moved allied and adversary weapons programs in the intended direction.

Five: NATO's Radical Response to the Nuclear Revolution

Summary: Gavin reinterprets NATO not as a conventional anti-Soviet alliance alone but as a radical nuclear arrangement that protected Western Europe while inhibiting West German nuclearization. He traces MC-48, NSC-68, China in the Korean War, thermonuclear testing in 1952, West German rearmament, the failed European Defense Community, Lord Ismay's formula about keeping Americans in, Russians out, and Germans down, and the dependence of massive retaliation on U.S. nuclear superiority. He then challenges the neat break between massive retaliation and flexible response by discussing Robert McNamara's Athens speech, MC-14/3, no-first-use refusal, Pershing II, Trident D-5, B-1, MX, cruise missiles, C3I, antisubmarine warfare, SS-20s, Euromissiles, and NATO expansion to Poland. The chapter concludes that NATO generated constant crisis but may have stabilized the German problem in a nuclear world. Source anchors: MC-48; West German rearmament; European Defense Community; Lord Ismay; flexible response; Pershing II; SS-20s; NATO expansion.

Analysis: MC-48 and West German rearmament make NATO's nuclear posture inseparable from the Federal Republic's political status, not just Soviet tanks, and Lord Ismay's formula about keeping Americans in, Russians out, and Germans down names the arrangement explicitly. The European Defense Community shows the alliance solving three problems at once: American presence, Soviet deterrence, and German restraint. That triple mandate turns NATO into a case for Empire and Periphery: the alliance deters a rival center while binding a powerful ally into a dependent, non-nuclear role, so the Federal Republic gains protection only by surrendering the independent capability that protection is meant to make unnecessary. Flexible response and Pershing II reveal continuity beneath changed rhetoric, because the United States still sought credible early-use and counterforce options after parity. SS-20s and NATO expansion extend the logic beyond the 1950s, suggesting that alliance crises were part of maintaining nonproliferation rather than failures beside it.

Six: Beyond Deterrence

Summary: Gavin argues that classic nuclear deterrence theory captures only part of U.S. nuclear statecraft because the United States has rarely needed nuclear weapons to prevent invasion of its homeland. He asks why a country protected by two oceans, weak neighbors, command of the commons, and overwhelming conventional, economic, and soft-power advantages built huge nuclear arsenals, sophisticated delivery systems, and aggressive strategies. The chapter introduces MIDAR, multiple and interactive deterrence, assurance, and reassurance, to show that U.S. nuclear weapons aim simultaneously at allies, adversaries, and neutrals through basic deterrence, extended deterrence, assurance, reassurance, compellence, and coercion. It also emphasizes methodological obstacles: secrecy, declassified-document gaps, Reid Pauly's rhetorical evaporation, proxy variables, Cold War conflation, disciplinary stove-pipes, and moral disagreement between deterrence and disarmament advocates. Source anchors: MIDAR; two oceans; command of the commons; extended deterrence; Reid Pauly; rhetorical evaporation; proxy variables; moral disagreement.

Analysis: MIDAR gives Gavin a vocabulary for missions that basic deterrence cannot explain, especially assurance and reassurance of allies. Two oceans and command of the commons sharpen the puzzle: homeland invasion was not the main U.S. problem, so nuclear policy must be tied to broader grand-strategic aims. Extended deterrence links nuclear forces to alliance management, while Reid Pauly, rhetorical evaporation, and proxy variables explain why the historical record is so hard to read. Moral disagreement then prevents the analysis from becoming purely technical, because every deterrence claim involves willingness to threaten catastrophe.

Seven: The History of What Did Not Happen

Summary: Gavin reflects on how historians can study thermonuclear war, the event that never occurred, without pretending that absence proves any single theory. He recounts how his own research on flexible response, the German question, and U.S.-Soviet cooperation over nonproliferation changed his view of the Cold War and challenged strategy literature that collapsed nuclear rivalry into superpower geopolitics. The chapter revisits the 2002 U.S. National Security Strategy, Iraq, Iran, Mao's China, preventive inclinations against the Soviet Union, China, and North Korea, Mira Rapp-Hopper's work, France in the 1950s, Pakistan, South Korea, Taiwan, Berlin, Cuba, SALT, SS-20s, and the Euromissile crisis. It ends by invoking Hiroshima, Nagasaki, Enola Gay, U-235, Nagasaki's plutonium device, Kenneth Waltz's nuclear Iran claim, and John Keegan's warning that a nuclear leader's first duty may be not to act. Source anchors: flexible response; German question; 2002 U.S. National Security Strategy; Mao's China; Mira Rapp-Hopper; SALT; Enola Gay; John Keegan.

Analysis: Flexible response and the German question show Gavin's own movement from inherited strategy categories toward archival skepticism, and the 2002 U.S. National Security Strategy and Mao's China prevent him from treating post-9/11 preventive thinking as wholly unprecedented. Mira Rapp-Hopper connects Soviet, Chinese, North Korean, and allied cases across administrations, while SALT and the Euromissile crisis demonstrate how arms control could create political side effects that strategists underplayed. The chapter's deeper move is methodological: thermonuclear war is the event that never occurred, yet the policies designed to prevent it filled the whole Cold War. That absence makes the chapter a case for Crisis Governance, because the historian must study a catastrophe that was decided against under uncertainty and cannot be validated by the normal test of occurrence. Enola Gay and John Keegan bring the chapter back to humility: the historian can unsettle false certainty, but cannot prove exactly why catastrophe did not happen, so deterrence claims must survive as bets made under pressure rather than as confirmed explanations.

Eight: Deterring while Disarming

Summary: Gavin opens with the Hiroshima Peace Museum, August 6, 1945, Korean slave laborers, the loss of more than 90 percent of Hiroshima's medical personnel, yellow rain, 80,000 to 140,000 deaths, Nagasaki, and the later scale of thermonuclear weapons. He compares Hiroshima-sized bombs with the fifty-megaton Tsar Bomba, SIOP-62's projected 108 million Soviet deaths, 104 million Chinese deaths, and 2.6 million Polish deaths, then follows Cold War awareness through Bikini Atoll, the Cuban missile crisis, John F. Kennedy's one-in-three-to-even estimate, On the Beach, Fail Safe, Dr. Strangelove, Euromissiles, Wargames, and The Day After. The chapter then stages the debate between Woodrow Wilson's arms-control vision, Walter Lippmann, Robert Jervis's security dilemma, Obama in Prague, the $1 trillion modernization plan, the 2017 UN ban negotiations, the Four Horsemen, Margaret Thatcher, Noam Chomsky, Acheson-Lilienthal, virtual deterrents, conventional and cyber capabilities, and Tsutomu Yamaguchi. Source anchors: Hiroshima Peace Museum; Tsar Bomba; SIOP-62; Bikini Atoll; Prague; Four Horsemen; virtual deterrents; Tsutomu Yamaguchi.

Analysis: Hiroshima Peace Museum, Tsar Bomba, and SIOP-62 keep the chapter grounded in physical destruction before Gavin turns to policy trade-offs. Bikini Atoll and the Cuban missile crisis explain why public fear once made nuclear danger visible, while Prague and the Four Horsemen show elite disarmament ambition colliding with modernization. Virtual deterrents and cyber capabilities are possible escape routes from the deterrence-disarmament binary, not settled answers. Tsutomu Yamaguchi gives the closing moral authority to someone whose body experienced both Hiroshima and Nagasaki, making any abstract comfort with nuclear policy harder to sustain.

Nine: Rethinking Nuclear Weapons and American Grand Strategy

Summary: Gavin concludes by asking what nuclear weapons are for in U.S. grand strategy and why public and expert debates so rarely probe that basic question. He criticizes the nuclear revolution school associated with Bernard Brodie, Thomas Schelling, Glenn Snyder, Robert Jervis, Kenneth Waltz, Stephen Van Evera, Stephen Walt, Charles Glaser, Chaim Kauffman, John Mearsheimer, Joshua Rovner, and Todd Sechser and Matthew Fuhrmann because it cannot explain U.S. exceptional behavior. He points to $5.5 trillion in U.S. nuclear spending from 1940 to 1996, an additional $1.2 trillion over thirty years, nuclear sharing, first-use willingness, predelegation, Nixon's hatred of MAD, SALT I, SALT II, ABM, Pershing II, MX, Trident D-5, cruise missiles, missile defense, antisubmarine warfare, and C3I as evidence of persistent nuclear activism. He pairs this with nuclear abstinence, nonproliferation, mutual vulnerability rhetoric, global norms, Pope Francis, the 2017 UN prohibition treaty, AI and autonomous systems, and Beatrice Fihn's warning that nuclear war leaves no other agenda. Source anchors: nuclear revolution school; $5.5 trillion; $1.2 trillion; Nixon; Pershing II; nuclear abstinence; Pope Francis; Beatrice Fihn.

Analysis: The nuclear revolution school gives Gavin a foil because its defensive logic predicts small arsenals, alliance obsolescence, and relaxed proliferation attitudes that U.S. policy did not follow. The $5.5 trillion and $1.2 trillion figures make the scale of nuclear activism impossible to dismiss as bureaucratic drift, while Nixon, Pershing II, and Trident D-5 show qualitative primacy continuing after rhetorical acceptance of parity. Nuclear abstinence and Pope Francis capture the opposite impulse, the desire to delegitimize and eventually reduce the bomb. Beatrice Fihn's warning closes the book by making grand strategy subordinate to survival: no strategic agenda matters if nuclear war ends the agenda itself.
