Red Famine: Stalin's War on Ukraine

Buy on Amazon — Red Famine: Stalin's War on Ukraine

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer.

Detailed overview

Anne Applebaum’s Red Famine is organized as a causal history of the Holodomor, not only as a narrative of mass hunger. The book begins with the Ukrainian national question: geography without secure natural borders, Kyivan Rus, the Dnieper, the black-earth grain belt, the Zaporozhian Cossacks, Taras Shevchenko, Mykhailo Hrushevsky, the Russian imperial bans on Ukrainian language, and Galicia’s different experience under Austria-Hungary. Applebaum uses that background to show why Stalin treated Ukrainian peasant resistance as more than an economic failure. In his mind, Ukrainian grain, Ukrainian language, Ukrainian schools, Ukrainian communists, and Polish border fears belonged to the same political danger.

The early chapters place the famine inside the unresolved wars of 1917-21. The Central Rada, Hrushevsky, Symon Petliura, Pavlo Skoropadsky, the Directory, Nestor Makhno, Denikin’s White Army, Jozef Pilsudski, the Red Army, pogrom violence, and the Bolshevik experience of near-defeat all appear as prerequisites for later policy. Applebaum’s point is that Stalin and the OGPU did not imagine Ukraine from nowhere in 1932; they remembered a place where peasant armies, national intellectuals, foreign allies, and armed borderlands had once nearly broken Bolshevik control. The 1921 famine, the American Relief Administration, Lenin’s hostage orders, and the delayed relief for southern Ukraine then become a rehearsal in both requisition and selective compassion.

The middle of the book follows the tightening of Soviet policy from the New Economic Policy into collectivization. Stalin’s turn against Bukharin, the Twenty-Five Thousanders, the komnezamy, Machine Tractor Stations, trudodni, de-kulakization, livestock slaughter, women’s revolts, Pavlohrad, Kryvyi Rih, Tulchyn, and the OGPU’s obsession with Petliurites show how peasant resistance was interpreted through civil-war memory. Applebaum keeps the reader inside the institutions that transformed policy failure into accusations: the Ukrainian Communist Party, Kosior, Kaganovich, Molotov, Balytsky, Postyshev, Stalin’s correspondence from Sochi, and the August 1932 law that made gleaning or taking a few wheat ears punishable like treason.

The famine itself is presented as a sequence of decisions. The procurement quota of 5.8 million tonnes, the blacklists, the border closures, the confiscation of seed grain, the search brigades with iron rods, the removal of potatoes, beets, cows, dogs, cats, cooking pots, clothing, and money, and the simultaneous purge of Ukrainization are treated as mechanisms that intensified hunger and prevented escape. Applebaum distinguishes the broader Soviet famine from the Ukrainian case by pointing to the explicit linkage of agricultural failure with Ukrainian language policy, national communists, Ukrainian teachers, Hrushevsky, Skrypnyk, Khvylovyi, the Budynok Slovo writers, churches, dictionaries, museums, and libraries.

The later chapters slow down inside starvation. Applebaum names scurvy, oedema, swollen bellies, night-blindness, typhus, cannibalism, dead children at school desks, corpses by roads, bread lines in Kharkiv, Torgsin shops, orphanages, labour brigades, and desperate survival foods: linden bark, acacia blossoms, pigweed, frogs, cats, dogs, field mice stores, rotten potatoes, nettles, and cow milk. The book also insists on the moral injury of famine: neighbours informing on neighbours, hungry activists stealing food, children denouncing or fearing adults, mothers giving children to orphanages, and people learning that state institutions could both kill and save.

The aftermath and cover-up carry the story into memory politics. Applebaum tracks 3.9 million direct Ukrainian excess deaths, 0.6 million lost births, the destruction of local death registries, the suppressed 1937 census, Walter Duranty’s denial, Gareth Jones and Malcolm Muggeridge, Alexander Wienerberger’s photographs, Vatican hesitation, foreign consular reports, diaspora protests, the U.S. congressional commission, James Mace, Ivan Drach’s Chernobyl comparison, glasnost, the 1991 independence vote, and the 1993 commemorations. The epilogue then returns to Raphael Lemkin, genocide law, Viktor Yushchenko, Viktor Yanukovych, Dmitry Medvedev, the Russian denial campaign, Crimea, Donbas, and the continuing argument over Ukrainian sovereignty. The book’s final claim is historical and political: the famine did not destroy Ukraine, but it damaged Ukrainian institutions, language politics, demography, trust, and memory in ways that continued into the post-Soviet conflict with Russia.

Core concepts and linkages

  • State Capacity: Applebaum shows famine as an administrative achievement of quotas, blacklists, police, sealed borders, search brigades, courts, censuses, and archives.
  • Language and Ideology: the attack on Ukrainization, dictionaries, schools, writers, churches, and later Russian denial makes language policy part of the violence.
  • Legibility: requisition records, death certificates, censuses, internal OGPU reports, village lists, and later demographic reconstruction determine what the state could see and what it tried to erase.
  • Coercive Labor: collectivization, trudodni, grain procurement, forced searches, deportations, and labour brigades tie hunger to extraction and compulsory work.
  • Knowledge Preservation: diaries, testimony, diaspora memoirs, photographs, censuses, archives, and memorial politics preserve a history the Soviet state tried to suppress.
  • National Identity: the book’s causal argument links grain policy to Ukrainian sovereignty, language, civic institutions, and the long conflict over whether Ukraine is a nation.
  • Crisis Governance: relief choices, border closures, Torgsin, orphanages, canteens, suppressed warnings, and later public-health-style remembrance show institutions responding to crisis by saving, starving, or silencing people.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Preface

Summary: The preface sets out the famine’s warning signs and the book’s method. Applebaum begins in early 1932 with secret police reports from Ukraine, the North Caucasus, the Volga region, and western Siberia describing swollen children, families eating grass and acorns, corpses near Odessa, and collective farmers writing to Stalin that they had not had bread since 1 January. She names Stalin, the Soviet Politburo, grain requisitions, kulak deportations, 5 million Soviet famine deaths, more than 3.9 million Ukrainian deaths, the Holodomor, Mykola Skrypnyk’s suicide, Raphael Lemkin’s genocide concept, Robert Conquest, Ukrainian archives in Kyiv, the Harvard Ukrainian Research Institute, and the Maidan and Crimea context in which she wrote. Source anchors: Odessa corpses; Stalin; Holodomor; Mykola Skrypnyk; Raphael Lemkin; Kyiv archives.

Analysis: Odessa corpses establish that the famine was visible before the worst decisions were complete, while Stalin and the Soviet Politburo are named because Applebaum is assigning responsibility to institutions and people rather than to weather. Holodomor and Mykola Skrypnyk connect hunger to the destruction of Ukrainian political and cultural life. Raphael Lemkin and Kyiv archives explain the book’s evidentiary position: it treats testimony, demographic work, and opened post-1991 records as the basis for revisiting a crime long denied.

Introduction: The Ukrainian Question

Summary: The introduction explains why Ukraine’s geography, language, and colonial history mattered before Stalin entered the story. Applebaum moves from Taras Shevchenko’s “Zapovit” to the Dnieper River, the East European Plain, Carpathian borders, black earth, Kyivan Rus, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, the Russian empire, Bohdan Khmelnytsky, Ivan Mazepa, Galicia, Volhynia, Podolia, the Ukrainian language, Jewish shtetls, Prosvita in Lviv, the 1876 ban on Ukrainian books and theatre, Donbas industrialization, and the 1917 collapse of empires. She shows a national movement rooted in peasants, language, Sunday schools, folklore, and civil associations but constantly constrained by imperial claims that Ukrainian was only a dialect or that Ukraine was merely “Little Russia.” Source anchors: Taras Shevchenko; Dnieper River; black earth; Kyivan Rus; Prosvita; Little Russia.

Analysis: Taras Shevchenko and Dnieper River make national identity literary and geographic at once, which is why later attacks on language and countryside are linked. Black earth explains why imperial rulers wanted Ukrainian territory, while Kyivan Rus and Little Russia show competing historical claims over the same past. Prosvita matters because it represents the voluntary civic institutions that the Soviet state later understood as politically dangerous rather than folkloric.

1 The Ukrainian Revolution, 1917

Summary: Chapter 1 follows the Ukrainian Revolution after the February 1917 fall of Tsar Nicholas II. It opens with the 1 April 1917 Kyiv march, blue-and-yellow flags, red socialist banners, Taras Shevchenko portraits, the Central Rada, and Mykhailo Hrushevsky’s call to build a free Ukraine. Applebaum then traces the All-Ukrainian National Congress, the First Universal, land reform promises, Ukrainianized battalions, Heorhii Narbut’s banknotes and stamps, Volodymyr Vynnychenko, Borotbysty peasant socialism, foreign recognition after the January 1918 independence declaration, the Brest-Litovsk treaty, Pavlo Skoropadsky’s hetmanate, Symon Petliura’s Directory, and the Bolsheviks’ small urban base in Donetsk and Kryvyi Rih. Source anchors: Central Rada; Mykhailo Hrushevsky; First Universal; Brest-Litovsk; Pavlo Skoropadsky; Symon Petliura.

Analysis: Central Rada and Mykhailo Hrushevsky show how historians, writers, and civic organizers became state builders when imperial authority collapsed. First Universal and Brest-Litovsk prove that Ukrainian independence was declared, negotiated, and recognized, even if it lacked coercive institutions. Pavlo Skoropadsky and Symon Petliura matter because their failed regimes left Bolsheviks with a memory of Ukrainian politics as unstable, armed, peasant, and vulnerable to foreign alliances.

2 Rebellion, 1919

Summary: Chapter 2 examines the civil-war violence that shaped Bolshevik fear of Ukraine. Applebaum moves through the Red Army, Denikin’s White Army, Makhno’s Black Army, the Directory, Heinrich Epp’s Mennonite testimony from Steinbach, Rosa the Kyiv torturer, poor peasants’ committees, forced grain seizures, and pogroms against Jews. She names Maksim’s 1914 anti-Jewish letter, Tsar Nicholas II’s anti-semitism, the Protocols of the Elders of Zion, Proskuriv, Denikin’s officers, Petliura’s official efforts to discourage pogroms, Sholom Schwartzbard’s later murder of Petliura in Paris, Pilsudski’s alliance, the 1920 Polish-Ukrainian campaign, Kyiv’s tram-car occupation, the Miracle on the Vistula, and Trotsky’s warning that Soviet power in Ukraine rested on Moscow and the Russian Red Army. Source anchors: Makhno's Black Army; Heinrich Epp; Proskuriv; Sholom Schwartzbard; Pilsudski; Trotsky.

Analysis: Makhno's Black Army and Heinrich Epp show that the countryside had learned to live under armed force rather than legal authority. Proskuriv and Sholom Schwartzbard let Applebaum register the pogrom catastrophe without reducing Ukrainian nationalism to Soviet caricature. Pilsudski and Trotsky are essential because they show why Moscow remembered Ukraine as a place where peasant revolt, national independence, and foreign armies could combine against Bolshevik rule.

3 Famine and Truce, the 1920s

Summary: Chapter 3 describes the uneasy peace after the Bolsheviks defeated Petliura, Denikin, Makhno, and Pilsudski. Applebaum follows the move of the Ukrainian capital to Kharkiv, Mikhail Frunze’s mostly Russian Red Army in Ukraine, War Communism, Stalin’s Ukrainian Labour Army, the renewed prodrazvyorstka, komnezamy, Matvii Havryliuk’s requisition work, drought, the collapse of grain production from 20 million tonnes before the revolution to 2.9 million in 1921, Odessa’s fall to 12.9 percent of previous levels, Volga refugees, cannibalism, Pravda’s famine announcement, Maxim Gorky’s appeal, Herbert Hoover’s American Relief Administration, Lenin’s hostility to Hoover, the ARA feeding 11 million people, and Moscow’s early refusal to let aid operate in southern Ukraine. Source anchors: Kharkiv capital; prodrazvyorstka; Matvii Havryliuk; American Relief Administration; Herbert Hoover; southern Ukraine.

Analysis: Kharkiv capital and prodrazvyorstka show that the new Ukrainian republic had formal institutions but policy remained driven by Moscow’s requisition needs. Matvii Havryliuk provides a continuity between civil-war grain squads and later village activists. American Relief Administration, Herbert Hoover, and southern Ukraine matter because 1921 demonstrates an alternative path: public acknowledgement and foreign aid saved lives in Russia, while delayed recognition in Ukraine killed people unnecessarily.

4 The Double Crisis, 1927-9

Summary: Chapter 4 follows two linked crises: renewed grain pressure and renewed suspicion of Ukrainian national culture. Applebaum describes the end of the NEP mood, OGPU reports on “political moods,” Balytsky’s claims about Ukrainian chauvinism, anonymous letters invoking Petliura Street and “Ukraine has not died yet,” peasant hopes for a Polish invasion, the SVU case against the Union for the Liberation of Ukraine, Mykhailo Hrushevsky’s mounting surveillance, teachers, musicians in Odessa receiving yellow and blue flowers, Ukrainian Academy of Science monitoring, 60,000 Ukrainians from Poland, and the 1928-9 smaller famine that killed at least 23,000 directly and 80,000 through disease and starvation effects. Source anchors: NEP; OGPU reports; Petliura Street; SVU case; yellow and blue flowers; 1928-9 famine.

Analysis: NEP is the temporary truce whose breakdown makes rural fear plausible, while OGPU reports and Petliura Street show how police language joined food collection to separatism. The SVU case and yellow and blue flowers reveal that culture and language were being treated as security risks before the Holodomor. The 1928-9 famine functions as warning evidence: hunger appeared under emergency methods, yet officials treated the response as a policing problem.

5 Collectivization: Revolution in the Countryside, 1930

Summary: Chapter 5 opens with Miron Dolot’s village on the Tiasmyn River, where Comrade Zeitlin and other outsiders arrive to impose collective farming on people who had still lived through local self-government, Christmas carols, private land, cows, horses, tools, and church life. Applebaum explains Stalin’s November 1929 acceleration, the defeat of Bukharin, the People’s Commissariat for Agriculture, kolkhozes, Machine Tractor Stations, trudodni, the Twenty-Five Thousanders, Lev Kopelev, Nadya’s ecstatic organizing letter, Pasha Angelina’s class-war memoir, poor peasants’ committees, Matvii Havryliuk’s return, teachers and Komsomol recruits, and the mix of ideology, ambition, fear, and criminal opportunity that made collectivization enforceable. Source anchors: Miron Dolot; Tiasmyn River; Comrade Zeitlin; Twenty-Five Thousanders; Lev Kopelev; Pasha Angelina.

Analysis: Miron Dolot and Tiasmyn River give collectivization a village scale before Applebaum moves into policy language. Comrade Zeitlin and Twenty-Five Thousanders embody the urban outsiders who had ideological authority but little agricultural knowledge. Lev Kopelev and Pasha Angelina show two sides of collaboration: one later haunted by belief, the other writing within socialist-realist hatred of kulaks. The chapter explains how Moscow’s order required local helpers to become meaningful.

6 Rebellion, 1930

Summary: Chapter 6 shows that collectivization produced immediate resistance, not passive acceptance. Applebaum describes livestock slaughter after peasants were ordered to surrender cows, pigs, sheep, horses, seed grain, and tools; Sholokhov’s fictional livestock bloodbath; a mare in Ekaterinovka labelled a “kulak agitator”; peasants calling collective farms a second serfdom; Antichrist language around church closures; graffiti against Stalin; border marches toward Poland from Kamianets-Podilskyi; babski bunty by women; Stalin’s “Dizzy with Success” article; March Fever; Balytsky’s OGPU reports; 2,000 mass protests in Ukraine; Pavlohrad, Bohdanivka, Kryvyi Rih, Tulchyn, and the use of cavalry, machine guns, arrests, and executions to suppress revolt. Source anchors: livestock slaughter; Ekaterinovka mare; Antichrist; Dizzy with Success; Pavlohrad; Tulchyn.

Analysis: Livestock slaughter and Ekaterinovka mare show resistance aimed at property before it became open violence. Antichrist and Dizzy with Success explain why religious fear and Stalin’s tactical blame-shifting both widened rebellion. Pavlohrad and Tulchyn matter because they convinced the OGPU and Stalin that Ukraine’s 1919 past had returned, even when many protests were driven by immediate survival rather than organized nationalism.

7 Collectivization Fails, 1931-2

Summary: Chapter 7 follows the consequences of forced collectivization into the first visible hunger of 1932. Applebaum describes villages in Odessa province such as Kozyrivka and Tarasivka, where medical teams found households emptying, corpses unburied, families eating carrion, boiled horsehide, dead horse bones, and yellow broth. She tracks OGPU reports of swollen villagers, starving horses, Kharkiv, Kyiv, Odessa, Dnipropetrovsk, and Vinnytsia shortages, letters to Stalin from people asking why collective farmers were eating dead horses, Petrovskyi’s demand to halt collections, Chubar’s warning of 100 districts needing aid, Kosior’s denial that “famine” should be discussed, Stalin’s April anger, the withdrawal of millet aid, and the 5.8 million tonne procurement quota. Source anchors: Kozyrivka; Tarasivka; dead horses; Petrovskyi; Chubar; 5.8 million tonnes.

Analysis: Kozyrivka and Tarasivka provide medical and village evidence that the state knew starvation had begun before the decisive winter. Dead horses show both the collapse of animal labour and the extremity of human hunger. Petrovskyi and Chubar matter because senior Ukrainian communists pleaded for relief, while 5.8 million tonnes shows Moscow choosing an impossible quota over the evidence gathered by its own officials.

8 Famine Decisions, 1932: Requisitions, Blacklists and Borders

Summary: Chapter 8 examines the decisions that intensified hunger in late 1932. Applebaum begins with Nadezhda Alliluyeva’s suicide, Voroshilov’s train-window reports, Budyonny’s exhausted horses, Ryutin’s Platform, Stalin’s fear of discontent, and the 7 August law treating kolkhoz property as sacred state property punishable by execution or ten years in camps. She then moves to the Ukrainian OGPU report of party members refusing impossible plans, Stalin’s letter warning Kaganovich that the USSR might “lose Ukraine,” Kaganovich and Molotov’s missions, blacklists, border controls, the North Caucasus, Kuban Ukrainians, and the push to transform Ukraine into a “fortress” of the Soviet Union. Source anchors: Nadezhda Alliluyeva; Ryutin's Platform; 7 August law; lose Ukraine; blacklists; border controls.

Analysis: Nadezhda Alliluyeva and Ryutin's Platform show Stalin’s household and party world registering the political cost of famine policy, and the chapter is a case for State Capacity because the famine was executed through the state's administrative reach rather than through mere neglect. The 7 August law criminalized hunger itself by making tiny food thefts into attacks on socialism, which turned courts and police into instruments of starvation. Lose Ukraine, blacklists, and border controls are the core of Applebaum’s causal account: Stalin interpreted procurement failure as Ukrainian political danger and responded by using the requisition, blacklist, and border machinery to punish villages and restrict movement, so the same channels that could have carried relief carried engineered deprivation instead.

9 Famine Decisions, 1932: The End of Ukrainization

Summary: Chapter 9 argues that the famine policy and the attack on Ukrainian culture were joined in December 1932. Applebaum describes the 14 and 15 December secret decrees blaming Ukrainization for grain failure, accusing party committees of infiltration by kulaks, former officers, Petliurites, Kuban Rada supporters, and “enemies with party cards.” She follows the purge of the Ukrainian Communist Party, Stalin’s return of Balytsky, Postyshev’s role as emissary, Orikhiv communists prosecuted for saying the plan was unrealistic, 14,230 arrests in November 1932, Balytsky’s Polish-Petliurite underground claims, Hrushevsky’s exile, Skrypnyk’s suicide, Khvylovyi’s suicide in Budynok Slovo, the firing of 4,000 teachers, the closure of churches, the destruction of Kyiv monuments, and the banning of Ukrainian authors and the letter g. Source anchors: December decrees; Ukrainization; Balytsky; Mykola Skrypnyk; Budynok Slovo; letter g.

Analysis: December decrees and Ukrainization give Applebaum the documentary link between agricultural punishment and national culture, and the chapter is a case for Language and Ideology because the famine was waged simultaneously on Ukrainian as a language. Balytsky and Mykola Skrypnyk show the two sides of that link: police enforcement and the collapse of national communism, so the purge treated Ukrainian schools, dictionaries, and newspapers as the political infrastructure that had to be broken alongside the villages. Budynok Slovo and letter g make the purge concrete at the level of writers’ apartments and alphabet reform, proving that the state attacked institutions, people, books, and language while villages starved, which is the ideological move of making a grain shortage into proof that a nation's culture itself was suspect.

10 Famine Decisions, 1932: The Searches and the Searchers

Summary: Chapter 10 moves into the winter searches that took food from starving families. Applebaum describes activist brigades using iron rods, hooks, lances, and spokes to probe ovens, beds, cradles, walls, chimneys, attics, doghouses, wells, orchards, and cemeteries. They took flour, beans, dried crusts, beet seedlings, poppy seedlings, corn cobs, buckwheat soup, cows, dogs, cats, pots, icons, coats, carpets, money, and parcels from relatives. The chapter names anonymous informers, Kaganovich’s bonuses, Lev Kopelev in Myrhorod, Viktor Kravchenko’s “Bolshevik iron,” Chubar’s concern that brigades might sympathize with locals, refusals by Mykola Musiichuk and Dmytro Slyniuk, hunger-motivated collaborators, and Andrii Richytskyi’s Arbuzynka trial after brutal cellar beatings and house destruction. Source anchors: iron rods; Lev Kopelev; Viktor Kravchenko; Mykola Musiichuk; Andrii Richytskyi; Arbuzynka.

Analysis: Iron rods identify the physical technology of starvation: the famine was enforced house by house. Lev Kopelev and Viktor Kravchenko show how educated communists trained themselves not to see starving peasants as victims. Mykola Musiichuk and Andrii Richytskyi mark the moral range inside the apparatus, from refusal and suicide to a man who could defend Arbuzynka violence because Kaganovich and Zatonskyi had validated it.

11 Starvation: Spring and Summer, 1933

Summary: Chapter 11 describes starvation as bodily collapse and social collapse. Applebaum explains glucose exhaustion, fat consumption, protein breakdown, scurvy, kwashiorkor, marasmus, oedema, pneumonia, typhus, night-blindness, swollen bellies, transparent skin, distended eyes, sores, children dying at school desks, corpses on roads to Donbas, people dying after eating wheat ears or raw beets, and survivors who remembered ghostlike faces or no emotions at all. The chapter then follows the psychology of hunger through mothers withholding bread from dying children, parents leaving children in holes, neighbours locking doors, thefts from fields and grain elevators, mob violence over milk or onions, hallucinations, cannibalism, the “red broom,” apathy, and people lying down to wait for death. Source anchors: oedema; night-blindness; Donbas roads; wheat ears; red broom; apathy.

Analysis: Oedema and night-blindness make starvation medically precise rather than metaphorical, while Donbas roads and wheat ears show death in motion, not only inside homes. Red broom condenses how survivors remembered the brigades and the famine’s violence. Apathy is the end point Applebaum needs the reader to grasp: once hunger reached that stage, rebellion became physically impossible, which made the state’s earlier fear of peasant revolt obsolete.

12 Survival: Spring and Summer, 1933

Summary: Chapter 12 studies how some people lived. Applebaum describes children begging relatives to take them to Kharkiv, families eating linden bark, buckwheat husks, gooseberries, wild geraniums, acacia blossoms, grass, pigweed, frogs, toads, cats, dogs, birds, hedgehogs, acorns, rotten potatoes, nettles, river reeds, mouse grain, belts, and shoes. She also shows survival through cows, fish, mushrooms, relatives in the Urals or Leningrad, Jewish neighbours, Donbas work, Kharkiv markets, ration cards, government canteens, school jobs, orphanages in Vinnytsia and Kharkiv, the Pavlohrad feeding effort, Liubov Drazhevska’s railway carriages of children, and Torgsin shops where wedding rings, baptismal crosses, earrings, medals, and gold coins could be exchanged for flour, oil, buckwheat, or bread. Source anchors: linden bark; family cow; Donbas work; orphanages; Liubov Drazhevska; Torgsin.

Analysis: Linden bark and family cow show survival at opposite ends of the food spectrum: wild substitutes and a precious remaining household asset. Donbas work and orphanages reveal that Soviet institutions could accidentally save people even while Soviet policy killed them. Liubov Drazhevska and Torgsin make the chapter morally difficult, because children survived in filthy state care and families lived by surrendering heirlooms to the same regime that had created their hunger.

13 Aftermath

Summary: Chapter 13 measures the famine’s demographic, geographic, and political aftermath. Applebaum moves from modern memorials in Kodaky, Hrebinky, and Barakhty to demographers’ estimates of 3.9 million excess deaths, 0.6 million lost births, 4.5 million missing Ukrainians, a 31 million pre-famine population, 13 percent direct losses, 3.5 million rural deaths, and life expectancy for males born in 1933 falling to five years. She compares Ukraine’s 14.9 percent rural excess deaths to Russia’s 3 percent, analyzes high mortality in Kyiv and Kharkiv provinces, lower rates in Chernihiv, Vinnytsia, Moldova, and Donetsk, the May 1933 relief, reduced quotas, Postyshev’s nationalist explanation, continued collectivization, Max Harmash’s dying village, Lidiia’s censored labour brigade, resettlement by 117,000 Russian peasants, Markivka’s empty houses, and later purges under Khrushchev. Source anchors: 3.9 million; 0.6 million; Kyiv province; Donetsk; Max Harmash; resettlement.

Analysis: 3.9 million and 0.6 million give the note a disciplined casualty basis rather than inflated memory figures. Kyiv province and Donetsk show that death rates followed political and administrative choices more than ordinary drought geography. Max Harmash and resettlement make aftermath visible at ground level: villages were emptied, labour had to be imported, and the state’s answer to Ukrainian demographic destruction helped deepen Russification.

14 The Cover-Up

Summary: Chapter 14 traces denial inside the USSR and abroad. Applebaum shows that Kosior wrote privately to Stalin about hunger while public speech denied famine, Red Army soldiers were told there was no famine, workers and students were ordered to keep silent, doctors were told to write infectious disease or cardiac arrest on death certificates, Odessa and Kharkiv officials withdrew death registers, and the 1937 census was suppressed after finding a Soviet population of 162 million instead of the expected 170 million. She then follows foreign knowledge through Lviv newspapers, ethnic German letters, Ukrainian diaspora protests in Winnipeg, Pope Pius XI, Cardinal Innitzer, Alexander Wienerberger photographs, Sergio Gradenigo, Gustav Hilger, British informants, Gareth Jones, Malcolm Muggeridge, Walter Duranty, The New York Times, the U.S. recognition debate, and Soviet propaganda against the “famine myth.” Source anchors: 1937 census; death registers; Alexander Wienerberger; Gareth Jones; Walter Duranty; famine myth.

Analysis: The 1937 census and death registers show that denial required bureaucratic violence after physical starvation had passed, and the chapter is a case for Knowledge Preservation because the cover-up was a sustained effort to control what later people could know. Alexander Wienerberger and Gareth Jones demonstrate that reliable evidence left the USSR despite censorship, so preservation here meant smuggling photographs and testimony past a state that destroyed its own records. Walter Duranty and famine myth explain how the Soviet story survived abroad: prestige journalism, diplomatic priorities, and later propaganda made the denial politically useful even when consuls, priests, refugees, and photographs described the deaths, which is the negative form of knowledge preservation, in which suppressing the record becomes as deliberate as keeping it.

15 The Holodomor in History and Memory

Summary: Chapter 15 follows the famine’s afterlife from private survivor memory to public commemoration and political dispute. Applebaum begins with the enforced Soviet silence after 1933: no churches, tombstones, public mourning, or permissible school stories, but private diaries, family testimony, Volodymyr Chepur’s remembered duty to bear witness, and Oleksandra Radchenko’s hidden writing kept an alternative history alive. The Nazi occupation briefly allowed public discussion, but mostly through anti-Soviet and anti-Semitic propaganda, even though Sosnovyi’s 1942 analysis in Kharkiv offered a sober early account of organized famine. After the war, the subject was again buried inside Soviet Ukraine while diaspora institutions, displaced-person camps, Woropay’s The Ninth Circle, Pidhainy’s Black Deeds of the Kremlin, the Harvard Ukrainian Research Institute, James Mace, Harvest of Despair, Robert Conquest’s Harvest of Sorrow, the U.S. congressional commission, Chernobyl, Rukh, Ivan Drach, and the 1993 commemorations gradually made the Holodomor publicly knowable. Source anchors: private diaries; Volodymyr Chepur; Nazi occupation; Sosnovyi; Black Deeds of the Kremlin; Harvest of Despair; Harvest of Sorrow; U.S. congressional commission; Chernobyl; 1993 commemorations.

Analysis: Private diaries and Volodymyr Chepur show Knowledge Preservation surviving below the level of public institutions, which matters because Soviet silence destroyed the normal channels of mourning. Nazi occupation and Sosnovyi make the chapter morally difficult: some truth about 1933 became printable under a murderous occupation that immediately folded it into its own propaganda, while a few careful accounts still preserved usable evidence. Black Deeds of the Kremlin, Harvest of Despair, Harvest of Sorrow, and the U.S. congressional commission show diaspora memory becoming research infrastructure, even when Cold War politics made scholars distrust it. Chernobyl and the 1993 commemorations then return the memory struggle to Ukraine itself, where the famine became part of national public life only when Soviet information control began to collapse.

Epilogue: The Ukrainian Question Reconsidered

Summary: The epilogue returns to Lemkin and to post-Soviet memory. Applebaum argues that survivors remembered the famine as state aggression and that archives support the view that starvation resulted from food removal, roadblocks, blacklists, trade restrictions, and propaganda rather than weather. She traces Raphael Lemkin’s Lviv education, Axis Rule in Occupied Europe, the 1948 Genocide Convention, Soviet narrowing of genocide law, Yushchenko’s National Memory Institute and Book of Memory, the 2010 Ukrainian court case against Stalin, Molotov, Kaganovich, Postyshev, and Kosior, Yanukovych’s refusal to use “genocide,” Medvedev’s “so-called Holodomor,” Kondrashin’s Russian village argument, Snizhne’s destroyed famine monument, Sputnik’s “Holodomor Hoax,” Crimea, Donbas, the FSB, Russian disinformation, and the 1991 Ukrainian independence vote. Source anchors: Raphael Lemkin; Genocide Convention; Yushchenko; Yanukovych; Snizhne; 1991 independence.

Analysis: Raphael Lemkin and Genocide Convention let Applebaum separate historical judgment from narrow legal definition, especially because the USSR helped shape that legal definition. Yushchenko and Yanukovych show how Holodomor memory became a marker inside Ukrainian politics after independence. Snizhne and 1991 independence connect the famine to present sovereignty: Russian denial and military pressure appear as continuations of an older refusal to accept Ukraine as a nation with its own history.
