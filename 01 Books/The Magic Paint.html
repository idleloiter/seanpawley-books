Detailed overview

The Magic Paint collects eight short pieces by Primo Levi, selected from A Tranquil Star and translated by Ann Goldstein, Alessandra Bastagli, and Jenny McPhee. The stories move between wartime memory, industrial chemistry, bureaucratic fantasy, speculative weapons, ritualized sport, failed art, clerical metaphysics, and animal estrangement. Levi's biographical note places him in Turin from 31 July 1919 to 11 April 1987, while the publication note dates the stories from 1949 through 1981, so the selection spans much of his postwar imaginative range rather than representing one moment.

The title story starts from Levi's professional intimacy with paints, formulations, acrylic enamel, infrared spectra, gas chromatographs, NMR, tantalum, and laboratory copying of competitors' samples. That technical language becomes comic and then deadly when a Naples agent, Signor Amato Di Prima, sends a paint said to protect against misfortune, Chiovatero tests it on E. coli, and Michele Fassio's alleged evil eye is treated as a measurable radiation problem. The story's cruelty lies in its scientific patience: a folk belief is not dismissed, but tested until its mechanism kills the person it was supposed to free.

Several stories build speculative institutions that are absurd because they run with cold administrative consistency. "Censorship in Bitinia" imagines censorship as occupational medicine, computer sorting, gallows errors, and finally hen-based decision-making. "Bureau of Vital Statistics" turns death into an office workflow where Arrigo assigns causes from index cards until an eight-year-old Norwegian girl named Karen Kvarna cracks his routine. These stories make bureaucracy monstrous not by making officials flamboyantly evil, but by making them tired, rule-bound, and irritated by poorly formatted paperwork.

Levi also returns to violence without making it clean. "The Death of Marinese" follows a captured partisan in a German truck, feverish and half-asleep, until a song, a grenade handle, and the last ten seconds of his life force decision out of exhaustion. "Knall" imagines a cheap silent killing device with cigar-like cylinders, comic wrappers, expiration dates, initiation rites, and a one-meter range, then asks why a bloodless weapon may fail to satisfy cultures that mythologize blood. "Gladiators" transfers public blood sport into a modern stadium where condemned or marginal men fight cars, spectators bet, and Nicola and Stefania discover their own appetite and revulsion too late.

The remaining stories concentrate on failure of communication across fragile boundaries. "The Fugitive" makes a perfect poem literally escape Pasquale's custody, moving from drawer to tray to wall to wallet to shoe to fragments, leaving only increasingly weak reconstructions. "Buffet Dinner" places Innaminka, a male kangaroo with a wife, a joey, a tail, and a white triangle of fur, inside an elegant human party whose stairs, coats, buffet table, canapes, ficus pot, and romantic misery are not designed for his body or mind. Across the collection, Levi keeps taking a premise that could become a joke and following its physical details until it exposes fear, shame, loneliness, appetite, or institutional violence.

Section-by-section notes

The Magic Paint

Summary: The narrator works in paint formulation and receives a Naples sample from Signor Amato Di Prima, who claims that an important client has found a paint that protects against misfortune and can replace horn amulets, hunchbacks, four-leaf clovers, and charms. The paint looks like ordinary clear acrylic enamel, but Di Prima reports a fishing boat's spectacular catches and a typographer's disappearance of errors; Chiovatero tests it on E. coli cultures, finds threefold growth compared with controls, and the laboratory traces the key ingredient to tantalum. After the team makes a tantalum soap and confirms the effect, Di Prima paints himself from head to foot and survives a Friday under a ladder with thirteen black cats. The narrator then tries to help Michele Fassio, whose right eye corrodes steel, breaks polyethylene thread, and emits a blue-range effect around 425 Nm, but painted eyeglasses reflect the destructive force into Fassio's brain and kill him. Source anchors: Signor Amato Di Prima; clear acrylic enamel; E. coli; tantalum; thirteen black cats; Michele Fassio; 425 Nm; painted eyeglasses

Analysis: Signor Amato Di Prima and clear acrylic enamel bring commercial imitation and laboratory skepticism into contact with Neapolitan luck culture. E. coli, tantalum, Michele Fassio, and 425 Nm make Legibility dangerous rather than merely clarifying: the evil eye becomes a measurable phenomenon precisely when measurement lets it harm more efficiently. The thirteen black cats episode keeps the story comic until the painted eyeglasses reverse the logic of protection, showing that a technical solution can become lethal when it treats a human curse as just another coating problem.

The Death of Marinese

Summary: "The Death of Marinese" begins after a partisan disaster in which no one is killed immediately, but Sante and Marinese are captured by Germans and carried downhill in a truck over icy roads under a gray sky. Sante is wounded on the rear bench, while Marinese stands near the cab, feverish, drowsy, and almost relieved to be free of decisions despite knowing that interrogation, beating, and death await him. When the German soldiers begin singing and stamping with hobnailed boots, Marinese notices the handle of a stick grenade in a soldier's belt, unscrews the safety cap, grips the cord, and imagines the last ten seconds before explosion. Fear rises from his body, he pulls the cord, curls around the grenade like a hedgehog while the soldiers beat him, and the blast tears apart Marinese and four Germans; Sante is executed on the spot, and the narrator's group captures the abandoned truck the next night. Source anchors: Sante; Marinese; Germans; icy road; stick grenade; ten seconds; four Germans; abandoned truck

Analysis: Sante, Marinese, the Germans, and the icy road make the story's violence specific to partisan capture rather than generalized heroism. The stick grenade and ten seconds focus the narrative on bodily decision: Marinese's courage is not a slogan but a fight against fever, fear, and the animal refusal to die. The four Germans and abandoned truck give the ending its bitter arithmetic, because Marinese's act kills enemies and yields a captured vehicle while also showing that his final attempt to free himself of the grenade comes too late.

Censorship in Bitinia

Summary: In Bitinia, a drab patronage culture develops an expanded censorship apparatus that doubles central staff, opens provincial branches, and creates dangerous work for censors. The job requires subtle training and causes a professional disease called paroxysmal dysthymia or Gowelius's disease, marked by diplopia, olfactory and auditory disorders, exaggerated reactions to colors or flavors, remissions, relapses, and psychological perversions. Electronic censorship then uses three lists of words, hints, plots, topics, and frames of reference: one list deletes passages, one eliminates works, and one causes the arrest and hanging of author and publisher. The machine approves Claire Efrem's Diary of a Sparrow, hangs Colonel Tuttle because "brigadier" becomes "brassiere," nearly kills an animal-husbandry author, and finally gives way to trained hens, which are cheap, decisive, calm, forgetful, and officially suitable for censorship. Source anchors: Bitinia; paroxysmal dysthymia; Gowelius's disease; Claire Efrem; Diary of a Sparrow; Colonel Tuttle; brassiere; trained hens

Analysis: Bitinia, paroxysmal dysthymia, and Gowelius's disease turn censorship from political repression into workplace pathology, making the censor's damage literal as well as civic. Claire Efrem, Diary of a Sparrow, Colonel Tuttle, and "brassiere" show Language and Ideology automated into lethal stupidity: the system misses allusion and murders over a typo. The trained hens are Levi's final administrative joke, because their cheapness, calmness, and weak memory make them ideal only for an institution that wants decisions without conscience.

Knall

Summary: "Knall" describes the sudden spread of a small silent killing device after earlier crazes for yo-yos, Chinese mushrooms, Pop art, Zen Buddhism, and hula-hoops. A four-inch knall costs about 3,000 lire, is shaped like a Tuscan cigar, comes loose or in boxes of twenty, and is wrapped in vulgar comic scenes of bare-breasted girls, Max and Moritz types, farmers, and falling victims. It kills instantly at close range, leaves only a ring-shaped bruise, can be fired once, expires like antibiotics or film, and can also shatter stone, pierce wood, melt metals, create steaming water whirlpools, or light cigarettes. Because it is nonmetallic and invisible to magnetic instruments and X rays, police rely on informers and anonymous calls, while secret clubs teach the rhythmic twisting sequence; a basement in F. is found with fifteen twelve-year-old boys and a twenty-three-year-old dead, all holding discharged knalls. Source anchors: yo-yo; four-inch knall; 3,000 lire; Tuscan cigar; ring-shaped bruise; X rays; basement in F.; bloodless

Analysis: The yo-yo and four-inch knall place mass violence in the same social channel as fads, which makes the weapon's spread feel banal rather than conspiratorial. The Tuscan cigar shape, ring-shaped bruise, X rays, and basement in F. give the device enough physical specificity to make the society's adaptation believable: people change theater seating, tram habits, and sidewalk movement around a one-meter danger radius. The bloodless quality is the story's final mechanism, because Levi suggests that the knall may not dominate society precisely because human violence is attached to blood, fire, wine, and ritual display.

Gladiators

Summary: Nicola goes to the stadium because Stefania pressures him after weeks of excuses, though he has no desire to watch the gladiator contest that his colleagues and hers discuss every Friday on betting forms. He buys grandstand tickets on Via San Secondo, sees advertisements for six entrants, hears about Turi Lorusso, and sits beside an old spectator who remembers Pinerolo, Alpignano, beaked cars, reformatories, New Prisons, insurance, disability, holidays, and pensions. The bouts pit men with hammers against vehicles: a flame-red Fiat 127, an Alfasud that injures Blitz or Craveri, a Renault 4 defeated by the Rudolf move, two forklifts in comic combat, a banged-up Peugeot denounced as rigged, and a fifth fight where a gladiator smashes a windshield and the driver tries to strangle him. When Lorusso finally appears, Stefania is pale and nauseated, Nicola feels rancor but chooses to leave, and both end the day with appetite and pleasure spoiled. Source anchors: Nicola; Stefania; Via San Secondo; Turi Lorusso; Fiat 127; Blitz; Renault 4; Rudolf

Analysis: Nicola and Stefania make the story about social complicity as much as sport, because neither is forced to attend and both use conformity, curiosity, and desire against their own scruples. Turi Lorusso, Fiat 127, Blitz, Renault 4, and the Rudolf move turn the arena into a modern consumer catalogue where cars, betting, pensions, and celebrity replace ancient armor. Via San Secondo and the final nausea make Moral Economy uncomfortable: revulsion does not erase Nicola's and Stefania's earlier willingness to buy tickets and help fund the violence they later condemn.

The Fugitive

Summary: Pasquale, who has a dull office job and is not a professional poet, recognizes the rare arrival of a real poem by a whistle in his ears and a shiver like an epileptic aura. After two silent years, while examining an insurance policy, he receives a poem called "Annunciation," writes it in tall smooth handwriting on copy paper, locks it in his drawer, and rewards himself with an ice cream. The next morning the poem appears in the in-box tray, then disappears again after the boss keeps him for ninety minutes and the photocopier breaks, and later Pasquale finds it stuck to the wall, under the desk pad, in his wallet, and eventually tries to secure it at home. The text escapes anyway, leaving only twenty postage-stamp fragments on a piece of wood and a few illegible shreds; Pasquale spends Sunday reconstructing it and spends the rest of his life producing thinner, bloodless versions. Source anchors: Pasquale; whistle; shiver; insurance policy; Annunciation; photocopier; twenty fragments; bloodless versions

Analysis: Pasquale, the whistle, and the shiver make poetic inspiration bodily and involuntary rather than a matter of discipline. The insurance policy, Annunciation, and photocopier place that inspiration inside office routines whose delays are comic until they become catastrophic. The twenty fragments and bloodless versions make Knowledge Preservation tragic rather than archival: once the original moment has gone, Pasquale can preserve scraps and make imitations, but he cannot recreate the living pressure that first produced the poem.

Bureau of Vital Statistics

Summary: Arrigo works on the ninth floor of a bureaucratic building with four elevators, one always out of service, time clocks on every floor, dusty cleared desks, and packets of index cards sent from the floor above. Each card gives a person's name and date of death, and Arrigo's job is to assign the cause, though the boss demands detailed correlations rather than generic terms like "accident." He gives Yen Ch'ing-Hsu of Han Tou a fall from a scaffold, Pedro Gonzales de Eslava hepatitis, a motorbike racer a fatal crash, and Pierre-Jean La Motte of Lyons and the University of Rio a brain hemorrhage after being irritated by Lorusso, bad coffee, late pay, and deductions. When a damaged Adelia card buys one woman time and the next card assigns Karen Kvarna, an eight-year-old from Slidre in Norway, to die the following day, Arrigo refuses, denounces the randomizer and the office, and is demoted from grade 7 to grade 6 to determine newborns' nose shapes in the attic. Source anchors: Arrigo; index cards; Yen Ch'ing-Hsu; Pedro Gonzales de Eslava; Pierre-Jean La Motte; Karen Kvarna; randomizer; newborns' noses

Analysis: Arrigo and the index cards turn fate into paperwork, where death is not predicted but completed by an employee's bored imagination. Yen Ch'ing-Hsu, Pedro Gonzales de Eslava, and Pierre-Jean La Motte reveal private prejudice entering official causes under the cover of administrative detail. Karen Kvarna and the randomizer make State Capacity grotesque: the office can assign, classify, and execute destiny, and Arrigo's punishment merely moves him from arbitrary death to arbitrary creation in newborns' noses.

Buffet Dinner

Summary: Innaminka, a male kangaroo, attends an elegant buffet dinner and immediately struggles with human architecture and etiquette. A butler with a green sash offers to take coats, but Innaminka's coat is part of his body; a polished black spiral staircase defeats his long hindquarters, feet, and tail until he climbs sideways for ten minutes. Upstairs he sees paintings, bronze and marble figures, men in evening attire, women in long black dresses with blue or green eyelids, a young hostess with gray eyes, no grass or hay, finished salad, peas in brown sauce, wine, orangeade, canapes, waiters, a ficus pot, and a woman who caresses his neck, chest, white triangle, tail, and thighs while speaking about a man who has hurt her. After another man drags the woman away, Innaminka waits for an elderly couple to leave, leaps over the cleared table, crashes down the stairs, passes the doorman, and escapes along Via Borgospesso in long elastic jumps. Source anchors: Innaminka; green sash; spiral staircase; white triangle; ficus tree; gray eyes; elderly couple; Via Borgospesso

Analysis: Innaminka, the green sash, and the spiral staircase make social exclusion physical: the party is not hostile at first, but every object assumes a human body. The white triangle, ficus tree, and gray-eyed hostess show how quickly curiosity and kindness can still leave him hungry, thirsty, embarrassed, and misunderstood. The elderly couple and Via Borgospesso give the ending its release, because Innaminka's escape is not a social victory but a return to movement, air, and bodily competence after a room that converted him into an exhibit and a substitute listener.
