The Library of Scott Alexandria

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer.

Detailed overview

The Library of Scott Alexandria is a large anthology of Scott Alexander essays assembled by Rob Bensinger and Nino Annighöfer. Its structure is closer to a curated intellectual archive than to a single-argument monograph: thirteen roman-numeral sections move from rationality, probability, science, medicine, game theory, promises, cognition, ethics, liberty, progress, social justice, politicization, and coordination. The collection repeatedly uses jokes, invented parables, medical anecdotes, probability puzzles, pseudo-dialogues, and literature reviews to make abstract failures of reasoning feel mechanically visible.

The first major movement is about how people protect beliefs from evidence. "Blue- and Yellow-Tinted Choices" uses Dan Ariely's Economist subscription experiments and the Lotto Lab cube illusion to show reference-class effects; "The Apologist and the Revolutionary" uses anosognosia, Ramachandran, split-brain confabulation, and cold water in the left ear to dramatize rationalization; "Beware the Man of One Study," "Debunked and Well-Refuted," and "How to Not Lose an Argument" attack the way political camps turn studies into uniforms. Alexander's method is to make the reader watch an error happen in a toy case, then recognize the same machinery inside partisan or personal reasoning.

The probabilism and science sections extend that method into statistical literacy. Medical-test base rates, "Magic Mystery Box" examples, Bayesian reference classes, schizophrenia correlations with geomagnetic storms, Google Correlate jokes, John Ioannidis, p-hacking papers, Alcoholics Anonymous evidence, the scientific-evidence pyramid, and the "Cowpox of Doubt" essays all treat evidence as something fragile, conditional, and socially mediated. The anthology keeps warning that being suspicious of bad evidence is itself dangerous if it curdles into helplessness, conspiracy thinking, or refusal to update on anything.

The medicine and human-enhancement sections are especially grounded in Alexander's psychiatric background. "Scientific Freud," "In Defense of Psych Treatment for Attempted Suicide," "Who By Very Slow Decay," "Medicine, As Not Seen on TV," "Searching for One-Sided Tradeoffs," "Wirehead Gods on Lotus Thrones," and "Transhumanist Fables" move between clinical experience, suicide prevention, cancer rhetoric, melatonin and ramelteon, chronic pain, polyamory, reinforcement, dopamine, and speculative enhancement. These essays are not just medical miscellany; they repeatedly ask what counts as a treatment, what counts as evidence of harm, and what moral stories people smuggle into diagnoses.

The middle of the anthology turns practical reasoning into game theory and commitment theory. "Backward Reasoning Over Decision Trees," "Nash Equilibria and Schelling Points," "Introduction to Prisoners' Dilemma," "Game Theory as a Dark Art," "Schelling Fences on Slippery Slopes," "Democracy is the Worst Form of Government Except for All the Others Except Possibly Futarchy," "Eight Short Studies on Excuses," and "Guilt: Another Gift Nobody Wants" show Alexander's characteristic move from small games to institutions. The point is rarely that formal models solve everything; instead, formal models expose why good outcomes can be unavailable to locally rational actors unless rules, commitments, markets, or norms change the payoff surface.

The later political sections become more abrasive because Alexander applies the same machinery to libertarianism, reaction, progress, social justice, and tribal conflict. The Non-Libertarian FAQ's economic, social, political, moral, and practical parts argue with rights-based libertarianism; the Anti-Reactionary FAQ answers Moldbug, Michael Anissimov, crime statistics, colonialism, aristocracy, and demographic claims; the social-justice and politicization sections introduce creepiness, superweapons, bravery debates, false rape-accusation statistics, niceness norms, universalizability, outgroups, Ferguson, fnords, motte-and-bailey, race and justice, and triggers. The anthology's political logic is not neutral detachment: it tries to keep empirical questions, moral questions, and policy questions from being deliberately fused into applause lines.

The final section, Competition and Cooperation, gives the anthology its bleakest and most famous endpoint. "Galactic Core," "The Two-Income Trap," "Just for Stealing a Mouthful of Bread," "Meditations on Moloch," "Misperceptions on Moloch," "Freedom on the Centralized Web," and the reviews of Singer on Marx and Red Plenty collect the earlier pieces' concerns about incentives into a theory of coordination failure. Prisoner's Dilemmas, dollar auctions, arms races, capitalism, education, science, government corruption, Congress, Malthusian traps, and platform centralization become versions of the same problem: systems can reward behavior that almost nobody endorses from outside the system.

Section-by-section notes

I. Rationality and Rationalization

Summary: The opening section introduces Alexander's concern with the mind's ability to make bad reasoning feel natural. "Blue- and Yellow-Tinted Choices" compares the Lotto Lab cube illusion, Dan Ariely's Economist subscription plans, the $125 combo option, Photoshop-altered dating choices, and anchoring effects to show that people judge value relative to nearby alternatives. "The Apologist and the Revolutionary" moves to anosognosia, Lishman's Organic Psychiatry, Ramachandran, cold water in the left ear, split-brain chicken-claw and snow-house experiments, and confabulation. "Historical realism," "Simultaneously Right and Wrong," "You May Already Be A Sinner," "Beware the Man of One Study," "Debunked and Well-Refuted," "The Least Convenient Possible World," and "Typical Mind and Politics" then turn the same problem toward history, religion, politics, and moral debate. Source anchors: Lotto Lab cube; Dan Ariely; Economist subscription; anosognosia; Ramachandran; cold water; split-brain; Typical Mind

Analysis: The Lotto Lab cube, Dan Ariely, and the Economist subscription experiment show Alexander beginning with perception because visual relativity makes cognitive relativity harder to deny. Anosognosia, Ramachandran, cold water, and split-brain confabulation make rationalization look neurological rather than merely dishonest, which lets the later essays treat political or religious excuses as ordinary cognition under pressure. Typical Mind turns Legibility inward by warning that even sincere introspection can misread other people, so the section gives the anthology its first rule: do not trust the feeling that your current explanation is the only explanation a reasonable person could have.

II. Probabilism

Summary: The second section teaches probability as an antidote to verbal certainty and fake debate victory. "Confidence Levels Inside and Outside an Argument" separates feeling persuasive inside an argument from assigning a calibrated probability outside it, while "Schizophrenia and Geomagnetic Storms" and "Talking Snakes" test how strange claims should move belief. "Arguments from My Opponent Believes Something" catalogs pseudo-refutations that treat an opponent's belief as religion, fanaticism, ideology, hatred, violence, stigma, or obligation. "Statistical Literacy Among Doctors Now Lower Than Chance" uses medical testing and base-rate errors, and "Techniques for Probability Estimates" recommends reference classes, frequency conversion, multiple statements, hypothetical evidence, bets, and calibration before the section reaches "Utilitarianism for Engineers" and made-up statistics. Source anchors: Confidence Levels; geomagnetic storms; Talking Snakes; Opponent Believes Something; medical testing; reference class; frequency; Utilitarianism for Engineers

Analysis: Confidence Levels, medical testing, reference class, and frequency conversion do the practical work in this section: they turn disagreement from a contest of clever phrases into a numerical habit. Geomagnetic storms and Talking Snakes are deliberately odd examples because Alexander wants the reader to practice with claims that feel embarrassing to quantify. Opponent Believes Something matters because it names a failure mode that later political essays revisit: turning probabilistic disagreement into a claim that the other side is quasi-religious or morally contaminated.

III. Science and Doubt

Summary: The science section studies how evidence can be real, noisy, gamed, and socially misreported all at once. "Google Correlate Does Not Imply Google Causation" and "Prisons are Built with Bricks of Law and Brothels with Bricks of Religion" attack causal overreach, while "Effects of Vertical Acceleration on Wrongness" works through Ioannidis-style claims about medical research without accepting the lazy conclusion that all medicine is nonsense. "Noisy Poll Results and the Reptilian Muslim Climatologists from Mars" uses phantom lizardmen, poll attire, and malicious answering; "Two Dark Side Statistics Papers" explains p-hacking through multiple dependent variables, optional stopping, confounder subgrouping, and multiple condition comparisons. "Alcoholics Anonymous," "The Control Group Is Out Of Control," "The Cowpox of Doubt," "The Skeptic's Trilemma," and "If You Can't Make Predictions, You're Still in a Crisis" keep doubt tied to prediction and replication. Source anchors: Google Correlate; Ioannidis; phantom lizardmen; p-hacking; Alcoholics Anonymous; control group; Cowpox of Doubt; predictions

Analysis: Google Correlate, Ioannidis, p-hacking, and the control group essays give Alexander a way to criticize scientific claims without abandoning science as a filter. Phantom lizardmen and poll attire show that data can contain performance, trolling, and identity signaling, not just ignorance. Alcoholics Anonymous, Cowpox of Doubt, and predictions turn Knowledge Preservation into a live scientific problem: the remedy for bad studies is stronger design, replication, prediction, and humility about effect sizes, not the conclusion that every basement theory deserves equal standing.

IV. Medicine, Therapy, and Human Enhancement

Summary: This section brings Alexander's medical and psychiatric interests to the foreground. "Scientific Freud" asks what psychoanalysis would look like if its claims were treated experimentally, "In Defense of Psych Treatment for Attempted Suicide" argues through mental disorder, regret, psychiatric care, and suicide outcomes, and "Who By Very Slow Decay" challenges uplifting cancer rhetoric by comparing it with Wilde, Wilfred Owen, and the bodily reality of terminal illness. "Medicine, As Not Seen on TV" contrasts television medicine with actual clinical uncertainty, "Searching for One-Sided Tradeoffs" asks why life hacks do not reach fixation, "Polyamory is Boring" deflates culture-war excitement, "Wirehead Gods on Lotus Thrones" examines chronic pain, dopamine, motivation, and pleasure circuitry, and "Don't Fear the Filter" and "Transhumanist Fables" move into enhancement and existential selection. Source anchors: Scientific Freud; attempted suicide; Wilfred Owen; television medicine; life hacks; Polyamory is Boring; Wirehead Gods; Transhumanist Fables

Analysis: Scientific Freud, attempted suicide, and television medicine make this section a test of whether clinical claims can survive contact with outcome data and messy patients. Wilfred Owen is not a decorative literary reference; Alexander uses the war-poem analogy to puncture Moral Economy scripts that make suffering serve a motivational or redemptive purpose. Life hacks, Wirehead Gods, and Transhumanist Fables widen medicine into enhancement, asking why interventions that seem obviously beneficial may fail because of equilibrium, motivation, side effects, selection filters, or the difficulty of knowing what a changed mind would endorse.

V. Introduction to Game Theory

Summary: The game-theory section starts with formal puzzles and then turns them into social diagnosis. "Backward Reasoning Over Decision Trees" teaches induction through sequential choices, "Nash Equilibria and Schelling Points" distinguishes stable outcomes from focal points, and "Introduction to Prisoners' Dilemma" shows why mutual defection can beat mutual cooperation from inside the decision situation. "Real-World Solutions to Prisoners' Dilemmas" introduces repeated interaction, reputation, side payments, enforcement, and behavioral economics, while "Interlude for Behavioral Economics" brings in Ultimatum Game, Prisoners' Dilemma, reality television, and "Friend or Foe." "Bargaining and Auctions," "Imperfect Voting Systems," and "Game Theory as a Dark Art" then use hostile takeovers, dollar auctions, bloodthirsty pirates, and voting rules to show strategic reasoning becoming predatory. Source anchors: decision trees; Nash Equilibria; Schelling Points; Prisoners' Dilemma; Ultimatum Game; Friend or Foe; dollar auction; bloodthirsty pirates

Analysis: Decision trees, Nash Equilibria, Schelling Points, and Prisoners' Dilemma give Alexander a compact vocabulary for traps that reappear in the final Moloch essays. The Ultimatum Game and Friend or Foe examples matter because they show humans using anger, fairness, and reputation to escape results that simple selfish calculation predicts. Dollar auction and bloodthirsty pirates make Information and Coordination adversarial: once people understand the strategic surface, they can design institutions or offers that make others' locally rational moves produce losses.

VI. Promises and Principles

Summary: The sixth section studies commitments, excuses, and the cost of keeping principles stable over time. "Beware Trivial Inconveniences" shows small frictions defeating intended action, while "Time and Effort Discounting" and "Applied Picoeconomics" connect akrasia, hyperbolic discounting, and internal bargaining. "Schelling Fences on Slippery Slopes" uses Murder-Gandhi, Slippery Hyperbolic Discounting, coalitions of resistance, and Martin Niemoller to explain why refusing a first step can preserve a boundary that later selves or coalitions will need. "Democracy is the Worst Form of Government Except for All the Others Except Possibly Futarchy" introduces prediction markets and the Mandate of Earth, "Eight Short Studies on Excuses" runs through game-players, students, sports fans, dictators, and disguised atheists, and "Revenge as Charitable Act" and "Guilt: Another Gift Nobody Wants" analyze punishment, fakeable signals, and undeserved guilt. Source anchors: trivial inconveniences; hyperbolic discounting; Murder-Gandhi; Schelling Fences; Martin Niemoller; prediction markets; excuses; undeserved guilt

Analysis: Trivial inconveniences and hyperbolic discounting make weakness of will mechanical rather than melodramatic: small costs and near-term effort can defeat stable preferences. Murder-Gandhi, Schelling Fences, and Martin Niemoller show why Alexander values rigid commitments in some cases, since later negotiation can occur after the coalition or self has already been weakened. Prediction markets, excuses, and undeserved guilt extend the section from individual willpower to institutional design, asking what systems can make promises, information, and moral emotions less exploitable.

VII. Cognition and Association

Summary: The cognition section examines how words, categories, reinforcement, and introspection smuggle judgments into thought. "Diseased Thinking" asks what people mean by disease through biology, involuntariness, rarity, unpleasantness, discreteness, and treatment, then "The Power of Positivist Thinking" and "When Truth Isn't Enough" press statements like "all men are created equal," "the lottery is a waste of hope," and "God exists" through hidden inferences. "Ambijectivity" and "The Blue-Minimizing Robot" look at classifications and goal systems, while "Basics of Animal Reinforcement" distinguishes context, intermittent reinforcement, small-scale discount rates, and behavior. "Physical and Mental Behavior," "Trivers on Self-Deception," "Ego-Syntonic Thoughts and Values," "Approving Reinforces Low-Effort Behaviors," "The Limits of Introspection," "Secrets of the Eliminati," "Tendencies in Reflective Equilibrium," and "Hansonian Optimism" connect self-deception, ego-syntonia, animal learning, and social modeling. Source anchors: Diseased Thinking; hidden inferences; Ambijectivity; Blue-Minimizing Robot; intermittent reinforcement; Trivers; ego-syntonic; introspection

Analysis: Diseased Thinking and hidden inferences show Alexander dissolving category debates by asking what predictions and decisions a word licenses. Ambijectivity and the Blue-Minimizing Robot make the same point with artificial examples: a label or objective function can appear clean while carrying concealed choices. Intermittent reinforcement, Trivers, ego-syntonic desires, and introspection move the analysis inside the mind, where approval, self-deception, and low-effort behavior can shape what feels like a value rather than merely what someone says they believe.

VIII. Doing Good

Summary: The eighth section shifts from reasoning well to choosing what helps. "Newtonian Ethics" uses force-like intuitions about moral action, "The Economics of Art and the Art of Economics" examines value and patronage, "A Modest Proposal" and "The Life Issue" quantify harms in uncomfortable ways, and "Nefarious Nefazodone and Flashy Rare Side-Effects" asks how vivid rare dangers distort decisions. "The Consequentialism FAQ" is the section's long centerpiece: it begins with a failed world containing hunger, war, racism, environmental damage, Haiti earthquake generosity, and anti-gay-marriage moralism, then moves through Whirlwind Metaethics, Clamzoria, the Hrogmorph, assigning value to other people, consequentialism, utilitarianism, rules, human rights, and objections. The remaining essays include "Doing Your Good Deed for the Day," "I Myself Am A Scientismist," "After Virtue," backward history of philosophy, virtue ethics, and Proving Too Much. Source anchors: Newtonian Ethics; Life Issue; Nefazodone; Consequentialism FAQ; Clamzoria; Hrogmorph; utilitarianism; After Virtue

Analysis: Newtonian Ethics, the Life Issue, and Nefazodone show Alexander forcing moral feeling into contact with quantities, side effects, and opportunity costs. The Consequentialism FAQ, Clamzoria, Hrogmorph, and utilitarianism supply the explicit ethical machinery: morality must refer to real-world changes and to other people's lives, not just to purity, identity, or metaphysical applause. After Virtue and the virtue-ethics essays matter because Alexander does not leave consequentialism unopposed; he argues that rival traditions often describe admirable character better than they guide hard choices.

IX. Liberty

Summary: The liberty section is anchored by the Non-Libertarian FAQ and then branches into policy essays. Its introduction separates economic, social, political, moral, and practical claims, then Part A answers arguments about government failure, private efficiency, taxation, regulation, and markets; Part B addresses social issues; Part C examines political issues; Part D challenges rights-based moral claims about force and natural rights; and Part E treats implementation. The surrounding essays include "A Blessing in Disguise," "Basic Income Guarantees," "The Nurture Assumption," "The Death of Wages is Sin," smoking and nicotine ambiguity, "The Life Cycle of Medical Ideas," "Vote on Values, Outsource Beliefs," a left-libertarian-ish manifesto, plutocracy, tulip subsidies, and a graduation speech. Source anchors: Non-Libertarian FAQ; Economic Issues; Political Issues; Moral Issues; Basic Income Guarantees; Nurture Assumption; Vote on Values; Plutocracy

Analysis: The Non-Libertarian FAQ, Economic Issues, Political Issues, and Moral Issues let Alexander argue with libertarianism at several levels instead of refuting only one slogan. Basic Income Guarantees and Vote on Values show him separating values he may share with libertarians from empirical beliefs about what policies accomplish. Plutocracy and the left-libertarian-ish manifesto complicate the section further: the target is not markets as such, but the tendency to mistake a clean rights formula for a complete account of public goods, coercion, poverty, and institutional competence.

X. Progress

Summary: The progress section uses reactionary and anti-progressive writing as a stress test for liberal confidence. "Intellectual Hipsters and Meta-Contrarianism" and the class-politics signaling essay begin with status games, then "Reactionary Philosophy in an Enormous, Planet-Sized Nutshell" and "The Anti-Reactionary FAQ" address Moldbug, Michael Anissimov, monarchy, aristocracy, democracy, crime statistics, Austria, colonialism, Progressivism, schools, demotism, and demographic collapse. Other essays such as "We Wrestle Not With Flesh And Blood," "Engines Alone Turn The Wheels of History," "Amid These Dark Satanic Mills," "The Wisdom of the Ancients," "Public Awareness Campaigns," "Social Psychology is a Flamethrower," "The Poor You Will Always Have With You," and "Society is Fixed, Biology is Mutable" test whether improvement comes from institutions, biology, propaganda, technology, or accidental coordination. Source anchors: Meta-Contrarianism; Reactionary Philosophy; Moldbug; Anti-Reactionary FAQ; Progressivism; Public Awareness Campaigns; Social Psychology; biology

Analysis: Meta-Contrarianism and Reactionary Philosophy show Alexander taking dissident-right arguments seriously enough to reconstruct their appeal before attacking them. Moldbug, the Anti-Reactionary FAQ, Progressivism, and crime statistics give the section its argumentative spine: claims about monarchy, colonialism, and democracy must survive historical comparison rather than aesthetic disgust with modernity. Public Awareness Campaigns, Social Psychology, and biology widen the question of progress by asking why reform campaigns backfire, why interventions can have perverse effects, and when social problems may be more biologically mutable than institutional rhetoric assumes.

XI. Social Justice

Summary: The social-justice section moves through poverty, gender, prejudice, speech, and accusation with Alexander's usual suspicion of applause lines. "Practically-a-Book Review: Dying to be Free" examines prostitution and rescue narratives, while "Drug Testing Welfare Users is a Sham" uses Tennessee welfare testing coverage to expose misleading policy journalism. "The Meditation on Creepiness," "The Meditation on Superweapons," "The Meditation on the War on Applause Lights," and "The Meditation on Superweapons and Bingo" analyze social concepts that can end debate by categorizing dissent as suspect. Later essays include formalist power relations, Oppression Olympics, male violence arguments, Racist Dystopia, social justice for the highly demanding of rigor, controlled prejudice experiments, discrimination costs, bravery debates, Phil Robertson, MsScribe, First Amendment norms, Apophemi on triggers, false rape accusation statistics, and niceness. Source anchors: Dying to be Free; Tennessee; creepiness; superweapons; applause lights; Racist Dystopia; bravery debates; false rape accusations

Analysis: Dying to be Free and Tennessee welfare testing show Alexander treating social-justice claims as empirical claims when they describe policy effects or victim outcomes. Creepiness, superweapons, applause lights, and bravery debates name rhetorical tools that can protect true claims badly by making disagreement socially impossible. Racist Dystopia and false rape accusations show the section's most difficult balancing act: Alexander wants rigorous evidence and due process without using rigor as camouflage for indifference to discrimination, coercion, or sexual violence.

XII. Politicization

Summary: The politicization section studies how tribes convert neutral descriptions into threats. "Right is the New Left" begins with political reversal and status, "Weak Men are Superweapons" describes attacking a movement through its worst members, and "You Kant Dismiss Universalizability" tests maxims such as firing political opponents, firing homophobes, or arresting robbers. "I Can Tolerate Anything Except the Outgroup" uses Red Tribe and Blue Tribe psychology, Ferguson articles, white people, doctors, priests, and dating profiles to explain why tolerance often stops at local enemies. "Five Case Studies on Politicization," "Black People Less Likely," "Nydwracu's Fnords," "All in All, Another Brick in the Motte," "Ethnic Tension and Meaningless Arguments," "Race and Justice," "Framing for Light Instead of Heat," "The Wonderful Thing About Triggers," "Fearful Symmetry," and "Archipelago and Atomic Communitarianism" then analyze fnords, motte-and-bailey, race statistics, narrative framing, triggers, and community design. Source anchors: Weak Men; universalizability; Outgroup; Red Tribe; Blue Tribe; Ferguson; fnords; motte-and-bailey

Analysis: Weak Men and universalizability show Alexander trying to make political norms symmetric before applying them to enemies. Outgroup, Red Tribe, Blue Tribe, and Ferguson explain why symmetry is psychologically hard: people experience attacks on their coalition as attacks on reality itself. Fnords and motte-and-bailey make Language and Ideology diagnostic, showing how slogans carry hidden emotional instructions and how groups retreat to minimal claims when challenged before returning to stronger claims when among allies.

XIII. Competition and Cooperation

Summary: The final section gathers coordination failures into the anthology's broadest social diagnosis. "Galactic Core" begins with a science-fiction scale, "The Two-Income Trap" reviews Elizabeth Warren's argument about suburban school districts and household competition, and "Just for Stealing a Mouthful of Bread" uses punishment, poverty, and musical imagery to ask when individual blame hides structural traps. "Meditations on Moloch" then names the central pattern through Prisoner's Dilemma, dollar auctions, fish farming, Malthusian rats, capitalism, the Two-Income Trap, agriculture, arms races, cancer, races to the bottom, education, science, government corruption, and Congress. "Misperceptions on Moloch," "Freedom on the Centralized Web," "Book Review: Singer on Marx," and "Book Review: Red Plenty" continue the problem through centralized platforms, Marx's alienation, Soviet planning, and the failure of systems that promised coordination without markets. Source anchors: Galactic Core; Two-Income Trap; Moloch; dollar auctions; Malthusian trap; arms races; centralized web; Red Plenty

Analysis: Moloch, dollar auctions, Malthusian trap, and arms races make explicit what earlier sections implied: intelligence and goodwill do not guarantee good outcomes when competition rewards defection. The Two-Income Trap and centralized web bring Platform Governance into the coordination story, because school districts, family budgets, platforms, and online infrastructure all create rule environments people must optimize within. Red Plenty and Singer on Marx close the anthology by refusing an easy escape: markets, states, science, and platforms all solve some coordination problems while generating others, so the task is to design institutions with fewer traps rather than to declare one system innocent.
