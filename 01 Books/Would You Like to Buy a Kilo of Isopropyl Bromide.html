Place in the vault

This note lives under Finance, Firms, and Industrialization. Use it as a memoir of small-batch chemical entrepreneurship: purchasing, synthesis, salesmanship, catalog work, improvisation, dangerous materials, and the informal networks that made a small specialty supplier possible.

Detailed overview

Max G. Gergel's book is a comic, technically dense memoir about Columbia Organic Chemicals and the world of American small chemical supply from the 1930s into the 1960s. The title comes from his traveling-salesman persona at American Chemical Society meetings, where he sold through cigar smoke, hotel-room gossip, Wyeth Laboratories contacts, National Institute of Health chemists, Ohio State friends, and the endless hunger of academic laboratories for odd compounds. He calls his lecture "How to run a small chemical company unsuccessfully," but the joke works because he knows every invoice, flask, bad odor, reagent shortage, unpaid salary, improvised apparatus, and customer relationship that kept the company alive.

The first book is a formation story. Gergel moves from Columbia High School and Calhoun Literary Society debates into Arvid Czarnitski's chemistry laboratory, Atherton Whaley's assistantship, LeConte College night raids, and University of South Carolina chemical engineering. The memoir treats education as apprenticeship in scavenging and nerve: broken flasks from garbage areas, stolen notes on 3-aminophthalhydrazide, thermite, induction-coil initiations, Model-T trips to labs, and early syntheses of alkyl chlorides matter as much as formal classes. Gergel's teachers, including Czarnitski, Copenhaver, Lipscomb, Whaley, E. Emmet Reid, and Max Revelise, become part of the firm's eventual capital.

The wartime chapters show chemistry as a local war economy rather than a smooth national mobilization machine. Gergel works with Santee-Cooper after Harry Colman is drafted, tests reservoir materials, returns to the University, teaches pre-flight cadets, learns aircraft engines as fast as he teaches them, and makes chemicals for Dr. Bost, Manhattan Project-related needs, and Columbia Organic's first customers. The book's recurring mechanism is barter between ignorance and urgency: Gergel takes jobs before he fully knows how to do them, then uses friends, teachers, assistants, scavenged equipment, and all-night labor to make the job real.

Book 2 becomes a business history of a small specialty firm that survives by living in gaps too odd or too small for large companies. Columbia Organic Chemicals and Industrials sell alkyl halides, soap powder, antifreeze from diethylene glycol, insecticides, rat killers, fluorinated esters, sulfur compounds, mercaptans, dinitriles, and custom "dog-preps" for universities and industrial research labs. Gergel's world includes DuPont, Dow, Union Carbide, Naval Research Labs, L. Light, Edcan, Wateree Chemical Company, Chemetron, Ohio State, Caribou Chemicals, and consultants shuttling compounds and favors through personal trust long before procurement systems are standardized.

The memoir is also an account of the hazards and dirt that business histories often sand away. Wateree stinks up Lugoff and Camden with sulfur compounds; residues are poured on Kahn property before the EPA era; arsenic termite killers, ammonia fumes, soap fines, fluorination reagents, antimony trifluoride, bromine, GLC purity failures, and distillation-room fires all appear as ordinary operating conditions. Gergel is funny about cats, sailboats, secretaries, ex-wives, and plant gossip, but the comedy depends on proximity to real chemical risk.

The late chapters show the small firm aging into a different chemical industry. Gas-liquid chromatography exposes impurities that boiling range, density, and refractive index had missed; customers demand higher purity; Columbia's catalog looks dated; newer firms compete for research dollars; and consulting for Chemetron sends Gergel to Rock Hill, Newport, Israel, Yavne, Ashkelon, and Jerusalem. The epilogue returns him from biblical landscapes and Israeli industrial dreams to Columbia's old plant, old product line, insufficient instrumentation, and no backup personnel. The book's real subject is not failure, but the exhausting craft of staying barely viable in a technical market that keeps raising the cost of competence.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Commercial Society: Gergel shows a technical market built from catalogs, sales calls, academic meetings, custom preparations, shortages, invoices, consultants, and obscure customer demand.
  • Informal Institutions: friendships, ACS hotel rooms, professor networks, immigrant chemists, reciprocal favors, reputation, and personal trust make Columbia Organic possible before formal procurement catches up.
  • Capital Allocation: the memoir repeatedly shows how a tiny firm converts underpaid labor, borrowed equipment, no-salary survival, DuPont apparatus, land purchases, and consulting fees into productive capacity.
  • Technological Change: gas-liquid chromatography, purity demands, analytical equipment, fluorination chemistry, and specialty synthesis change what competence costs.
  • State Capacity: Santee-Cooper, wartime pre-flight instruction, Chemical Warfare recruitment, Army contracts, public reservoirs, and pre-EPA regulation place the small firm inside changing public capacity.
  • Industrial Policy: the Israel chapters ask whether specialty chemistry can become a deliberate national development strategy rather than only a local entrepreneurial accident.
  • Knowledge Preservation: the memoir preserves tacit chemical craft through named reactions, odors, apparatus, accidents, suppliers, teachers, customers, and shop-floor routines.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Prologue

Summary: Gergel opens as a Columbia Organic Chemicals traveling salesman at American Chemical Society meetings, where Sam Tubis, Micky Freed, Peter Russell, Charlie Grogan, Tony Schrecker, Ken Greenlee, and Tom Osdene gather in lobbies and hotel rooms instead of attending lectures. The group smokes cigars, trades chemical gossip, lies about amorous adventures, and turns personal friendship into Columbia Organics customers. Ray Dessy of V.P.I. asks Gergel to speak to graduate students, the title becomes "How to run a small chemical company unsuccessfully," and the longer book title becomes his stock answer when audiences ask whether he is writing. Source anchors: American Chemical Society; Columbia Organic Chemicals; Sam Tubis; Ken Greenlee; Ray Dessy; V.P.I.; Isopropyl Bromide.

Analysis: The prologue explains the sales machinery behind the memoir. American Chemical Society meetings, Columbia Organic Chemicals, Sam Tubis, Ken Greenlee, Ray Dessy, and V.P.I. show Informal Institutions doing market work: hotel-room sociability, gossip, old friends, and comic performance become the trust layer through which obscure chemicals are sold. Gergel's joking title is not decorative; it tells the reader to expect a business history told through customers, lectures, and the performance required to make a tiny supplier memorable.

Chapter 1

Summary: In 1936 Gergel is a tiny, loud Columbia High School student in the Calhoun Literary Society, humiliated in debate against Memminger High School and then redirected by chemistry teacher Arvid Czarnitski. He recalls Wardlaw Junior High, scavenging broken flasks and test tubes from the University of South Carolina garbage area, making phosphorus from urine, hydrogen sulfide, and explosives at home on Calhoun Street. Czarnitski, Atherton Mikell Whaley, Mr. Crow, room 312, a five-dollar monthly assistantship, Columbia High's chemical club, thermite, silver nitrate branding, and LeConte College night raids make chemistry into work, mischief, and identity. Source anchors: Columbia High School; Arvid Czarnitski; Wardlaw Junior High; Atherton Whaley; room 312; LeConte College; thermite.

Analysis: Chapter 1 makes Gergel's chemical career begin in humiliation, scavenging, and patronage. Columbia High, Arvid Czarnitski, Wardlaw, Atherton Whaley, room 312, LeConte College, and thermite show Knowledge Preservation as apprenticeship rather than archive: broken flasks, copied procedures, dangerous demonstrations, and permissive teachers transmit tacit lab craft. Columbia Organic's later improvisation grows from these habits of finding discarded apparatus, copying useful procedures, charming adults, and turning danger into confidence.

Chapter 2

Summary: At the University of South Carolina, Whaley's work under Dr. Copenhaver on n-butyl chloride and other alkyl chlorides pulls Gergel toward serious organic synthesis. Gergel remains small enough to see Dr. Hopkins about delayed puberty, then grows while studying chemistry, working with Dr. E. Emmet Reid's intellectual world, and building status through laboratory competence. The chapter includes zinc chloride-catalyzed alkyl chloride synthesis, hydrochloric acid, alcohols, Copenhaver's students, Columbia High obligations, university labs, and Gergel's increasing movement from mascot to working chemist. Source anchors: University of South Carolina; Dr. Copenhaver; n-butyl chloride; zinc chloride; Dr. Hopkins; alkyl chlorides; Whaley.

Analysis: Chapter 2 links bodily maturation to chemical apprenticeship without making either sentimental. Dr. Copenhaver, n-butyl chloride, zinc chloride, Dr. Hopkins, alkyl chlorides, and Whaley show Gergel growing into a technical world at the same time he literally grows. The alkyl chloride work is especially important because it foreshadows the specialty halides that Columbia Organic will sell for decades.

Chapter 3

Summary: Gergel enters chemical engineering at the University, where open-book exams, curved grades, Physics, Descriptive Geometry, Plane Analytics, Chemical German, and freshman attrition reduce the class to fewer than a hundred. He works as a grader, assistant, and lab operator while classmates disappear and professors use competition to sort students. The chapter follows Engineering discipline, registrar grade peeking, all A's, lost friendships, Ragsdale, fraternity dropouts, and the department's pride that half the applicants vanish in the first year. Source anchors: Engineering attrition; open-book exams; Chemical German; registrar; Ragsdale; curved grades; University.

Analysis: Chapter 3 explains why Gergel later treats exhaustion as normal business practice. Engineering attrition, open-book exams, Chemical German, the registrar, Ragsdale, and curved grades show a training system that rewards stamina, opportunism, and technical recall under pressure. Columbia Organic's later all-night production runs and underpaid multitasking look less accidental after this account of education as survival by workload.

Chapter 4

Summary: Whaley writes from Ohio State about using the Prins reaction to make heptachloropropane, dehydrochlorinating it to hexachloropropene, and fluorinating with antimony trifluoride and bromine to make trifluorotrichloropropene. Gergel and colleagues make the camphor-smelling hepta from chloroform, tetrachloroethylene, and aluminum chloride while he also studies Thermodynamics with Mercer, Ragsdale, latent heat, entropy, Moliere diagrams, Otto cycles, and Rankine cycles. The chapter mixes high chemical specificity with the absurdity of student schedules and laboratory odor. Source anchors: Prins reaction; heptachloropropane; hexachloropropene; antimony trifluoride; Thermo; Mercer; Rankine cycles.

Analysis: Chapter 4 shows Gergel's memoir working as both technical record and comic self-portrait. The Prins reaction, heptachloropropane, hexachloropropene, antimony trifluoride, Thermo, Mercer, and Rankine cycles are not generic school details; they name the precise chemical and engineering repertoire that later lets him accept strange contracts. The emerald-green reaction and camphor odor also show how sensory memory anchors his business history.

Chapter 5

Summary: By junior year Gergel has one A and four B's, gives up on Phi Beta Kappa and graduate school, and prepares for industry or Chemical Warfare as Army recruiters court senior engineers. He makes raw materials for Whaley's trifluorotrichloropropene work, supplies the Research Foundation, grades papers, teaches coach classes, supervises assistants, and checks Columbia High and Dreher labs. The chapter includes the Mercer Brigade of draft-wary engineers, Economics, Advanced Organic, Principles of Chemical Engineering, Direct and Alternating Currents, Geology, Determinative Mineralogy, and a class reduced to fewer than forty students. Source anchors: Phi Beta Kappa; Chemical Warfare; Research Foundation; Mercer Brigade; Advanced Organic; Dreher; trifluorotrichloropropene.

Analysis: Chapter 5 turns limited prospects into entrepreneurial preparation. Phi Beta Kappa, Chemical Warfare, Research Foundation work, the Mercer Brigade, Advanced Organic, Dreher, and trifluorotrichloropropene show Gergel learning to live without a clean academic path. His later company depends on exactly this mix of partial credentials, practical output, teaching labor, and willingness to make needed compounds for whoever will pay.

Chapter 6

Summary: Gergel handles Dr. Guy F. Lipscomb's mail while Lipscomb works on solidified glue and artificial blood at Continental Chemical Company, signs letters, gives lectures, grades papers, and manages recommendations. A confidential South Carolina Public Service Authority letter from R. M. Cooper pulls him into Santee-Cooper after chemist Harry Colman is drafted. The chapter includes Sumter Street porch meetings, Jeep M. as proctor, Pinopolis reservoir work, Santee-Cooper emergency needs, Harry Colman, Public Service Authority bureaucracy, and Gergel's movement from university assistant to field chemist. Source anchors: Guy F. Lipscomb; Continental Chemical; artificial blood; R. M. Cooper; Santee-Cooper; Harry Colman; Pinopolis reservoir.

Analysis: Chapter 6 matters because Gergel becomes useful outside the classroom. Guy F. Lipscomb, Continental Chemical, artificial blood, R. M. Cooper, Santee-Cooper, Harry Colman, and Pinopolis reservoir show chemistry inserted into wartime infrastructure and State Capacity. The habit of answering mail, signing for Lipscomb, and stepping into Colman's vacancy anticipates the small-company rule that whoever is present must become purchasing agent, chemist, salesman, and administrator.

Chapter 7

Summary: Gergel leaves Santee-Cooper in February 1943 after the Pinopolis reservoir team has enough information to stand up in court and the draft is closing in. Mr. Heyward, Mrs. Beck, Carl Wilson, the Wests, Preacher, Dr. Blizzard, and the flotsam-and-jetsam crew mark his departure from a working field team. He returns excited by pre-flight school and full-time chemical production, with Santee unable to shield him from military service and colleagues joking about plaques, stars, free travel, and strong drink. Source anchors: February 1943; Santee-Cooper; Pinopolis reservoir; Mr. Heyward; Mrs. Beck; Preacher; Dr. Blizzard.

Analysis: Chapter 7 turns Santee-Cooper into Gergel's first adult workplace rather than a mere job. February 1943, Pinopolis reservoir, Mr. Heyward, Mrs. Beck, Preacher, and Dr. Blizzard show a young chemist leaving a crew that has matured him and returning to a war economy that still may draft him. The farewell matters because Columbia Organic will later be built from similar temporary teams, affectionate jokes, and practical competence under pressure.

Chapter 8

Summary: Around 1944 Gergel works on Columbia Organic Chemicals' first catalog while teaching Physics and Theory of Flight six hours a day to pre-flight cadets. With Ayers and Sloan he confronts Rappenecker over Norman's workload, shifts into Aircraft Engines and Communications, and learns LeRhone, Lycoming, and Continental engines while teaching cadets and mechanics. The chapter also covers WTS classes, Bowles, flight-school politics, Columbia Organics' birth in a lawyer's office, and Gergel's willingness to teach material he has only just learned. Source anchors: first catalog; Physics and Theory of Flight; Ayers; Sloan; Rappenecker; LeRhone engine; Aircraft Engines.

Analysis: Chapter 8 shows Columbia Organic being born beside teaching exhaustion. The first catalog, Physics and Theory of Flight, Ayers, Sloan, Rappenecker, and the LeRhone engine show Gergel converting institutional overload into leverage and knowledge. His aircraft-engine teaching is funny because he starts ignorant, but it also names the business method: take the assignment, learn fast, and sell competence before it is fully comfortable.

Chapter 9

Summary: Gergel buys the sailboat Dawn from Skipper George Sumner of the Sea Scout ship Viking, recalling rivalry with the Rainbow and earlier sailing with George Dieter, Pete, and Ike Smith. Sumner, a former World War I Canadian Navy destroyer captain, also runs a Columbia business making press cloth and converting human hair from China and Japan into filtration mats. The chapter ties Lake Murray sailing, the Dixie Starlight, Sea Scout Cove, Prosperity, wartime shortages of young sailors, and the Dawn's reliability to Gergel's nonlaboratory life. Source anchors: Dawn; Skipper George Sumner; Viking; Rainbow; Dixie Starlight; Lake Murray; human hair filtration.

Analysis: Chapter 9 matters because boats and chemistry share Gergel's affection for craft, maintenance, and improvised crews. Dawn, George Sumner, Viking, Rainbow, Dixie Starlight, Lake Murray, and human hair filtration show the same Columbia world producing sailors, filter-press suppliers, and chemists. The chapter also keeps Gergel from becoming only a laboratory operator; his business memory is tied to water, weather, weekends, and local mentors.

Chapter 10

Summary: Gergel finds a kitten in the chemical plant while preparing one of Dr. Bost's bromides, feeds him milk from a beaker, names him Borso D'Este, and appoints him plant cat after Borso eats a praying mantis. The chapter moves through Columbia Organic's animal life, Dr. Bost's bromides, cats, plant routines, heartbreak, and the comic seriousness with which Gergel integrates the cat into production culture. Borso joins the firm's human cast as a working presence in a place of flasks, reagents, and odd companionship. Source anchors: Borso D'Este; Dr. Bost's bromides; kitten; milk beaker; praying mantis; plant cat; Columbia Organic.

Analysis: Chapter 10 uses Borso to show the plant as a household as well as a workplace. Borso D'Este, Dr. Bost's bromides, the milk beaker, praying mantis, plant cat role, and Columbia Organic all make affection part of the company's operating culture. The cat story matters because Gergel's firm survives on loyalty and tolerance for eccentricity, not only on formulas and invoices.

Chapter 1, Book 2

Summary: Book 2 opens in early 1945 with Columbia Organics staffed by Jules, Mama, Max R., Clive, Maison, and Blair Turner, still holding part of an Army contract and selling alkyl halides to colleges. Gergel loses his University job but is rescued by Professor Ball, who asks him to teach Direct and Alternating Currents despite his ignorance. He is twenty-five, has about $900, an old sailboat, a small house at Ballentine's Landing, no running water, an outhouse, jerrycans, Clive at home, and a business that breaks even only if he takes no salary. Source anchors: early 1945; Jules; Clive; alkyl halides; Professor Ball; Ballentine's Landing; jerrycans.

Analysis: Book 2 begins by making undercapitalization concrete. Early 1945, Jules, Clive, alkyl halides, Professor Ball, Ballentine's Landing, and jerrycans show Capital Allocation at household scale: Columbia Organics converts family labor, no salary, teaching rescue, and bare equipment into chemical output. Gergel's break-even point matters because every later expansion, catalog, and contract sits on this fragile domestic base.

Chapter 2, Book 2

Summary: In 1948 Industrials hires Irving Anderson, an ex-Army sergeant, to crank the soap machine and fill Sudsy Washing Powder bags with borrowed Woods Brothers equipment. A 1947 antifreeze shortage lets Gergel buy diethylene glycol from Dow Chemical and Union Carbide contacts, add triethanolamine as rust inhibitor, sell gallons and cases, and buy a new truck. The business now sells soap powder, concrete cleaner, insecticides, rat killers, antifreeze, and arsenic termite-killer materials to customers such as the twitching Mr. Jackson. Source anchors: Irving Anderson; Sudsy Washing Powder; Woods Brothers; diethylene glycol; Dow Chemical; triethanolamine; Mr. Jackson.

Analysis: Chapter 2 of Book 2 shows the firm surviving by opportunistic product switching. Irving Anderson, Sudsy Washing Powder, Woods Brothers, diethylene glycol, Dow Chemical, triethanolamine, and Mr. Jackson show a company moving between consumer goods, industrial cleaners, antifreeze, and poisons as shortages appear. This is not a clean specialty-chemicals story yet; it is cash flow built from whatever the local market will absorb.

Chapter 3, Book 2

Summary: From 1949 to 1951 Industrials outpaces Organics while Gergel learns sales and agrees to make almost any academic "dog-prep" for a price. Dr. Parry Borgstrom of the Naval Research Labs visits as an itinerant scientist and book collector, qualifying vendors while pretending casual friendship through Gittman. The chapter covers Naval Research Labs, custom preparations, academia's difficult compounds, Borgstrom's con, Gergel's counter-con, and the desperation that makes Columbia willing to accept strange work. Source anchors: 1949; Industrials; dog-prep; Parry Borgstrom; Naval Research Labs; Gittman; vendor qualification.

Analysis: Chapter 3 of Book 2 explains the information asymmetry in custom chemical supply. Industrials, dog-preps, Parry Borgstrom, Naval Research Labs, Gittman, and vendor qualification make Information and Coordination a sales problem: buyers need proof that a tiny firm can produce obscure compounds reliably, while Gergel must read whether each friendly visitor is a customer, inspector, trickster, or all three.

Chapter 4, Book 2

Summary: As the shortage market fades in the early 1950s, Gergel turns toward newer specialty fields and receives a letter from Henry de Laszlo of L. Light in England asking him to attend an ACS meeting in Atlantic City. Gergel plans to see Dr. Reid for help selling to northern industries and describes de Laszlo as an aristocratic chemical entrepreneur, like Ed Stirling of Edcan, who charms academics into donating chemicals that L. Light then sells. The chapter includes L. Light, slow delivery, indifferent purity, Atlantic City, large northern industries, and the morally flexible trade in laboratory curiosities. Source anchors: Henry de Laszlo; L. Light; ACS Atlantic City; Dr. Reid; Edcan; Ed Stirling; donated chemicals.

Analysis: Chapter 4 of Book 2 places Columbia Organics inside a wider gray market of specialty suppliers. Henry de Laszlo, L. Light, ACS Atlantic City, Dr. Reid, Edcan, Ed Stirling, and donated chemicals show Commercial Society operating through morally flexible exchange, not clean textbook markets. The chapter matters because compounds, reputations, and customers circulate through informal trust before purity standards and procurement rules tighten.

Chapter 5, Book 2

Summary: Bill Wannamaker and Ray Christena from DuPont's May plant in Camden ask about forming a sulfur-compound company, leading to Wateree Chemical Company at Lugoff near the railroad. Wateree makes sulfur compounds, mercaptans, and malodorous products that let farmers and tobacco from the area be identified by smell, eventually forcing a move to the Camden boondocks. DuPont officials secretly ask Gergel about odors blamed on the May plant, while Bill, Charles, Wateree Indians lore, Lugoff property values, nylon-plant complaints, and prairie contamination turn entrepreneurship into nuisance. Source anchors: Bill Wannamaker; Ray Christena; DuPont May plant; Wateree Chemical; Lugoff; mercaptans; Camden.

Analysis: Chapter 5 of Book 2 is the clearest environmental chapter in the memoir. Bill Wannamaker, Ray Christena, DuPont's May plant, Wateree Chemical, Lugoff, mercaptans, and Camden show State Capacity by its absence: before effective environmental regulation, small chemical enterprise can externalize odor onto neighbors and even onto DuPont's reputation. Gergel's humor about farmers and tobacco smelling of Wateree does not erase the mechanism: profitable sulfur chemistry makes property, air, and blame move together.

Chapter 6, Book 2

Summary: Wateree prospers with sodium butyrate, mercaptans, sulfones, and sulfur derivatives, while Gergel visits Ohio State and meets German chemists on the Grundemann-Herndon project from the Max Planck institute. Rudy Pvaetz, Ehrenfred Kober, Gerhardt Ottman, Henry Ulrich, Ken Greenlee, Vince Wiley, Ralph Pelly, Leo Paridon, Henne, Caribou Chemicals, meta-aminobenzotrifluoride, phenothiazine, beer, and Beethoven fill the chapter's network. Columbia buys and sells fluorine compounds through friendships as much as formal channels. Source anchors: sodium butyrate; mercaptans; Ohio State; Max Planck; Gerhardt Ottman; Caribou Chemicals; meta-aminobenzotrifluoride.

Analysis: Chapter 6 of Book 2 shows technical networks becoming supply networks. Sodium butyrate, mercaptans, Ohio State, Max Planck chemists, Gerhardt Ottman, Caribou Chemicals, and meta-aminobenzotrifluoride connect academic projects, immigrant expertise, small companies, and Columbia's catalog. The singing, beer, and Beethoven matter because trust in this market is built socially before it becomes orders and raw-material exchanges.

Chapter 7, Book 2

Luke and Claire Jones feed Gergel and Clive, Luke helps set up the DuPont building, Max Revelise returns from the road, and Dick Sellers from DuPont Experimental Station assists with columns and first runs. The plant works three shifts, seven days a week, with fruit juice supplied by DuPont for eye irritation and families on Lovers Lane enjoying the surplus. DuPont equipment, Heitmiller, dinitrile, Luke Jones, Max Revelise, Dick Sellers, and ammonia fumes turn Capital Allocation into a shop-floor arrangement: outside apparatus and expertise arrive with domestic support, shift labor, and bodily irritation. The fruit juice detail is comic, but it also marks the reality of a contract that runs continuously through families, shifts, and eyes.

The plant's physical growth is therefore not anonymous expansion; it is a memorial structure built from the people whose knowledge and purchases kept the firm alive.

Chapter 9, Book 2

Summary: Between Jules's death in 1959 and Max Revelise's death in 1961, Gergel rebuilds the plant after the 1958 fire, buys land from Mr. Kahn, and converts an old schoolhouse into offices. He admits that before the EPA era his workers poured residues from reaction flasks onto Kahn property, negotiates the acre down to $3000, and adds Sonny's distillation room, four production labs, Elaine the secretary, his dog Tuba, and new family changes after Eleanor Elizabeth's birth. Source anchors: Jules; Max Revelise; 1958 fire; Mr. Kahn; EPA; Sonny's distillation room; Eleanor Elizabeth.

Analysis: Chapter 9 of Book 2 puts grief, expansion, and pollution in the same business ledger. Jules, Max Revelise, the 1958 fire, Mr. Kahn, pre-EPA residues, Sonny's distillation room, and Eleanor Elizabeth show a firm growing through family loss and environmental shortcuts. The land deal is funny because Gergel confesses the trespass to Kahn, but it also shows why State Capacity matters: without enforceable environmental rules, contamination becomes a negotiable neighbor problem rather than a public constraint.

Howard Hartough, Chemetron, Rock Hill Laboratories, Newport, fluorinated esters, Tube Turns, and Girdler Catalysts show Columbia's small-plant knowledge being rented by a holding-company chemical division. The consultancy matters because Gergel's hard-won improvisation has become expertise legible to larger firms.

Chapter 11, Book 2

Summary: Gergel describes Columbia at the end of an era, facing stricter purity requirements and instrumentation that old measures such as boiling range, density, and refractive index cannot satisfy. Gas-liquid chromatography, or GLC, exposes impurities, isomerizes alkyl halides under column conditions, and lets customers challenge compounds once accepted as good. Columbia buys analytical equipment, learns to make purer chemicals, drops troublesome listings, and confronts letters from customers who can now prove what is wrong. Source anchors: gas-liquid chromatograph; boiling range; density; refractive index; alkyl halides; purity requirements; analytical equipment.

Analysis: Chapter 11 of Book 2 explains Technological Change as a discipline imposed on the small firm. Gas-liquid chromatography, boiling range, density, refractive index, alkyl halides, purity requirements, and analytical equipment show old bench judgment losing authority to instruments. Columbia's earlier advantage, making odd compounds fast, now has to be paired with proof, documentation, and capital-intensive analysis.

Chapter 12, Book 2

Summary: In 1963 Hartough asks Gergel to visit Israel and report on the possibility of a Swiss-type specialty chemical industry. Gergel had already discussed an Israeli organic-specialties plant with Alfred Bader, scientific advisor Lahav, Ernst Bergmann of Hebrew University, Cyril Woolf of Allied, Bill Pearce of MCB, and Howard and Cora Hartough. The chapter follows visits to Israeli chemical companies, unpaid consultants, reports that may not be read, Yavne, American sales possibilities, and the gap between industrial hopes and Israeli habits of soliciting advice without acting on it. Source anchors: 1963; Israel; Howard Hartough; Alfred Bader; Lahav; Ernst Bergmann; Hebrew University.

Analysis: Chapter 12 of Book 2 takes Gergel's small-company knowledge international. Israel, Hartough, Alfred Bader, Lahav, Ernst Bergmann, Hebrew University, and the Swiss-type chemical model make Industrial Policy concrete: specialty chemistry appears as a possible national development strategy rather than only a Columbia business niche. The chapter matters because Gergel can see the appeal of an Israeli organic-specialties plant while recognizing that consultant visits do not automatically create industry.

Epilogue

Summary: Gergel spends his final day in Israel driving to Yavne by way of Ashkelon, swimming in blue water, collecting orange shells, and recalling Philistines, Samson, Delilah, David, Titus, Vespasian, and the rabbis allowed to open a school after Jerusalem's fall. He visits Arjeh Galun, drives to Jerusalem, sees David's tomb, looks from the hills toward the old city held by Jordan, and from Ramat Rachel sees Jericho and Bethlehem. He then flies back to Columbia, where an old plant, old product line, weak catalog, few brilliant chemists, no backup personnel, new competitors, and rising research-dollar pressure await him. Source anchors: Yavne; Ashkelon; Samson; Arjeh Galun; Ramat Rachel; Jericho; Columbia.

Analysis: The epilogue makes the return to Columbia feel intentionally deflating. Yavne, Ashkelon, Samson, Arjeh Galun, Ramat Rachel, Jericho, and Columbia set biblical and Israeli landscapes beside the mundane burden of running an under-resourced chemical company. The contrast matters because the memoir's romance of chemistry always ends back at the plant: old equipment, thin staff, bad catalog, and another day's survival.
