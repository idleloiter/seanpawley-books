Detailed overview

Malcolm Cowley's anthology arranges Faulkner as the maker of a single county, not as a writer of isolated novels and stories. The extracted text opens by placing Yoknapatawpha County around Jefferson, Frenchman's Bend, the Tallahatchie bottom, the Chickasaw dispossession, the McCaslin plantation, and the Sartoris and Compson families. Cowley keeps interrupting the fiction with editor's notes because the book is trying to make scattered publications behave like one local chronicle: Chickasaw chiefs such as Ikkemotubbe and Issetibbeha, white founders such as Doctor Habersham, Alexander Holston, Louis Grenier, and Thomas Sutpen, and later figures such as Flem Snopes, Dilsey, Percy Grimm, and Isaac McCaslin appear as residents of the same historical ground.

The first large movement goes back before Jefferson has settled into courthouse records and marble monuments. "A Justice," "The Courthouse," "Red Leaves," and "Was" make the county out of sale, memory, kinship, and fraud: Sam Fathers remembers being Had-Two-Fathers, the town's records begin in an iron chest and a lost Carolina lock, Issetibbeha's death sends Chickasaw men hunting an enslaved man who is supposed to die with him, and Tomey's Turl turns Uncle Buck McCaslin's household into a comic chase toward Miss Sophonsiba Beauchamp's "Warwick." Cowley wants the reader to see that the old order is not a stable Eden. It is built from land transfers, slavery, improvised law, and rituals that are already decaying as soon as they are narrated.

The Civil War and Reconstruction selections then connect family romance with institutional wreckage. Granny Millard, Ringo, Louvinia, Colonel Nathaniel G. Dick, the burned Sartoris house, and the road full of self-emancipated people in "Raid" show the war not as a battlefield abstraction but as wagons, mules, silver, borrowed horses, Federal paperwork, and hungry people moving through Mississippi. "Wash" makes the ruin harsher by pairing Thomas Sutpen's aristocratic self-image with Wash Jones's worshipful poverty, Milly's childbirth, the black stallion, the store, and the scythe. Cowley places "The Bear" after these stories so Isaac McCaslin's refusal of the McCaslin inheritance can be read against the plantation books, Carothers McCaslin's descendants, Sam Fathers's teaching, Old Ben, and the retreating Big Bottom.

The middle of the anthology changes social register without leaving the county. "Spotted Horses" brings Flem Snopes, Buck Hipps, Jody Varner, Ratliff, Mrs. Littlejohn, Eck Snopes, and a pen full of barbed-wire ponies into Frenchman's Bend, turning the moral vocabulary of plantations and honor into a cash comedy of auctions, lawsuits, bad bargains, and social appetite. Cowley then pairs the Snopes invasion with the fading Compsons in "That Evening Sun" and "Dilsey," the war-haunted aviators in "Ad Astra," and the brittle old-house symbolism of the order he describes in his editor's note. The old families are not simply replaced by new people; their kitchens, banks, porches, jails, stores, and airfields reveal different ways that debt, race, fear, sex, labor, and public law keep pressing on private households.

"Old Man" widens the geography beyond Yoknapatawpha while keeping Faulkner's interest in coercive systems. Parchman is a cotton plantation prison; the 1927 Mississippi flood becomes both a natural catastrophe and a bureaucratic assignment; the tall convict's dime-novel train robbery, the plump convict's 199-year sentence, Mound's Landing, Memphis headlines, National Guard tents, levee gangs, skiffs, and the pregnant woman in the cypress snag turn the river into a force that briefly unmoors the categories of prison, marriage, duty, and escape. Cowley explicitly treats the story as a Mississippi companion to Huckleberry Finn, but the anthology's placement also makes it a river version of Faulkner's county logic: a man tries to return to confinement because freedom has become more demanding than the state farm.

The final selections bring mechanized modernity and old memory into the same field. "Death Drag" introduces barnstorming planes, an X-shaped municipal runway, Captain Warren, Jake, Jock, and the commercial spectacle of risk; "Percy Grimm" turns the State National Guard, the American Legion, the courthouse square, Joe Christmas, Joanna Burden, and Gavin Stevens into a study of racialized militarism; "Delta Autumn" returns Isaac McCaslin to the Delta in cars and motor launches, with Roth Edmonds's lover and child forcing the old hunter to face the survival of the McCaslin bloodline among the very people his renunciation was supposed to honor. "The Jail" closes by letting a building, its walls, locks, whitewash, and remembered prisoners, hold the county's record more stubbornly than official archives.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Knowledge Preservation: Cowley's anthology turns scattered novels and stories into a county chronicle, and "The Jail" makes walls, scratches, whitewash, and remembered prisoners compete with courthouse archives as carriers of local memory.
  • Legibility: Jefferson's courthouse origin, land grants, slave bills of sale, tax rolls, and marriage records show how disputed settlement and dispossession become administrative facts.
  • Coercive Labor: Tomey's Turl, the Chickasaw slave quarters, the McCaslin plantation, and Parchman bind kinship, ritual, cotton, punishment, and forced work into the same county history.
  • Moral Economy: "Spotted Horses" exposes how Flem Snopes and Buck Hipps violate local expectations about fair dealing, usable goods, and accountable sellers.
  • Language and Ideology: Percy Grimm's uniform, white American war talk, and civic order vocabulary show racial violence becoming thinkable as public duty.
  • Path Dependence and Institutional Drift: the anthology follows Yoknapatawpha institutions as courthouse, plantation, bank, jail, store, airfield, and hunting camp inherit earlier violence rather than leaving it behind.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

THE OLD PEOPLE

Summary: Cowley's first divider announces four early Yoknapatawpha pieces about Chickasaws, white settlers, and the McCaslin plantation before and around the time of Uncle Buck and Uncle Buddy. The note names the Chickasaws as Faulkner's Indians, explains how settlers such as Thomas Sutpen could buy or cheat them out of land, and says the planters turned dispossession and enslaved labor into cotton, more enslaved people, and a local aristocratic order. It also defines what Faulkner admires in the planters as a moral code rather than silk, silver, champagne, or baronial display. Source anchors: Chickasaws; Thomas Sutpen; McCaslin plantation; Uncle Buck; Uncle Buddy; cotton.

Analysis: Chickasaws, Thomas Sutpen, and the McCaslin plantation give the anthology its starting materials: land taken from Native owners, wealth made through cotton, and a white code Cowley says later heirs lose. Uncle Buck and Uncle Buddy matter because Cowley is not beginning with national politics but with household arrangements, kin obligations, and plantation habits that Faulkner will keep testing in later stories.

Editor's Note

Summary: The editor's note identifies "A Justice," "The Courthouse," "Red Leaves," and "Was" as the contents of this opening group and tells the reader how each piece connects to the larger Yoknapatawpha cast. Quentin Compson, Caddy, and Jason enter through "A Justice"; Sam Fathers appears as the Chickasaw hunter living among Black workers; "The Courthouse" comes from Requiem for a Nun and introduces Jefferson's founders, Thomas Sutpen, his French architect, and his wild enslaved men; "Red Leaves" belongs with Faulkner's Chickasaw stories; and "Was" opens Go Down, Moses with Uncle Buck, Uncle Buddy, their poker-playing bachelor household, and their refusal to profit comfortably from slavery. Source anchors: Quentin Compson; Sam Fathers; Requiem for a Nun; Thomas Sutpen; Red Leaves; Go Down, Moses.

Analysis: Quentin Compson, Sam Fathers, and Thomas Sutpen make the editor's note a map rather than a preface. Cowley is teaching the reader to carry names from one excerpt to another: Requiem for a Nun explains Jefferson's civic origin, Red Leaves gives the Chickasaw material a violent ritual form, and Go Down, Moses turns the McCaslin plantation into the moral ledger that later weighs on Isaac McCaslin.

A Justice

Summary: "A Justice" is told through a Compson child who visits the family farm with Grandfather Compson, Roskus, Caddy, and Jason and listens to Sam Fathers in his shop. Sam, called Uncle Blue-Gum by Black workers and Sam Fathers by white people, says he was once Had-Two-Fathers, son of a Chickasaw man and an enslaved woman owned by the Man. His story brings in Doom, formerly Ikkemotubbe, returning from New Orleans with enslaved people, a puppy, a gold box of salt, and a calculating eye for succession. The child's Saturday farm visit opens onto plantation ownership, Chickasaw inheritance, racial classification, and the memory of Herman Basket. Source anchors: Grandfather Compson; Roskus; Caddy; Sam Fathers; Had-Two-Fathers; Ikkemotubbe.

Analysis: Grandfather Compson and Roskus place the story inside a white household routine, while Sam Fathers and Had-Two-Fathers move it into a history that the child only partly understands. Ikkemotubbe's New Orleans return and Herman Basket's account make inheritance and violence inseparable, so the story gives Cowley's anthology a first example of Faulkner's habit of embedding public dispossession inside a remembered conversation at a farm.

The Courthouse (A Name for the City)

Summary: "The Courthouse" explains Jefferson's origin through accident, record keeping, and frontier improvisation rather than planned civic virtue. A Chickasaw Agency trading post accumulates land grants, patents, slave bills of sale, tax rolls, militia rolls, and marriage and death notations in an iron chest behind the post office store. A jailbreak by Natchez Trace bandits, possibly confused by legend with the Harpes but more likely linked to John Murrel's world, moves the chest to a lean-to beside the jail. The story turns Alexander Holston's fifteen-pound Carolina lock, Doctor Samuel Habersham, Louis Grenier, Pettigrew the mail rider, Compson, Peabody, Ratcliffe, and Mohataha's Bureau of Indian Affairs account into the accidental birth of county government. Source anchors: iron chest; Natchez Trace; Alexander Holston; Carolina lock; Pettigrew; Mohataha.

Analysis: The iron chest and Carolina lock make law look material, heavy, and absurd before it looks dignified. Alexander Holston's property claim, Pettigrew's federal mail logic, and Mohataha's account show how Jefferson's courthouse comes from overlapping claims by settlers, the United States, and dispossessed Chickasaws. Land grants, tax rolls, slave bills of sale, and marriage records create Legibility by converting those contested claims into documents officials can store, retrieve, and enforce. Cowley therefore makes Jefferson's official center begin in debt, paperwork, panic, and opportunism.

18-

Summary: This section is "Red Leaves," set after Issetibbeha's death, when Three Basket and another Chickasaw cross the plantation to the slave quarters looking for the enslaved man expected to accompany the dead chief. The cabins are whitewashed brick, the lane is marked by bare feet and children's objects, and the central cabin contains ceremonial sticks daubed with red clay. Three Basket remembers the old way before quarters and Negroes, complains that slavery has made men manage labor, and treats the missing man as a ritual problem. The story uses Issetibbeha, Doom, Three Basket, the slave quarters, the creek-bottom drums, and the fugitive's fear to show a Native ruling order already altered by plantation wealth. Source anchors: Issetibbeha; Three Basket; slave quarters; red clay; Doom; creek-bottom drums.

Analysis: Issetibbeha's death and Three Basket's search turn slavery into a Chickasaw ritual crisis rather than only a white plantation fact. The slave quarters, red clay records, and creek-bottom drums show that the enslaved people have their own collective life inside the plantation. Doom's earlier rule hangs over the section because the old Chickasaw order has absorbed the economics of human property without gaining control over the fear and resistance it creates.

Was

Summary: "Was" begins with domestic farce: Cass Edmonds and Uncle Buck discover that Tomey's Turl has escaped again, while Uncle Buddy battles a fox and dogs in the kitchen. Tomey's Turl has run toward Mr. Hubert Beauchamp's plantation to see Tennie, and the chase pulls Uncle Buck and Cass toward Miss Sophonsiba, Warwick, Black John, old Jake, Mr. Hubert's dinner horn, toddies, red ribbon, and a wager. The comic machinery depends on the McCaslin brothers' bachelor habits, Tomey's Turl's half-McCaslin status, Tennie's desirability, and Miss Sophonsiba's long campaign to marry Uncle Buck. Source anchors: Tomey's Turl; Uncle Buck; Uncle Buddy; Tennie; Miss Sophonsiba; Warwick.

Analysis: Tomey's Turl, Tennie, and Miss Sophonsiba make "Was" a comedy about slavery's family entanglements rather than an escape plot alone. Uncle Buck and Uncle Buddy are ridiculous, but the chase exposes Coercive Labor at household scale: they pursue Tomey's Turl as property even while blood, desire, and marriage bargains bind him to both plantations. Warwick supplies the mock-aristocratic stage on which Faulkner lets farce reveal the McCaslin inheritance.

THE UNVANQUISHED

Summary: This divider moves from early plantation origins to Civil War and Reconstruction material centered on the Sartoris clan and the wreckage around them. Cowley says the Chickasaws moved west in President Jackson's time, while a few remained under Francois Vidal or Frank Weddel, and he brings Sam Fathers forward as the survivor faithful to old customs. He then links Uncle Buck's marriage to Miss Sophonsiba, Uncle Buddy's service in Virginia, Colonel John Sartoris's demotion, Thomas Sutpen's command, and Sartoris's return to lead cavalry attached to Forrest. Source anchors: President Jackson; Frank Weddel; Sam Fathers; Miss Sophonsiba; John Sartoris; Forrest.

Analysis: President Jackson and Frank Weddel compress the Indian removal background before Cowley turns to John Sartoris and Forrest. Sam Fathers keeps the Chickasaw memory alive, while Miss Sophonsiba and Uncle Buddy carry the McCaslin comedy into wartime genealogy. The section prepares for stories where military honor, household survival, and plantation ruin are all tested outside the grand language of Confederate memory.

Editor's Note

Summary: The note identifies "Raid" and "An Odor of Verbena" as pieces from The Unvanquished and "Wash" as the story of Colonel Sutpen's death. Cowley describes Faulkner as romantic about the Sartorises, quotes the family name's fatal glamour, and then says "An Odor of Verbena" treats Colonel John Sartoris more harshly. He also points to Colonel William Falkner, the author's great-grandfather, as Sartoris's prototype: commander of the Second Mississippi Infantry, railroad builder, author of The White Rose of Memphis, and a man killed in a duel. Source anchors: The Unvanquished; An Odor of Verbena; Wash; John Sartoris; William Falkner; Second Mississippi Infantry.

Analysis: The Unvanquished and An Odor of Verbena tell the reader that Cowley is selecting from a connected Sartoris book, while Wash brings in Sutpen from a different but neighboring legend. John Sartoris and William Falkner matter because Cowley is showing how Faulkner transforms family history into fiction without letting the heroic pose go unexamined. The Second Mississippi Infantry detail keeps the romance attached to a real military and regional genealogy.

Raid

Summary: "Raid" follows Granny Millard, Bayard, and Ringo after the Sartoris house has burned and Loosh has gone off with the mules and silver. Granny writes with pokeberry juice, borrows Mrs. Compson's hat, parasol, and hand mirror, pins Colonel Nathaniel G. Dick's name inside her dress, and sets out in a wagon from Joby's cabin while Louvinia warns her not to waste time on colonels. The road toward Hawkhurst and the railroad is lined with burned houses, abandoned gins, white women and children in Black cabins, and groups of Black people moving at night toward the Union lines. Source anchors: Granny Millard; Ringo; pokeberry juice; Colonel Nathaniel G. Dick; Loosh; Hawkhurst.

Analysis: Granny Millard's pokeberry juice and Colonel Nathaniel G. Dick's name turn paperwork into a weapon for someone without soldiers. Ringo and Loosh give the journey its racial pressure: Ringo reads the road and the railroad as signs of movement, while Loosh's departure shows emancipation already happening beyond Granny's control. Hawkhurst and the burned houses make the raid a civilian ordeal rather than a cavalry episode.

Wash

Summary: "Wash" opens with Thomas Sutpen standing over Milly and her newborn child, comparing her unfavorably to a mare while thinking about Griselda's colt by Rob Roy. Wash Jones, a poor white squatter on Sutpen land, has spent years worshiping the Colonel's courage, drinking with him after the war, carrying him home from the store, and believing that Sutpen's touch will somehow make things right. The story moves through Vicksburg, Sherman, General Lee's citation, Judith Sutpen, the black stallion, Milly's pregnancy, Dicey the midwife, and the scythe leaning by the porch until Sutpen's contempt destroys Wash's illusion. Source anchors: Thomas Sutpen; Milly; Griselda; Wash Jones; General Lee; scythe.

Analysis: Thomas Sutpen, Milly, and Griselda make the story's cruelty exact: Sutpen values bloodstock more readily than the girl he has used. Wash Jones's faith in General Lee's decorated Colonel is the mechanism that lets him endure insult from Black workers, poverty, and postwar collapse until the scythe becomes the only answer left to him. Cowley places this after the Sartoris material so aristocratic charisma appears from below, through the eyes of a man it ruins.

Editor's Note

Summary: This note bridges Colonel Sartoris, Major Cassius de Spain, the Sutpen land, and the hunting world of "The Bear." The county has built Sartoris a marble monument in the Jefferson cemetery; Drusilla disappears from family records; young Bayard loses the railroad but becomes a Jefferson banker; and Major de Spain acquires much of Sutpen's hundred square miles through tax sales and mortgage foreclosures. De Spain remodels Wash Jones's shack into a hunting lodge where General Compson, McCaslin Edmonds, Isaac McCaslin, Sam Fathers, Boon Hogganbeck, and the bear Old Ben enter the anthology. Source anchors: marble monument; Major de Spain; Sutpen land; Isaac McCaslin; Sam Fathers; Old Ben.

Analysis: The marble monument and Major de Spain's purchase of Sutpen land show how violence becomes property and memory. Isaac McCaslin and Sam Fathers inherit the terrain as hunters rather than planters, while Old Ben gives the land a nonhuman center stronger than courthouse title. This editor's note is crucial because it changes Wash Jones's death site into the hunting camp where Faulkner will test whether renunciation can answer plantation history.

The Bear

Summary: "The Bear" follows Isaac McCaslin from childhood apprenticeship to the annual November hunt for Old Ben in the Big Bottom. Sam Fathers teaches Ike patience, humility, tracks, and the meaning of the warped two-toed print; Major de Spain, General Compson, McCaslin Edmonds, Walter Ewell, Tennie's Jim, Boon Hogganbeck, Lion, and the dogs form the hunting company. The story moves from the chase to the McCaslin ledgers, where Ike reads Carothers McCaslin's sexual exploitation and decides the land itself has been cursed by slavery, then returns to the killing of Old Ben and the later shrinking of the wilderness. Source anchors: Isaac McCaslin; Old Ben; Sam Fathers; Big Bottom; Carothers McCaslin; Lion.

Analysis: Isaac McCaslin, Sam Fathers, and Old Ben make the hunt an education in limits before it becomes an inheritance case. Big Bottom is not scenery; it is the place where Carothers McCaslin's ledger record can be felt as a wound in land and kinship. Lion's role in the kill proves that Old Ben is mortal, but that victory also begins the loss of the wilderness Ike believed had judged the plantation world.

THE PEASANTS

Summary: This divider introduces Frenchman's Bend several decades after the Civil War and turns from planters to the Snopes clan. Flem Snopes, son of a bushwhacker and horse thief, appears as a silent young man who neither drinks nor smokes and whose intentions remain hidden even from himself. Cowley says Flem gains control of the general store, cotton gin, and blacksmith shop, then installs Lump, I. O., Mink, Ike, Eck, and other Snopes relatives before moving toward Jefferson after even Ratliff is beaten. Source anchors: Frenchman's Bend; Flem Snopes; general store; cotton gin; Mink Snopes; Ratliff.

Analysis: Frenchman's Bend and Flem Snopes bring a different social engine into the anthology: not planter honor but acquisition without confession. The general store and cotton gin are local choke points, so Flem's control of them explains how Snopes power spreads through ordinary commerce. Mink Snopes and Ratliff show the range of that world, from violence to shrewd bargaining, before Cowley gives the reader the horse auction.

Editor's Note

Summary: Cowley identifies "Spotted Horses" as the first seed of The Hamlet and explains how Faulkner's Snopes material grew over roughly a dozen years. The note names "The Hound," "Lizards in Jamshyd's Courtyard," "Mule in the Yard," and "Brass" as related Snopes stories, and it explains that the sewing-machine agent Suratt became Ratliff when a real man of that name appeared at home. Cowley also places The Town and The Mansion as later volumes and calls the expanded Hamlet version of "Spotted Horses" a peak example of American backwoods humor after the Davy Crockett almanacs and Mark Twain. Source anchors: The Hamlet; Spotted Horses; Suratt; Ratliff; The Town; Mark Twain.

Analysis: The Hamlet, The Town, and Ratliff let Cowley present Snopes material as a long comic construction rather than a single joke. Suratt's change of name shows Faulkner adjusting fiction to local reality, and Mark Twain marks the older American comic line that "Spotted Horses" enters. The note matters because it tells the reader to take the auction's hilarity as serious social diagnosis.

Spotted Horses

Summary: "Spotted Horses" begins when Flem Snopes and Buck Hipps arrive at Varner's store with a wagon and a string of small, gaudy, dangerous Texas ponies tied with barbed wire. Quick, Freeman, Jody Varner, Eck Snopes, Mrs. Littlejohn's yard, Ratliff, and the men on the gallery watch the animals slash, rear, hit fences, and make the lot look too small for their violence. Hipps eats gingersnaps, claims the horses will gentle, and prepares the community for an auction in which appetite, pride, and Flem's silence do much of the cheating before anyone signs a paper. Source anchors: Flem Snopes; Buck Hipps; Texas ponies; barbed wire; Jody Varner; Ratliff.

Analysis: Flem Snopes and Buck Hipps divide the fraud between silence and performance: Flem withholds ownership while Hipps performs mastery over the Texas ponies. Barbed wire and Jody Varner's near injury make the goods visibly unusable, so Ratliff's warnings test whether Frenchman's Bend can resist wanting a bargain. The auction then violates the community's Moral Economy: Flem and Hipps exploit local expectations of usable goods and accountable sellers, while the lawsuits shift the resulting injuries back onto neighbors. Snopes capitalism looks comic until the fence, auction, and courthouse turn laughter into cost.

THE END OF AN ORDER

Summary: Cowley introduces this section by saying the old South in Faulkner is defeated from within after the landowners survive the Northern armies and carpetbaggers. Jefferson once has a Sartoris as mayor, a Benbow as county judge, and Major de Spain as local magnate, but Snopeses and their allies enter the bank, power company, and politics. The note contrasts that destructive spread with Black figures of endurance such as Elnora, Clytie, Dilsey, and Aunt Mollie Beauchamp, then points toward "That Evening Sun," "Ad Astra," "A Rose for Emily," and "Dilsey." Source anchors: Sartoris mayor; Benbow judge; Major de Spain; Snopeses; Dilsey; Aunt Mollie Beauchamp.

Analysis: Sartoris mayor, Benbow judge, and Major de Spain identify the public offices through which the old families briefly preserve authority. Snopeses in the bank and politics show that replacement happens through local institutions, while Dilsey and Aunt Mollie Beauchamp complicate Cowley's contrast by making endurance visible in kitchens and households. The section prepares a set of stories where public decline is measured through domestic labor and racial fear.

Editor's Note

Summary: The editor's note specifies the sources and family cycles behind the pieces in this section. "That Evening Sun" belongs to the Compson children cycle; "Ad Astra" follows Bayard Sartoris's twin grandsons in the Royal Air Force, where John is killed and Bayard returns from the Armistice feeling dead; "A Rose for Emily" is named as Faulkner's symbol of old-order decay; and "Dilsey" comes from the final part of The Sound and the Fury, Faulkner's own favorite among his novels. The note also directs the reader to the Compson appendix for the family's earlier history and survivors. Source anchors: That Evening Sun; Compson children; Ad Astra; Royal Air Force; The Sound and the Fury; Compson appendix.

Analysis: That Evening Sun and The Sound and the Fury put the Compson household at both ends of the section, first through children's perception and then through Dilsey's Easter labor. Ad Astra and the Royal Air Force move the Sartoris line into the First World War, so the section is not only about antebellum decline. The Compson appendix reference shows Cowley's editorial method again: he wants each excerpt to be read as part of a county record.

That Evening Sun

Summary: "That Evening Sun" is narrated by Quentin Compson looking back to a Jefferson where Monday laundry once meant Black women carrying sheet-wrapped bundles on their heads from white kitchens to cabins in Negro Hollow. Nancy, a washerwoman and occasional Compson cook, is threatened by Jesus after her pregnancy and after the public violence involving Mr. Stovall, the bank cashier and Baptist deacon who kicks out her teeth. Caddy, Jason, Quentin, Father, Mother, Dilsey, Jesus's razor scar, the jail window, cocaine, the cold kitchen stove, and Nancy's cabin all gather around the children's inability to grasp the danger that Nancy knows is waiting. Source anchors: Quentin Compson; Nancy; Jesus; Mr. Stovall; Negro Hollow; cold stove.

Analysis: Quentin Compson's retrospective voice and Nancy's fear make the story depend on what a child saw but did not understand. Jesus and Mr. Stovall are different threats, one domestic and one publicly respectable, and Negro Hollow marks the short distance between white household service and racial danger. The cold stove matters because Nancy's work in the Compson kitchen cannot protect her once Father walks the children away from her cabin.

Ad Astra

Summary: "Ad Astra" follows exhausted aviators after the Armistice as Sartoris drives toward Amiens with Comyn, Bland, the narrator, and a turbaned subadar who speaks about men moving in water and all men being brothers. Their drinking carries them from a road between beet fields to the Cloche-Clos, where Monaghan arrives with an American military policeman and a captured German with a bandaged head. The story's concrete world is British tunics, British wings, Oxford talk, R.F.C. caps, Amiens, French officers, whiskey bottles, the subadar's blocked commission, and Monaghan's claim that he brought the German down and will take him home. Source anchors: Sartoris; Amiens; subadar; Comyn; Monaghan; German prisoner.

Analysis: Sartoris, Comyn, and Monaghan show air-war masculinity after combat has supposedly ended, while the subadar and German prisoner expose the racial and national limits of the aviators' brotherhood talk. Amiens and the Cloche-Clos make peace feel like occupation by drunk survivors rather than a clean ending. The section belongs here because Cowley has moved the old Southern families into a European war where courage no longer supplies moral clarity.

Dilsey

Summary: "Dilsey" begins on a cold, wet Easter morning as Dilsey Gibson leaves her cabin in a black straw hat, maroon velvet cape, purple silk dress, army overcoat, and later a work apron to build the Compson kitchen fire. Mrs. Compson calls from the stairs for a hot water bottle, Luster is late after a show, Benjy must be dressed, Jason must not be disturbed, and Quentin's presence in the house adds another pressure. The section follows wood, biscuit flour, the stove, jaybirds in the mulberries, the back stairs, Luster's errands, and Dilsey's movement between her cabin and the rotting Compson house. Source anchors: Dilsey Gibson; Easter morning; Mrs. Compson; Luster; Benjy; kitchen fire.

Analysis: Dilsey Gibson and the kitchen fire make survival physical before it becomes spiritual. Mrs. Compson, Luster, and Benjy define the labor Dilsey must coordinate while the white family depends on her and complains about that dependence. Easter morning matters because Faulkner sets resurrection language against aching knees, wet wood, late breakfast, and a house whose order exists only because Dilsey keeps crossing the yard.

MISSISSIPPI FLOOD

Summary: Cowley's flood section turns from Yoknapatawpha to the 1927 Mississippi River disaster, when Parchman convicts are sent to threatened levees and a tall convict is ordered into a rowboat to find a woman in a cypress snag and a man on a cottonhouse ridgepole. The editor gives the scale: more than 20,000 square miles underwater for six weeks, 600,000 people driven from homes, hundreds drowned, huge losses of horses, cattle, hogs, sheep, chickens, crops, and levees. He then explains that "Old Man" was half of The Wild Palms and worked as counterpoint to the freedom-and-love plot of the other half. Source anchors: 1927 Mississippi flood; Parchman; cypress snag; cottonhouse; The Wild Palms; counterpoint.

Analysis: The 1927 Mississippi flood and Parchman allow Cowley to widen the anthology without abandoning Faulkner's concern with forced labor. The cypress snag and cottonhouse are the mission that will pull the tall convict into unwanted freedom, while The Wild Palms and counterpoint explain why the story alternates pressure with another plot in its original book. Cowley's statistics make the river historical before Faulkner makes it mythic.

Editor's Note

Summary: The editor's note says the tall convict comes from the pine hills southeast of Frenchman's Bend but that "Old Man" otherwise stands apart from the Yoknapatawpha saga. Cowley presents the Mississippi River as the title's "Old Man" and as the connection between the county and the rest of the South. He quotes Faulkner's Paris Review explanation to Jean Stein about alternating "Old Man" with The Wild Palms whenever the other story began to sag, then argues that the flood story gains by standing alone and can stand beside Huckleberry Finn as a Mississippi River tale. Source anchors: Frenchman's Bend; Old Man; Mississippi River; Jean Stein; Paris Review; Huckleberry Finn.

Analysis: Frenchman's Bend ties the tall convict lightly to the county, but Old Man and Mississippi River give the story a broader geography. Jean Stein and Paris Review let Cowley document Faulkner's compositional reason for the alternating structure, while Huckleberry Finn states the literary comparison Cowley wants the reader to risk. The note frames the river as a force large enough to change the anthology's scale.

Old Man

Summary: "Old Man" begins at the Mississippi State penal farm in May 1927 with a tall train-robber convict who blames Diamond Dick, Jesse James pamphlets, the Detectives' Gazette, and the third-class mails for teaching him a false method of robbery. A second, plump convict reads Memphis newspaper flood headlines about Cairo, Memphis, sandboils, Mound's Landing, Vicksburg, the National Guard, and Hoover while chained men work under guards and trusties. After the levee breaks, convicts travel by truck, train, launch, and skiff through water, refugee platforms, army tents, stew lines, and levee camps before the tall convict is ordered to paddle out for the woman and the man on the cottonhouse. Source anchors: State penal farm; Diamond Dick; Memphis newspaper; Mound's Landing; National Guard; skiff.

Analysis: State penal farm and National Guard make the flood a state operation as much as a natural event. Parchman joins cotton production to Coercive Labor, while the emergency lets officials redeploy prisoners to levees and rescue work without restoring choice or legal standing. Diamond Dick explains the tall convict's idea of honor, and the skiff assignment converts a man trained to obey armed guards into someone responsible for strangers after water washes ordinary roads, sentences, and gender rules loose.

MODERN TIMES

Summary: Cowley defines modern Yoknapatawpha as the period after the old slaveholding names have thinned out: Sartoris survives in a middle-aged woman and her son, Compson in an irascible bachelor, McCaslin in a childless old hunter, and Sutpen, Grenier, and Beauchamp largely in records or Black quarters. He says the old code has been replaced by "grab-and-git" and names Popeye from Sanctuary as the time's symbol. The section will include "Death Drag," Sanctuary's "Uncle Bud and the Three Madams," "Percy Grimm" from Light in August, and "Delta Autumn" from Go Down, Moses. Source anchors: Sartoris; Compson; McCaslin; Popeye; Death Drag; Delta Autumn.

Analysis: Sartoris, Compson, and McCaslin appear here as depleted names rather than ruling houses. Popeye gives Cowley a figure for modern predation, while Death Drag and Delta Autumn show different forms of the new time: machines and roads on one side, old hunters facing the ruined woods on the other. The divider works because it gathers aviation, fascistic policing, and late McCaslin reckoning under one historical pressure.

Death Drag

Summary: "Death Drag" opens with a black-painted airplane looping illegally over the town square and landing at a rough municipal field made from forty acres of ridge, gully, and former cotton land with an X-shaped runway. Two helmeted strangers, one tall and one short with a limp and a tragic face, ask about the town, the field, and whether any local man flew in the war; the boys mention Captain Warren of the Royal Flying Corps. A third stranger appears, the group discusses a paid show with wing-walking and a death-drag, and the town's small boys, fair association, taxi problem, barn, haycock, and Captain Warren's past all gather around the plane as a commercial machine of spectacle. Source anchors: black airplane; X-shaped runway; Captain Warren; Royal Flying Corps; wing-walking; death-drag.

Analysis: The black airplane and X-shaped runway put modern risk on land that used to grow cotton, linking machinery to the county's older ground. Captain Warren and the Royal Flying Corps connect Death Drag to Faulkner's war aviators, but wing-walking and death-drag turn combat skill into a paid local attraction. The story matters in this section because danger is no longer attached to honor or hunting; it is advertised, priced, and staged.

Percy Grimm

Summary: "Percy Grimm" traces a twenty-five-year-old Jefferson man whose life is organized by a missed war, the State National Guard, and a belief in white American military superiority. Too young for the European War, he fights an ex-soldier over France, embraces the civilian-military act, wears his captain's uniform on martial holidays, and forms a self-appointed platoon around the courthouse when Joe Christmas is brought back from Mottstown accused of killing Joanna Burden. The Legion commander, Sheriff Kennedy, Gavin Stevens, pistols, the Grand Jury, the fire alarm, Buford's shots, and the crowded square all lead Grimm from order talk to armed pursuit. Source anchors: Percy Grimm; State National Guard; Joe Christmas; Joanna Burden; Sheriff Kennedy; Grand Jury.

Analysis: Percy Grimm and the State National Guard show a hunger for command before there is a legitimate emergency. Joe Christmas and Joanna Burden supply the racial charge that lets Grimm convert civic procedure into military fantasy. His uniform, war envy, and claims of white American superiority form a Language and Ideology that recasts private racial violence as public duty, while Sheriff Kennedy and the Grand Jury mark the legal channels he overrides. Modern Jefferson's violence succeeds because it wears the vocabulary of order.

Delta Autumn

Summary: "Delta Autumn" returns old Isaac McCaslin to the Delta in cars, trucks, and a motor launch during the last week in November. He remembers wagons, bear, deer, wild turkey, General Compson, Major de Spain, the Big Bottom, Tallahatchie, Sunflower, Yazoo, and Indian-named towns while traveling with Roth Edmonds, Legate, and younger hunters through roads, neon, cotton fields, gins, and dwindling wilderness. Roth's affair with a light-skinned woman and their child forces Ike to confront the continuation of McCaslin blood across racial lines, even as he talks about Hitler, Roosevelt, Willkie, does, fawns, and what men fight to protect. Source anchors: Isaac McCaslin; Delta; Roth Edmonds; Legate; Tallahatchie; light-skinned woman.

Analysis: Isaac McCaslin and Delta bring "The Bear" into old age, after the wilderness has retreated two hundred miles from Jefferson. Roth Edmonds and the light-skinned woman make Ike's renunciation incomplete, because McCaslin inheritance has continued through desire, abandonment, and racial refusal. Legate's crude jokes and the Tallahatchie landscape keep the revelation from becoming symbolic only; it arrives during a wet hunting trip among men who still want deer.

THE UNDYING PAST

Summary: Cowley's last divider quotes Gavin Stevens from Requiem for a Nun: the past is never dead and is not even past. The note says this judgment often seems to speak for Faulkner, whose later work tries to recreate the full past of Yoknapatawpha County with increasing ambition. It singles out "The Jail," a narrative prologue to the last act of Requiem for a Nun, as the closest fulfillment of that ambition and prepares the reader for a civic object that remembers beyond church registers and courthouse records. Source anchors: Gavin Stevens; Requiem for a Nun; past is never dead; Yoknapatawpha County; The Jail; courthouse records.

Analysis: Gavin Stevens and Requiem for a Nun give Cowley a direct statement of Faulkner's historical method. Yoknapatawpha County and courthouse records matter because the last selection asks whether official archives can hold what the jail walls hold. The Jail is therefore a closing device: it changes the anthology from a sequence of family stories into a meditation on where communal memory is stored.

The Jail (Nor Even Yet Quite Relinquish--)

Summary: "The Jail" says the jail is older in memory than the courthouse and has seen Jefferson mutate from halting-place to community, settlement, village, and town. Gavin Stevens claims the walls preserve a better continuous history than church registers or courthouse records because prisoners inscribe lusts, yearnings, doggerel, and sexual drawings beneath calcimine, creosote, and whitewash. The passage then recalls Pettigrew's pony express, Jason Compson's horse trade with Ikkemotubbe, Doctor Habersham's black bag, Mohataha's wagon, the Federal land agent, Ratcliffe's store, Holston's tavern, the purple silk dress, the parasol, and Mohataha's final westward departure after making her X on the paper. Source anchors: jail walls; Gavin Stevens; calcimine; Ikkemotubbe; Mohataha; Federal land agent.

Analysis: Jail walls and calcimine make memory layered, physical, and partly obscene rather than cleanly official. Scratched names, repeated whitewash, and remembered prisoners turn the building into Knowledge Preservation outside the courthouse archive. Gavin Stevens supplies the interpretive claim, but Ikkemotubbe, Mohataha, and the Federal land agent supply the historical wound the walls cannot erase: dispossession becomes a scene in front of Ratcliffe's store and Holston's tavern. The anthology therefore ends with the stubborn survival of what official papers try to settle.
