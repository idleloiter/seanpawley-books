Detailed overview

Faulkner builds The Unvanquished as Bayard Sartoris's retrospective account of how the Civil War and Reconstruction enter the Sartoris household first as rumor, play, and family legend, then as burned property, fraud, killing, and a refusal of inherited vengeance. The opening image behind the smokehouse, where Bayard and Ringo make a miniature Vicksburg from chips, dust, a hoe-scraped river, and water hauled from the well, gives the book its method: public events arrive through local objects and household relations. Loosh's knowledge of Corinth and Vicksburg, John Sartoris's return on Jupiter, Granny's silver, Louvinia's kitchen, and the hidden stock pen all turn military history into pressure on the plantation's daily arrangements.

The first three chapters move from boyish Confederate romance into a damaged landscape of retreating armies, fleeing enslaved people, burned houses, and improvised authority. Bayard and Ringo begin by imagining General Pemberton and Grant as roles in a dust game, but they soon watch Loosh use Union victory as a claim on himself, Philadelphy, and the buried silver. Granny Rosa Millard responds to catastrophe with procedure: she borrows horses, insists on Mrs Compson's hat and parasol, remembers Colonel Nathaniel G. Dick, and uses Yankee paperwork to recover more property than she lost. The comedy of the 110 mules and the official order dated August 14, 1863 depends on exact bureaucracy, but the comedy is inseparable from enslaved people seeking exit and from Granny's guilty insistence that the family has lied.

The middle of the book turns Granny's improvisation into a system and then shows why the system cannot survive its own success. Ringo's copied United States Forces, Department of Tennessee letterheads, his window-shade map, the purple pokeberry circles marked Complete, Ab Snopes's sales trips to Memphis, and the burned-out U.S. brands on the mules make a wartime economy out of forgery, logistics, and nerve. Granny treats the operation as restitution and charity for widows, children, and displaced dependents, but the book keeps the moral accounting visible: she confesses theft and false witness in church, yet continues because Ab Snopes can still tempt her with Grumby's stolen horses and the hope of money for John Sartoris's return.

Granny's death at the abandoned cotton compress changes Bayard's story from comic survival to revenge pursuit. The rain, the Tallahatchie road, Ab Snopes's betrayal, Grumby's Independents, Uncle Buck McCaslin's pistol, the notched pine stick, the murdered black man carrying Grumby's warning, and the bearded Matt Bowden all make vengeance a practical hunt through a county no larger than a coin on a map. Bayard and Ringo kill Grumby and nail his severed hand to Granny's grave, but Ringo's judgment that "it was them mules" that killed her keeps the death tied to the earlier machinery of restitution, paperwork, livestock, and war profiteering rather than to one villain alone.

The last two chapters widen the violence into postwar politics and private inheritance. "Skirmish at Sartoris" places Drusilla, Aunt Louisa, Mrs Habersham, Cassius Q. Benbow, the Burdens from Missouri, the carpetbagger voting box, and John Sartoris's former troop inside the same rebuilt Jefferson where the courthouse and stores are going up again. "An Odor of Verbena" then tests Bayard against the honor code that Drusilla, George Wyatt, and the Sartoris men expect him to obey after B. J. Redmond kills John Sartoris. Bayard walks into Redmond's office unarmed and lets Redmond fire twice; the refusal to kill becomes the book's final break from the pattern that made Grumby's death feel necessary.

The book's narrative logic is therefore not a simple celebration of being undefeated. Its title is repeatedly tested against defeat: Vicksburg falls, the Sartoris house burns, Loosh leaves, Granny dies, the Confederate cause is lost, and John Sartoris is murdered after building his postwar power. What remains "unvanquished" is more unstable: Granny's will to care for people through illegal means, Ringo's tactical intelligence under slavery and after emancipation, Drusilla's refusal of ornamental womanhood, and Bayard's final decision that the Sartoris name need not require another killing. Faulkner lets each survival carry damage with it, so that endurance is never cleanly separated from coercion, pride, comedy, and grief.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Ambuscade

Summary: Bayard remembers the summer when he and Ringo, both twelve, make Vicksburg from chips behind the smokehouse and treat the River as a trench in the dirt until Loosh sweeps the model flat and says Corinth is "on the way." John Sartoris rides home from Tennessee on Jupiter, hides the livestock in a new creek-bottom pen with Joby, Loosh, Ringo, and Bayard, has Granny's silver buried in the orchard, and tells the household enough for Bayard to understand that Vicksburg has fallen. Bayard and Ringo spy on Loosh after a dream-warning, follow his night ride toward Corinth, and later see Union soldiers at the gate while they shoot one horse from ambush and run back to the house. The chapter ends with Colonel Nathaniel G. Dick entering the house, finding the children hidden under Granny's skirt, and sparing them after Granny's furious performance of helpless gentility. Source anchors: smokehouse Vicksburg; Loosh; Corinth; Jupiter; creek-bottom pen; Colonel Dick.

Analysis: The smokehouse Vicksburg, Loosh, Corinth, Jupiter, the creek-bottom pen, and Colonel Dick give the chapter its exact conversion of war news into household crisis. Loosh knows Union movement before Bayard can admit it, so his destruction of the dirt Vicksburg is not a childish insult but the first sign that enslaved people on the Sartoris place have their own intelligence network and their own expectation of liberation. John Sartoris's work on the creek-bottom pen and the buried silver makes him heroic to Bayard while also showing how little military rank can protect the farm. Colonel Dick's scene with Granny establishes the book's recurring weapon: paperwork, manners, and gendered assumptions can delay armed men when guns cannot.

Retreat

Summary: Granny prepares to leave for Memphis with Bayard, Ringo, Joby, the wagon, the musket, Mrs Compson's rose cuttings, and the silver trunk, even though Louvinia warns her that moving the trunk will expose it. In Jefferson, Uncle Buck McCaslin explains the McCaslin household, the McCaslin relationship to land, and John Sartoris's demotion and irregular cavalry before Bayard and Ringo continue with Granny toward Memphis. Union raiders steal Old Hundred and Tinney from the wagon; Bayard and Ringo borrow an old white horse, lose Granny, are found by John Sartoris, and help him bluff about sixty Yankees out of their arms, clothes, boots, and blankets near a bridge. When the family returns to Sartoris, Loosh reveals the buried silver to Union troops, the house is burned, and he leaves with Philadelphy, saying he belongs to himself and God. Source anchors: Memphis; silver trunk; Uncle Buck McCaslin; Old Hundred; Tinney; Loosh and Philadelphy.

Analysis: Memphis, the silver trunk, Uncle Buck McCaslin, Old Hundred, Tinney, and Loosh and Philadelphy make "Retreat" a chapter about property in motion. Granny thinks she can preserve the Sartoris household by carrying the silver trunk, but the trip exposes how livestock, silver, weapons, and people are all being detached from old claims. Uncle Buck McCaslin's comic history of land and obligation gives a counterexample to Sartoris ownership, while Old Hundred and Tinney become practical measures of loss and recovery. Loosh and Philadelphy leaving after the house burns turns the Confederate retreat into emancipation as exit inside the family yard, and Granny's question about the silver cannot answer Loosh's question about who buried whom in darkness.

Raid

Summary: Granny, Bayard, and Ringo set out from Joby's cabin after the house burns, carrying a note written in pokeberry juice, Mrs Compson's hat, the parasol, a hand mirror, and the name Colonel Nathaniel G. Dick pinned inside Granny's dress. Their road to Hawkhurst passes burned houses, abandoned gins, women and children living in cabins, and groups of black refugees moving at night toward the Yankee army and the river, including a sick woman with a baby who says Jesus will get her to Jordan. At Hawkhurst, Cousin Denny shows Ringo the wrecked railroad, with burned ties and rails twisted around trees, and Cousin Drusilla appears on Bobolink after protecting the horse from Union soldiers. At the river, Drusilla tries to turn the wagon as Union cavalry holds back refugees, the bridge is blown, the wagon is swept into the water, and Granny later reaches Colonel Dick's camp, where an official order gives her ten chests, 110 mules, and 110 black people by mistake. Source anchors: pokeberry juice; Hawkhurst; Jordan; twisted rails; Drusilla; 110 mules.

Analysis: Pokeberry juice, Hawkhurst, Jordan, twisted rails, Drusilla, and 110 mules connect the chapter's comedy to displacement and military destruction. Granny's handwritten memory aid for Colonel Dick looks small beside the refugee columns, but it becomes powerful once Union clerks translate her claim into an order. Hawkhurst and the twisted rails give Ringo his first railroad only as wreckage, so technological wonder arrives already ruined. Drusilla on Bobolink offers Bayard another model of courage outside the parlor, while the 110 mules turn a near-drowning among refugees into a throughput problem of animals, bridges, orders, and army movement.

Riposte in Tertio

Summary: Granny and Ringo develop the accidental Colonel Dick order into a mule-recovery business, using United States Forces, Department of Tennessee letterhead, copied signatures, a window-shade map, purple pokeberry circles marked Complete, and Ab Snopes as the seller who takes batches to Memphis. Ab reports $450 for nine mules while boasting that the operation has moved 105 mules for $6,722.65, and Ringo scouts Colonel G. W. Newberry's Illinois Infantry near Mottstown for another nineteen head. The Newberry raid nearly fails because orders have circulated to watch for Granny under false names like Plurella Harris, but Ringo distracts the soldiers long enough to save the mule train. A Federal lieutenant later finds the Sartoris pen, collects the forged orders, recovers the marked mules, pays Granny a ten-dollar fence voucher, and leaves her to confess in church that she has stolen and borne false witness for food, clothes, widows, children, and dependents. Source anchors: Department of Tennessee; window-shade map; Ab Snopes; Plurella Harris; ten-dollar voucher; church confession.

Analysis: Department of Tennessee letterhead, the window-shade map, Ab Snopes, Plurella Harris, the ten-dollar voucher, and the church confession show Granny's operation becoming both more efficient and more exposed. Ringo supplies the technical skill: scouting regiments, copying orders, choosing signatures, and tracking towns on the map. Ab Snopes supplies the market and the corruption, because the same man who sells the mules back to the Yankees can sell Granny's location. The ten-dollar voucher is comic humiliation, but the church confession makes the moral economy explicit: Granny knows the difference between restitution and theft even while she insists that hungry people around Jefferson forced her to keep working.

Vendée

Summary: After Granny is killed at Grumby's abandoned cotton compress on the Tallahatchie road, Brother Fortinbride buries her in the rain while hill people, Jefferson people, black families, Uncle Buck McCaslin, Bayard, and Ringo gather around the grave. Bayard asks Uncle Buck for a pistol, and the three begin hunting Grumby and Ab Snopes through wet roads, pine bottoms, cabins, hidden pens, and the trail of Grumby's Independents. They find Ab Snopes tied to a sapling after Grumby's men discard him, whip him with galluses and bridle reins, and continue after Grumby with Uncle Buck's pistol after Uncle Buck falls sick. Matt Bowden and Bridger finally deliver Grumby to Bayard and Ringo; Grumby tries to trick them with his pistol, Bayard shoots him, Ringo attacks with a pocket knife, and the boys nail Grumby's severed hand to Granny's grave before returning to find John Sartoris and Drusilla home. Source anchors: Brother Fortinbride; Tallahatchie; Uncle Buck; Ab Snopes; Matt Bowden; Grumby's hand.

Analysis: Brother Fortinbride, Tallahatchie, Uncle Buck, Ab Snopes, Matt Bowden, and Grumby's hand make "Vendée" a revenge chapter that keeps narrowing the question of guilt. Brother Fortinbride's funeral refuses ornamental consolation and sends people back to the work Granny had enabled. Uncle Buck gives Bayard adult help but also presses him to ask what touching Granny's killer will actually mean. Ab Snopes is punished as the betrayer who led Granny toward Tallahatchie, but Matt Bowden's delivery of Grumby reveals that Grumby is despised even by his own gang because he killed an old woman out of fear. Grumby's hand on the grave gives Bayard and Ringo proof, yet Ringo's later claim that the mules killed Granny denies the revenge any simple moral finish.

Skirmish at Sartoris

Summary: After the war, Bayard recalls John Sartoris's old troop facing Aunt Louisa, Mrs Habersham, Mrs Compson, and the other Jefferson women across a carpetbagger voting box while Drusilla stands beside John. The chapter reconstructs Aunt Louisa's letters from Hawkhurst, written on wallpaper with pokeberry juice, accusing Drusilla of refusing the role of Gavin Breckbridge's bride-widow after Shiloh and of riding in John Sartoris's troop in male clothes. Mrs Habersham and the Jefferson women inspect the cabin, Drusilla's bed, Father and Bayard's pallet, the sawmill, Joby, Ringo, and Drusilla's overalls, then force the issue of marriage by treating Drusilla's condition as sexual disgrace. Reconstruction politics enter through Cassius Q. Benbow's scrip dollar, the Burdens from Missouri, black Republican organization, and John Sartoris's effort to stop the election by bringing his mounted men to the voting place. Source anchors: carpetbagger voting box; Aunt Louisa; Gavin Breckbridge; Mrs Habersham; Cassius Q. Benbow; Burdens from Missouri.

Analysis: The carpetbagger voting box, Aunt Louisa, Gavin Breckbridge, Mrs Habersham, Cassius Q. Benbow, and the Burdens from Missouri let Faulkner join family coercion to public coercion. Aunt Louisa uses Gavin Breckbridge's death at Shiloh to demand that Drusilla become a symbolic widow rather than a soldier, while Mrs Habersham turns the cabin and sawmill into evidence against Drusilla's body. Cassius Q. Benbow and the Burdens from Missouri show that emancipation has become electoral power in Jefferson, which John Sartoris meets with ex-Confederate organization and intimidation. The chapter matters because Drusilla is trapped between two victorious informal institutions: the women who demand marriage and the men who turn military habit into postwar rule.

An Odor of Verbena

Summary: Bayard, now at the University, learns that B. J. Redmond has shot John Sartoris, and he returns home to Aunt Jenny, Drusilla, George Wyatt, Ringo, Louvinia, and the house prepared for mourning. Drusilla places verbena in Bayard's lapel, gives him the derringer from the Sartoris code of retaliation, and expects him to kill Redmond, while Aunt Jenny speaks of blockade runners and the phrase "No bloody moon." In Jefferson, George Wyatt and former members of John's troop try to arm Bayard, but Bayard walks unarmed into Redmond's law office under the sign B. J. Redmond, Atty at Law. Redmond fires twice and misses; Bayard does not shoot, Redmond leaves by train, George Wyatt understands what Bayard has done, and Bayard returns home to learn that Drusilla has gone to Montgomery, leaving one sprig of verbena on his pillow. Source anchors: B. J. Redmond; Aunt Jenny; verbena; derringer; George Wyatt; Montgomery.

Analysis: B. J. Redmond, Aunt Jenny, verbena, the derringer, George Wyatt, and Montgomery make the final chapter a test of whether Bayard will keep repeating Sartoris violence. Drusilla's verbena and derringer try to turn Bayard into John's avenger, while George Wyatt represents the troop's simpler expectation that a Sartoris must answer a killing with a killing. Aunt Jenny's "No bloody moon" gives Bayard a different language for refusing theatrical courage. Redmond's two missed shots matter because Bayard wins by letting the vengeance ritual exhaust itself; Drusilla's departure for Montgomery shows that his refusal breaks her image of him, but the verbena on the pillow keeps her judgment and loss inside the room.
