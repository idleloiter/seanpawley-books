Detailed overview

Andrew Roberts writes Napoleon against two distortions: the post-1940 habit of reading him backward through Hitler, and the older caricature of a Corsican adventurer whose ruin came from simple megalomania. The introduction opens at Les Invalides on June 23, 1940, with Hitler visiting Napoleon's tomb, then counters that image through Churchill's refusal to equate the two men and through Roberts's own archive work with the Correspondance Generale, St Helena testimony, memoirists, physical relics, and Napoleon's surviving letters. The book's method is not to absolve Napoleon of executions, censorship, dynastic coercion, or catastrophic war, but to test each received accusation against named decisions, dates, offices, battlefields, and documentary traces.

The first large movement makes Napoleon's rise a product of Corsican family pressure, French military schooling, revolutionary contingency, and personal appetite for information. Ajaccio, Letizia, Carlo Buonaparte, Pasquale Paoli, Brienne, Valence, the Regiment de la Fere, and the failed mulberry nursery matter because Roberts treats Napoleon's ambition as a family rescue project before it becomes a European one. The young artillery officer reads Voltaire, Machiavelli, Rollin, Arab history, Venetian history, English history, and classical drama with the same acquisitive intensity that later appears in his questions to mayors, ministers, engineers, and marshals.

The Revolution gives that intense technician a career ladder. Toulon, 13 Vendemiaire, Josephine, the Army of Italy, Lodi, Arcole, Campo Formio, Egypt, the Pyramids, Jaffa, Acre, and Brumaire are handled as a sequence of practical tests: artillery placement, political nerve, newspaper management, battlefield timing, patronage, and the ability to turn victory into legitimacy. Roberts does not present the coup of Brumaire as an accidental ending to the Revolution; he shows Napoleon, Lucien, Sieyes, Ducos, Cambaceres, and Daunou converting exhausted republican institutions into a system in which the First Consul could command ministers, ambassadors, local administrators, armies, and constitutional language at once.

The middle of the biography is most interested in how conquest and administration reinforced each other. The Constitution of the Year VIII, the Conseil d'Etat, the Bank of France, the Code Napoleon, the Concordat, the Legion d'Honneur, the Tuileries, the coronation at Notre Dame, and the imperial court are not ornamental scenery around campaigns; they are the machinery by which Napoleon tried to make victory durable. At the same time, Austerlitz, Jena, Friedland, Tilsit, the corps system, Berthier's filing practices, and the Grande Armee show why military command became the strongest language of his rule and why Europe had to keep organizing coalitions around the question of whether one state could impose administrative unity by repeated operational shock.

Roberts makes the fall begin before Moscow, in the economic logic of the Continental System and the diplomatic damage around Russia, Oldenburg, the Hanseatic Towns, Sweden, Turkey, and the Duchy of Warsaw. The invasion of 1812 is therefore not treated as an irrational desire to possess Moscow; it emerges from Napoleon's attempt to force Alexander back into a commercial war against Britain while still managing Spain, Austria, Prussia, Poland, and the Ottoman flank. The huge army crossing the Niemen is multinational, horse-dependent, wagon-bound, and built for a short decision that Barclay de Tolly and Kutuzov refuse to offer.

The closing sections are strongest when they keep Napoleon's administrative reflexes visible inside defeat. At the Berezina, Eble's Dutch pontonniers, Oudinot's feint at Studzianka, Victor's stand against Wittgenstein, Ney's rearguard, and Napoleon's cold riverside supervision turn a disaster into a partial escape; at Dresden and Leipzig, Roberts shows tactical skill surviving inside a strategic position ruined by Austria, Prussia, Russia, Sweden, Bavaria, Wellington, exhausted cavalry, young conscripts, and unreliable subordinates. Elba, the Hundred Days, Waterloo, Longwood, Hudson Lowe, Las Cases, Marchand, Montholon, Bertrand, and the deathbed return to Ajaccio property make the final Napoleon both prisoner and editor of his own legend.

Section-by-section notes

Introduction

Summary: Roberts opens by confronting the twentieth-century image of Napoleon through Hitler's visit to Les Invalides on June 23, 1940, Churchill's rejection of the Napoleon-Hitler comparison, Pieter Geyl's Buchenwald lectures later published as Napoleon: For and Against, and Claude Ribbe's claim that Napoleon anticipated Nazi racial crimes. He explains that his own childhood skepticism hardened as he noticed evidence that did not fit the tyrant caricature: Napoleon's humor, his refusal to punish many betrayers, the number of wars declared on France by coalitions, liberal British visitors to Elba and St Helena, and the continued fascination of opponents who had seen him close up. Roberts also stresses the materials behind the biography, including more than 1,500 memoirists, the Correspondance Generale, a lock of hair, Longwood wallpaper, a condolence letter after the Battle of the Nile, and Consulate medals. Source anchors: Les Invalides, Churchill, Pieter Geyl, Buchenwald, St Helena, Longwood wallpaper, Battle of the Nile.

Analysis: Les Invalides and Churchill let Roberts define the book against a precise historical comparison rather than a vague reputation problem: the question is why Hitler wanted Napoleon's symbolic capital and why Churchill, facing Hitler, still called Napoleon a great man of action. Pieter Geyl and Buchenwald show that hostile interpretations were themselves made under coercive twentieth-century conditions, which gives Roberts a reason to reopen evidence without pretending Napoleon was harmless. St Helena, Longwood wallpaper, and the Battle of the Nile letter announce the biography's evidentiary habit: Roberts wants the reader to move between grand claims about Europe and small surviving objects that test whether the claims fit Napoleon's recorded conduct.

Part One: Rise

Summary: The rise section begins with Napoleone di Buonaparte's birth in Ajaccio on August 15, 1769, just after France acquired Corsica, and follows a family caught between Carlo Buonaparte's ambitions, Letizia's severity, Pasquale Paoli's Corsican nationalism, and French patronage. Roberts tracks the boy through Brienne, the Ecole Militaire, the Regiment de la Fere at Valence, his father's death at Montpellier, the mulberry nursery debt, and his habit of living cheaply so he could buy and annotate books. The French Revolution then supplies opportunities and dangers: Toulon turns artillery competence into public reputation, 13 Vendemiaire makes him useful to Barras, Josephine links him to Parisian power, the Italian campaign gives him Lodi, Arcole, and Campo Formio, Egypt gives him the Pyramids and Jaffa, Acre checks eastern fantasies, and Brumaire converts military prestige into rule. Source anchors: Ajaccio, Letizia, Pasquale Paoli, Valence, Toulon, Josephine, Arcole, Brumaire.

Analysis: Ajaccio, Letizia, and Pasquale Paoli make Napoleon's Frenchness a problem of National Identity acquired through Corsican defeat, family strategy, schooling, and shifting allegiance. Valence and Toulon connect reading, artillery, and revolutionary patronage to Elite Formation through technical competence and political sponsorship. Josephine, Arcole, and Brumaire show battlefield performance, social access, propaganda, and institutional weakness being converted into personal authority.

Part Two: Mastery

Summary: The mastery section begins after Brumaire with Napoleon in the Luxembourg Palace, outmaneuvering Sieyes, Ducos, Lucien, Boulay de la Meurthe, Daunou, and Cambaceres while building the Constitution of the Year VIII around the First Consul. Roberts then follows the governing machinery: the Conseil d'Etat, Talleyrand at foreign affairs, Fouche at police, Gaudin at finance, the Bank of France, the Tuileries, the Code Napoleon, the Concordat, the Legion d'Honneur, and the plebiscitary language used to present command as national consent. Military mastery is handled through Marengo, Trafalgar, the coronation at Notre Dame, Austerlitz, Jena, Eylau, Friedland, Tilsit, the Continental System, Iberia, Wagram, Marie Louise, and the King of Rome, with the corps system and Berthier's staff work explaining how Napoleon moved large armies faster than coalition opponents expected. Source anchors: Constitution of the Year VIII, Conseil d'Etat, Bank of France, Code Napoleon, Austerlitz, Tilsit, Continental System, Wagram.

Analysis: The Constitution of the Year VIII, Conseil d'Etat, Bank of France, and Code Napoleon create State Capacity through administrative compression of appointments, law, finance, and local government. Austerlitz and Tilsit show the machine reordering Europe, but the Continental System converts victory into an enforcement burden against Britain, Russia, smuggling, and neutral shipping. Wagram and Marie Louise add Legitimacy and Succession through dynastic marriage while making authority dependent on coercion and continued military success.

Part Three: Denouement

Summary: The denouement starts with Russia because Roberts treats 1812 as the collision between Napoleon's commercial war against Britain and Alexander's need to reopen trade through the ukaz, Oldenburg, the Hanseatic Towns, and the fraying memory of Tilsit and Erfurt. Napoleon prepares a multinational Grande Armee of more than 600,000 men, crosses the Niemen on June 24, 1812, expects a short campaign, and instead meets Barclay de Tolly's withdrawal, Bagration's survival, Kutuzov's refusal to be trapped, Borodino's slaughter, Moscow's fires, Smolensk's empty stores, Krasnoi, and the freezing retreat. The section then follows Eble's pontonniers at the Berezina, Oudinot's feint at Studzianka, Victor against Wittgenstein, Ney's reappearance with only hundreds from his corps, the 29th Bulletin, Dresden, Leipzig, Bavaria's defection, Wellington crossing into France, Fontainebleau, Elba, L'Inconstant, Waterloo, Longwood House, Hudson Lowe, Las Cases, Montholon, Bertrand, Marchand, and Napoleon's death on May 5, 1821. Source anchors: Oldenburg, Niemen, Barclay de Tolly, Borodino, Berezina, Leipzig, Elba, Longwood House.

Analysis: Oldenburg and the Niemen make Russia a policy failure before weather catastrophe. The Grande Armee depends on Logistics and Throughput through forage, horses, roads, depots, and allied loyalty while trying to enforce the Continental System. Barclay's withdrawal denies decisive battle, Borodino consumes strength without settlement, and Eble's bridges preserve remnants at Berezina. Leipzig, Elba, and Longwood turn War, Crisis, and Collapse into coalition defeat, political return, and captivity narrative.

Epilogue, Notes, Bibliography, and Index

Summary: The epilogue tracks what Napoleon's defeat did to the people and institutions that survived him: Tsar Alexander becomes Europe's strongest monarch, Metternich manages the Vienna settlement, Francis I, Frederick William III, Pius VII, Ferdinand VII, Louis XVIII, Charles X, and Louis-Philippe each inherit parts of the post-Napoleonic order, and Napoleon III later revives the imperial name. Roberts follows the Bonaparte family through Madame Mere in Rome, Cardinal Fesch's collection, Joseph in Bordentown, Jerome at Les Invalides, Pauline in Florence, Marie Louise with Neipperg, the Duke of Reichstadt at Schonbrunn, Alexandre Walewski in diplomacy, and Count Leon's wasted resemblance to his father. The reference apparatus then underscores scale: Napoleon's 33,000 letters in the Correspondance Generale, the massive bibliography, the index of marshals, ministers, mistresses, opponents, battles, institutions, and the recurrent use of memoir evidence from Marchand, Las Cases, Caulaincourt, Bausset, Fain, Bourrienne, and others. Source anchors: Tsar Alexander, Metternich, Madame Mere, Bordentown, Marie Louise, Duke of Reichstadt, Correspondance Generale, Las Cases.

Analysis: Tsar Alexander and Metternich test Path Dependence and Institutional Drift after 1815: restored powers suppress liberal and national movements, yet the Bourbons cannot remove Napoleonic law and administration embedded in France. Madame Mere, Bordentown, Marie Louise, and the Duke of Reichstadt show failed dynastic succession. Correspondance Generale and Las Cases create Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation through a contest between administrative records and Napoleon's crafted captivity story.
