Detailed overview

Brzezinski writes from Washington in April 1997, after the Soviet collapse and before NATO's first post-Cold War enlargement, to turn American victory into a program for Eurasia. The introduction defines Eurasia as the "chessboard" because Europe, Russia, Central Asia, the Middle East, China, Japan, India, and the Korean peninsula contain the chief possible challengers to American primacy. The book's immediate task is to prevent a hostile Eurasian power or coalition from excluding the United States from that continent; its longer task is to convert American preeminence into a more institutionalized structure of cooperation.

The first chapter explains why American primacy is new even though hegemony is not. Brzezinski compares the United States with Rome, imperial China, the Mongol Empire, Spain, France, and Great Britain, but he insists that none of them combined American reach across all oceans, control of both Eurasian extremities, technological leadership, economic scale, and mass-cultural appeal. He treats the Spanish-American War, the Panama Canal, Woodrow Wilson's Fourteen Points, World War II, the Berlin blockade, the Korean War, Afghanistan, the Persian Gulf, NATO, APEC, NAFTA, the IMF, the World Bank, and Bretton Woods as steps in the creation of an American-centered global system that works less by formal annexation than by alliances, markets, co-opted elites, and institutions.

The middle of the book maps the Eurasian board by separating "geostrategic players" from "geopolitical pivots." France, Germany, Russia, China, and India are players because they can alter the regional distribution of power; Ukraine, Azerbaijan, South Korea, Turkey, and Iran are pivots because their location and vulnerability shape the choices of stronger states. That distinction controls the book's later chapters: Western Europe is the democratic bridgehead, Russia is the postimperial "black hole," the Caucasus and Central Asia are the Eurasian Balkans, and East Asia must be stabilized through a triangular relationship among America, China, and Japan.

Europe receives a deliberately double treatment. Brzezinski wants a larger, more politically coherent Europe because it deepens America's bridgehead on the Eurasian mainland, but he also wants that Europe anchored in NATO and the Atlantic relationship. France seeks "grandeur" through a Europe that can reduce American predominance; Germany seeks redemption and security through Europe plus America. The practical prescription is therefore not a choice between Paris and Berlin, but a widened EU and NATO, a managed Franco-German partnership, support for Poland, Hungary, the Czech Republic, the Baltic states, and eventually Ukraine, and refusal to let Moscow veto qualified European states.

Russia is the book's most consequential warning case. The 1991 Soviet collapse creates a central Eurasian void: Russia loses Ukraine's 52 million people, Odessa, Black Sea predominance, the Baltic ports of Riga and Tallinn, the Caspian monopoly, and direct imperial control over the Caucasus and Central Asia. Brzezinski rejects a U.S.-Russian condominium, a Russian reintegration of the "near abroad," and an antihegemonic axis with China and Iran. His preferred route is a postimperial Russia tied to transatlantic Europe, but that requires Moscow to accept Ukraine's separate identity, Azerbaijan's corridor to the Caspian, Uzbekistan's independence, and NATO/EU enlargement as facts rather than humiliations.

The chapters on the Eurasian Balkans and the Far East show how energy, nationalism, and alliance management turn the map into policy. Azerbaijan is the cork in the Caspian bottle; Uzbekistan is the Central Asian state most able to resist renewed Russian control; Kazakstan is a vast shield split between Kazaks and Russians; Turkey and Iran are both pivots and contestants; pipelines through Georgia, Turkey, Iran, Afghanistan, or Pakistan matter because they decide whether Moscow monopolizes access. In East Asia, Brzezinski argues that China should be treated as a regional power to be accommodated, Japan as America's global partner rather than an anti-Chinese military proxy, Taiwan as a dangerous test of American credibility, and Korea as the pivot that links China, Japan, and U.S. forces.

The conclusion gathers these regional prescriptions into a staged American geostrategy. In the short run, Washington must preserve Eurasian pluralism and prevent any coalition of China, Russia, Iran, Germany, France, Japan, or other powers from forcing America off the continent. In the middle term, it should cultivate strategic partners: an enlarged Europe, a Europe-oriented Russia, a regionally preeminent but internationally embedded China, a globally active Japan, and a stabilizing India. In the long run, Brzezinski imagines a Trans-Eurasian Security System linking expanded NATO, Russia, China, Japan, India, and Europe, so that the United States leaves behind not another national empire but a cooperative order built during the narrow window of its post-Cold War supremacy.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Introduction: Superpower Politics

Summary: Brzezinski opens by locating five hundred years of world power in Eurasia, then naming the late-twentieth-century anomaly: the United States, a Western Hemisphere state, has become the first non-Eurasian arbiter of Eurasian power. He treats Europe as the western periphery, Asia as the eastern economic center, and Eurasia as the place where a dominant hostile challenger could still arise. The November 1940 Hitler-Stalin discussions, the defeat of the Soviet Union, American technology, communications, finance, trade, and the term geostrategy all appear as evidence that American primacy depends on managing the Eurasian landmass. Source anchors: Eurasia; Western Hemisphere; Soviet Union; Hitler; Stalin; November 1940; geostrategy.

Analysis: Eurasia and the Western Hemisphere set up the book's asymmetry: the world's central arena is being managed from outside it. Hitler, Stalin, and November 1940 let Brzezinski anchor his claim in a prior nightmare of Eurasian exclusion, where both dictators understood that American entry would block continental domination. Technology, communications, finance, and trade show that he is not reducing power to territory, but geostrategy remains the organizing tool because a hostile Eurasian coalition would threaten American primacy at its source.

Chapter 1: Hegemony of a New Type

Summary: Chapter 1 explains how America moved from hemispheric isolation to global reach through the Spanish-American War of 1898, the Philippines, Hawaii, two-ocean naval doctrine, the Monroe Doctrine, manifest destiny, the Panama Canal, World War I, Wilson's Fourteen Points, World War II, the Cold War, the Berlin blockade, Korea, Afghanistan, and the Persian Gulf. Brzezinski compares U.S. primacy with Rome, Han China, the Ch'ing dynasty, the Mongol Empire, Spain, France, and Great Britain, then distinguishes America through control of oceans, military deployment on Eurasia's western and eastern extremities, roughly 30 percent of global GNP after the Cold War, cultural appeal, NATO, APEC, NAFTA, the IMF, the World Bank, and Bretton Woods. Source anchors: 1898; Panama Canal; Fourteen Points; Rome; Mongol Empire; NATO; Bretton Woods.

Analysis: The 1898 war, Panama Canal, and Fourteen Points show how American power acquired naval, industrial, and ideological instruments before it became global. Rome and the Mongol Empire provide contrast: Brzezinski uses their roads, legions, military speed, cultural confidence, and failures of succession to clarify why American power is broader but less openly imperial. NATO and Bretton Woods make the chapter a modern empire and periphery case: Washington rules through institutions, allied consent, elite bargaining, and market access rather than through the older imperial pyramid.

Chapter 2: The Eurasian Chessboard

Summary: Chapter 2 turns the metaphor into a map, arguing that Eurasia contains about 75 percent of the world's people, about 60 percent of global GNP, about three-fourths of known energy resources, the next six largest economies after America, most nuclear powers, and all plausible challengers to U.S. primacy. Brzezinski distinguishes geostrategic players such as France, Germany, Russia, China, and India from geopolitical pivots such as Ukraine, Azerbaijan, South Korea, Turkey, and Iran. He also lists the policy questions that follow: the desired form of Europe, Russia's future, a possible Central Eurasian Balkans, China's Far Eastern role, Japan's response, and dangerous coalitions such as China-Russia-Iran, China-Japan, German-Russian, or Franco-Russian alignments. Source anchors: 75 percent; 60 percent; France; Germany; Ukraine; Azerbaijan; China-Russia-Iran.

Analysis: The 75 percent population figure, 60 percent GNP figure, and energy-reserve claim explain why Eurasia is not scenery but the concentration of usable power. France, Germany, Russia, China, and India are named as active players because they can choose policies that alter the board, while Ukraine and Azerbaijan become chokepoints and gateways because their independence blocks Russian imperial restoration and opens or closes access routes. The China-Russia-Iran scenario gives the chapter its practical edge: Brzezinski is designing American policy to keep grievances from becoming a continental coalition.

Chapter 3: The Democratic Bridgehead

Summary: Chapter 3 treats Europe as America's bridgehead on the Eurasian mainland and as the possible springboard for expanding democracy eastward. Brzezinski contrasts France's wish for grandeur, Charles de Gaulle's Europe "from the Atlantic to the Urals," Alain Juppe's 1995 language of world power, French nuclear weapons, Francophone Africa, and Hervé de Charette's 1996 NATO remarks with Germany's search for redemption, reunification, the Franco-German partnership, the December 6, 1990 federal Europe commitment, the Weimar Triangle, Poland, Hungary, the Czech Republic, the Baltic states, Ukraine, and Václav Havel's Aachen speech of May 15, 1996. Source anchors: France; Germany; Atlantic to the Urals; December 6, 1990; Weimar Triangle; Václav Havel.

Analysis: France and Germany are not interchangeable allies here; France wants Europe to restore French rank, while Germany wants Europe and America to make German power acceptable. The December 6, 1990 commitment and Weimar Triangle show the order and governance mechanism Brzezinski favors: lock Germany into a European core while using Poland and NATO enlargement to move the bridgehead east. Václav Havel's Aachen appeal gives the enlargement policy a moral and strategic timetable, making Central Europe an active test of whether America means to keep Europe open.

Chapter 4: The Black Hole

Summary: Chapter 4 describes the 1991 Soviet collapse as the sudden removal of the Eurasian heartland from the map. In two weeks in December 1991, Russia, Ukraine, and Belarus dissolved the Soviet Union, the Commonwealth of Independent States replaced it, the Soviet flag came down from the Kremlin, and Russia emerged with about 150 million people while roughly 20 million Russian speakers lived outside its borders. Brzezinski names Ukraine's 52 million people, Odessa, Crimea, the Black Sea Fleet, Riga, Tallinn, Belarus, Chechnya, Tajikistan, Azerbaijan, Kazakstan, Turkmenistan, Uzbekistan, the Caspian Sea, Boris Yeltsin, Andrei Kozyrev, Evgenniy Primakov, China, Iran, NATO, the EU, and the Council of Europe as pieces in Russia's postimperial choice. Source anchors: December 1991; CIS; Ukraine; Odessa; Caspian Sea; Primakov; Council of Europe.

Analysis: December 1991 and the CIS show that Russia's problem is not only reduced power but national identity after empire. Ukraine, Odessa, Crimea, and the Black Sea Fleet explain why Brzezinski treats Ukrainian independence as pivotal: without Ukraine, Russian restoration becomes less European and more coercive toward non-Slavic regions. Primakov, China, Iran, NATO, the EU, and the Council of Europe define the alternatives he rejects and accepts; Russia can chase antihegemonic illusions, dominate the near abroad, or accept a European path that requires recognizing Ukraine, Azerbaijan, and Uzbekistan as sovereign facts.

Chapter 5: The Eurasian Balkans

Summary: Chapter 5 identifies the Caucasus and Central Asia as a larger, richer, more unstable version of the Balkans: Kazakstan, Kyrgyzstan, Tajikistan, Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan, Azerbaijan, Armenia, Georgia, and Afghanistan, with Turkey and Iran as nearby contestants and possible pivots. Brzezinski emphasizes oil, natural gas, gold, the U.S. Department of Energy estimate of demand rising more than 50 percent between 1993 and 2015, Soviet borders drawn in the 1920s and 1930s, Nagorno-Karabakh, Abkhazia, Ossetia, Tamerlane, Samarkand, Bukhara, the Fergana Valley, Chechnya, the CIS, Russian bases, pipelines through Georgia and Turkey, the 1995 Turkmenistan-Iran rail link, Nursultan Nazarbayev, Eduard Shevardnadze, and Suleyman Demirel. Source anchors: Kazakstan; Azerbaijan; Nagorno-Karabakh; Tamerlane; Fergana Valley; CIS; Turkmenistan-Iran rail link.

Analysis: Kazakstan and Azerbaijan carry different functions: Kazakstan is the shield that physically separates much of Central Asia from Russia, while Azerbaijan is the cork that controls Western access to the Caspian bottle. Nagorno-Karabakh, Abkhazia, Ossetia, and the Fergana Valley show why the region cannot be treated as empty commodity-system geography; borders, minorities, and civil wars decide who can turn oil and gas into leverage. The CIS, Turkmenistan-Iran rail link, and pipelines through Georgia and Turkey are the institutional and material mechanisms by which Russia either preserves leverage or loses its monopoly over the region's external connections.

Chapter 6: The Far Eastern Anchor

Summary: Chapter 6 argues that American Eurasian policy needs both maritime Japan and mainland China, but the relationship among America, China, and Japan is unstable. Brzezinski names China's view of Japan, Russia as "the hungry land," India, Taiwan, ASEAN, ARF, APEC, the Paracel Islands, Spratly Islands, Senkaku Islands, North Korea, the Kurils, Xinjiang, South Korea, the 1996 Taiwan minicrisis, Hong Kong, Macao, Deng's "one country, two systems," the G-7, Pakistan, Afghanistan, Southeast Asia, Japanese-Korean reconciliation, and a possible American-Japanese free trade agreement. He concludes that China should be accommodated as a regional power, while Japan should be encouraged toward global peacekeeping and economic leadership rather than regional military assertion. Source anchors: China; Japan; Taiwan; 1996 minicrisis; Hong Kong; South Korea; G-7.

Analysis: China and Japan pose opposite problems for American policy: China has a regional base but must be kept from coercive dominance, while Japan has global economic capacity but cannot safely become the regional military center. Taiwan, the 1996 minicrisis, and Hong Kong define the maritime-power line between accommodation and deterrence, because island access, coastal credibility, and sea-linked trade let China be included in the G-7 and world markets while still being warned that force against Taiwan would damage American credibility. South Korea and Japanese-Korean reconciliation matter because Korea decides whether U.S. forces remain anchored on the mainland edge and whether Japan becomes a constructive partner or a destabilized military actor.

Chapter 7: Conclusion

Summary: The conclusion converts the regional chapters into a time-sequenced program for American statecraft. Brzezinski argues that America is the only global superpower, a non-Eurasian arbiter of Eurasia, and likely the last state to hold such concentrated primacy; he cites military, economic, technological, and cultural power, President Clinton's phrase "indispensable nation," Ukraine, Uzbekistan, Kazakstan, Turkey, Iran, Taiwan, China, Japan, India, NATO, the EU, the G-7, OSCE, Kaliningrad, a Transatlantic Free Trade Agreement, a possible American-Japanese free trade agreement, and the proposed Trans-Eurasian Security System. He frames the program as short-run pluralism, middle-term strategic partnerships, and long-run shared responsibility through institutions that could eventually update the 1945 order. Source anchors: indispensable nation; Ukraine; Kaliningrad; OSCE; Trans-Eurasian Security System; 1945 order.

Analysis: The "indispensable nation" claim, Ukraine, and Kaliningrad show that Brzezinski's conclusion is not a general defense of American activism; it is a Eurasian design with concrete pressure points. OSCE, the G-7, NATO, the EU, and the Trans-Eurasian Security System turn the conclusion toward polycentric order: American primacy should seed overlapping institutions before a hostile coalition can form. The 1945 order matters because Brzezinski wants American power to revise global management before demographic pressure, migration, weapons proliferation, Taiwan, Russia's self-definition, or European fatigue makes the post-Cold War window close.
