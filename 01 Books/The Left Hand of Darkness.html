Detailed overview

Le Guin frames the novel in the 1976 introduction as neither prediction nor gadget-centered extrapolation. She names Mary Shelley, Philip K. Dick, Schrödinger, and the novelist's license to lie in order to tell truths that cannot be stated as forecasts. That preface matters for the whole book because Gethen is not offered as a probable future Earth. It is a controlled imaginative pressure chamber: a cold world, a human species without fixed sex, and a political mission whose success depends less on technology than on whether people can believe one another across habits of speech, fear, and pride.

The plot is built around Genly Ai's slow failure to read Winter before he can become useful to it. As First Mobile for the Ekumen, he arrives with an ansible, knowledge of Hain, Terra, Ollul, NAFAL ships, and eighty-plus inhabited worlds, but he repeatedly misunderstands Karhidish shifgrethor, Argaven XV's fearfulness, Tibe's opportunism, and Estraven's indirect protection. The opening in Erhenrang looks like a diplomatic ceremony around the Arch of River Gate, yet it is also a lesson in how a truthful message can be made politically unusable when the hearer fears humiliation more than ignorance.

Estraven supplies the counterline to Genly's official mission. In Karhide he is the King's Ear who loses office while trying to delay Tibe and protect the Envoy; in Orgoreyn he becomes an exile, a factory worker, a dependent of Yegey, and finally the rescuer who spends the last of Ashe's money, steals food in Turuf, and carries Genly out of Pulefen Farm under cover of sove-snow. Le Guin keeps returning to the word "traitor" because Estraven repeatedly betrays the smaller demand in order to keep faith with a larger human bond: he betrays Tibe's border game, Orgota secrecy, Argaven's fear, and even his own hope of returning safely to Estre.

The alternation of Genly's report, Estraven's journal, myths, anthropological notes, and religious excerpts turns Gethen into more than a setting. The Getheren and Hode tale, the Berosty prophecy, the Stok and Estre legend, the Yomesh passage on Meshe's Seeing, and the Orgota creation myth all give local forms to problems Genly is living through without yet understanding them. Names, shadows, hands, ice, darkness, kemmer, and foreknowledge are not decorative lore; they are the language in which Gethenians explain loyalty, time, death, and the limits of asking for certainty.

Orgoreyn initially appears to Genly as modern, orderly, and ready for Open Trade because Mishnory has broad streets, public bureaus, transient-houses, inspections, showers, and officials willing to ask direct questions. The same order soon reveals the Sarf, controlled radio, the Thirty-Three's secrecy, forged registries, Kundershaden Prison, and the Voluntary Farms. Karhide is volatile, poor, proud, and ruled by a frightened king, but its villages and hearths finally make Genly's survival possible. The novel never makes one nation simply good and the other bad; it tests political forms by how they handle the stranger, the outlaw, and the inconvenient truth.

The Gobrin Ice crossing is the book's central proof, but not because it proves Genly's story to a court. It forces Genly and Estraven into a practical equality made from orsh, gichy-michy, kadik-germ, the Chabe stove, ration arithmetic, snow terms, frostbite, tent discipline, and shared exhaustion. On the Ice, Genly finally sees Estraven as both man and woman, friend and alien, and Estraven hears mindspeech in Arek's dead voice. By the time the starship lands at Athten Fen, the diplomatic alliance rests on a private relation that cannot be reduced to treaty language: one person crossed the Ice with another and came back changed enough to speak truthfully for him.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Introduction - added in 1976

Summary: Le Guin rejects the idea that science fiction is mainly extrapolative prediction, using the example of purified food additives fed to mice, the reputation of science fiction as depressing, and the limits of "If this goes on" reasoning. She places the book nearer to Mary Shelley's invention, Philip K. Dick's crooked present, and Schrödinger's thought-experiment than to a forecast. She says the novelist's business is lying, but lying in the service of a truth that cannot be reduced to a newspaper statement. She identifies Gethenian androgyny as a way to describe psychological reality rather than a program for future biology, and she closes by pointing to Genly Ai's claim that truth belongs to imagination. Source anchors: extrapolative; Mary Shelley; Philip K. Dick; Schrödinger; thought-experiment; Gethenian androgyny; Genly Ai.

Analysis: The introduction tells the reader how to use Gethenian androgyny, Genly Ai, and the thought-experiment of Winter. Mary Shelley and Philip K. Dick mark a tradition in which invented worlds expose present assumptions, while Schrödinger gives Le Guin a model for constructing a condition and watching what it reveals. That framing makes the novel a Speculative Governance case rather than a simple prediction about sex or politics; every later detail, from kemmer to the ansible, becomes an imaginative test of what people notice and refuse to notice.

1. A Parade in Erhenrang

Summary: Genly Ai dates his report from the Archives of Hain as Ansible Document 01-01101-934-2-Gethen and describes the parade in Erhenrang on the forty-fourth diurnal of Year One, Ekumenical Year 1491. King Argaven XV sets the keystone of the Arch of the River Gate with red cement called bloodbond while gossiwors play and Karhidish domains compete in public display. Therem Harth rem ir Estraven, the King's Ear, warns Genly that he has lost favor, and Pemmer Harge rem ir Tibe turns the ceremony toward suspicion and ridicule. At dinner in the Corner Red Dwelling, Genly mistrusts Estraven's indirect warning because he still reads shifgrethor as evasion rather than protection. Source anchors: Erhenrang; Arch of the River Gate; Argaven XV; bloodbond; gossiwors; Estraven; Tibe; shifgrethor.

Analysis: Erhenrang, Argaven XV, and the Arch of the River Gate make the mission immediately political rather than abstractly interstellar. Bloodbond and gossiwors show a court culture where symbols do public work, while shifgrethor turns Language and Ideology into diplomatic constraint because Estraven cannot simply tell Genly what danger Tibe represents without violating the code that gives warning its meaning. The chapter matters because Genly's failure begins inside a specific Karhidish etiquette: he sees Estraven's caution as duplicity, so the only Gethenian trying to protect him becomes the one he distrusts most.

2. The Place Inside the Blizzard

Summary: This East Karhidish hearth tale tells of Getheren and Hode of Shath, brothers and kemmerings whose forbidden bond is broken when Hode kills himself and Getheren is driven onto the Gobrin Ice. In the white place inside the blizzard, Getheren meets the dead Hode, cannot get him to speak his own name, loses his left hand, and returns under the name Ennoch. Only when Getheren reclaims his name, his shadow, and his place at Shath does the curse lift and the hearth prosper again. The tale ties the Gobrin Ice to death, names, shadows, kinship, and the left hand before Genly and Estraven ever cross it. Source anchors: Getheren; Hode; Shath; Gobrin Ice; Ennoch; left hand; name and shadow.

Analysis: Getheren, Hode, and Shath give the Ice a moral geography that later travel chapters will activate. Ennoch's loss of name and shadow anticipates Estraven's exile, because a person without a lawful name becomes socially half-dead in Karhide. The left hand also prepares the book's title logic: division and wholeness, life and death, kin and outlaw, are already embedded in a Karhidish story rather than imported by Genly's Ekumenical categories.

3. The Mad King

Summary: Genly reaches the Red Hall for his first formal audience just after a proclamation declares Estraven exiled for treason. Argaven questions the Envoy about the Ekumen, Hain, the ansible, eighty-three habitable worlds, and three thousand nations, but he also calls Genly's people permanent kemmerers and treats sexual difference as evidence of perversion. Genly tries to explain trade in knowledge rather than conquest, while Spimolle and the court machinery turn the interview into a test of fear. Argaven finally refuses commitment, grants Genly freedom of Karhide, and leaves him thinking of the Fastnesses and Foretelling as another possible route. Source anchors: Red Hall; Estraven's exile; Argaven; ansible; Hain; eighty-three worlds; Spimolle; Fastnesses.

Analysis: The Red Hall converts the ansible, Hain, and eighty-three worlds into objects of royal anxiety. Estraven's exile has removed the one court figure who could translate Genly's purpose into Argaven's political language, and Spimolle's presence makes the audience a managed performance. The chapter matters because Karhide does not reject the Ekumen from ignorance alone; Argaven understands enough to fear loss of status, sexual contamination, and a decision that might bind his shifgrethor.

4. The Nineteenth Day

Summary: Tobord Chorhawa's tale from Gorinhering follows Berosty rem ir Ipe, who asks the Weaver Odren at Thangering Fastness to Foretell the day of his death. Odren answers that Berosty will die on Odstreth, the nineteenth day, and Herbor of Geganner later receives the paired answer that Berosty will live longer than Herbor. Berosty murders Herbor with a red stone table, waits in dread through the month of Thern, and hangs himself on the foretold day. The story makes Foretelling accurate but useless because the answer helps create the action Berosty fears. Source anchors: Berosty rem ir Ipe; Odren; Thangering Fastness; Odstreth; Herbor of Geganner; red stone table; Thern.

Analysis: Berosty rem ir Ipe and Herbor of Geganner turn Odren's exact answer into a trap rather than guidance. Thangering Fastness and Odstreth show why Handdara Foretelling is not simple prophecy for practical advantage: the questioner brings fear, and the answer gives that fear a date and shape. This prepares Genly's visit to Otherhord by making the value of knowing inseparable from the danger of asking the wrong thing.

5. The Domestication of Hunch

Summary: Genly leaves Erhenrang by landboat, crosses Eskar Pass into Old Karhide, and reaches Otherhord Fastness to ask whether Gethen will join the Ekumen within five years. Faxe explains the Handdara discipline and gathers nine Foretellers, including Zanies, Celibates, a kemmerer, a Pervert, and the Weaver, to make the answer. The Foretelling produces a clear "Yes," but Faxe immediately undercuts Genly's satisfaction by saying the practice shows how useless answers can be. Their conversation about ignorance, hunch, Nusuth, and the ground of action changes Genly's problem from obtaining information to understanding what kind of information can be lived with. Source anchors: Otherhord; Faxe; Handdara; Foretellers; Zanies; Pervert; Yes; Nusuth.

Analysis: Otherhord and Faxe give Genly a non-courtly Gethenian intelligence, one that treats ignorance as a discipline rather than a defect. The Foretellers' "Yes" seems to validate the Ekumen mission, but Nusuth and the mixed company of Zanies, Celibates, and the Pervert keep the scene from becoming a usable political certificate. The chapter matters because the book's later successes depend less on certainty than on Genly learning when certainty is too small for the situation.

6. One Way into Orgoreyn

Summary: Estraven narrates his own exile after a runner from the King's House brings the Order of Exile and nails it to his door in Erhenrang. He cannot sign a letter to Ashe, cannot call friends without endangering them, and walks eighty-five miles to Kuseben because Tibe has blocked ships and landboats. Ashe meets him at the Gulf, but Estraven refuses money and companionship, steals a rowboat, is struck by a sonic gun, and is rescued by a Kuseben patrol officer who honors the king's order rather than Tibe's murder attempt. He wakes in the Commensal Hospital of Charisune Coastal Area Four, faces an Inspector, receives papers for Mishnory, works at Fish Island, and is eventually collected from the wash-house by Commensal Yegey. Source anchors: Order of Exile; Ashe; Kuseben; sonic gun; Charisune; Inspector; Fish Island; Yegey.

Analysis: The Order of Exile, Ashe, and Kuseben show Estraven's political fall as an immediate physical stripping away of house, money, name, and safe speech. The sonic gun and Charisune Hospital expose Tibe's cruelty and Orgoreyn's bureaucracy in the same movement: one system tries to kill him unofficially, the other labels him into existence as a registrable unit. Fish Island and Yegey then move Estraven from destitution back into politics, but only as someone dependent on Orgota factions he already distrusts.

7. The Question of Sex

Summary: The Ekumenical Investigator Ong Tot Oppong reports from year 93 on Gethenian physiology, explaining somer, kemmer, kemmerhouses, pregnancy, parenthood, and the absence of fixed male and female social classes. The report warns against calling Gethenians neuters and notes that each adult may bear children and sire children at different times. It links the 26-day sexual cycle to social forms, observes that rape, sexual frustration, and permanent gender hierarchy are rare, and speculates cautiously about the relation between ambisexuality and Gethen's lack of war. The report also admits its own limits because any observer from a bisexual society keeps importing the wrong categories. Source anchors: Ong Tot Oppong; somer; kemmer; kemmerhouse; pregnancy; 26-day cycle; no war; bisexual observer.

Analysis: Ong Tot Oppong, somer, and kemmer give the reader the technical vocabulary Genly emotionally lacks. The kemmerhouse and 26-day cycle make sex a periodic public institution rather than a constant social sorting device, while the "no war" speculation is presented as tempting but not proven. The chapter makes Legibility unstable because the bisexual observer keeps importing categories that misclassify Gethenians as absence, neutrality, or anomaly where they live a different kind of presence.

8. Another Way into Orgoreyn

Summary: Genly crosses into Orgoreyn at Siuwensin during a border foray, is swept up with refugees, locked overnight in a dark storage-barn, and then singled out by officials once they recognize his name. He drives through East Orgoreyn's grainlands, hears radio bulletins that promise order while omitting the raid, passes inspection stations, and reaches Mishnory along the River Kunderer. Commissioner Uth Shusgis receives him lavishly, installs him in a heated apartment, and introduces him to Commensals Slose, Obsle, Yegey, and other notables. At Slose's supper, Genly explains Terra, Gde, Ollul, the Ekumen, Open Trade, the ansible, the waiting NAFAL ship, and the eleven people in stasis, while Estraven sits beside him like an unwelcome ghost. Source anchors: Siuwensin; East Orgoreyn; Mishnory; Shusgis; Slose; Obsle; Yegey; NAFAL ship.

Analysis: Siuwensin and East Orgoreyn first make Orgoreyn look more rational than Karhide, because signs, roads, inspections, and radio all seem to work. Shusgis, Slose, Obsle, and Yegey then convert Genly's mission into a factional opportunity around Open Trade and the NAFAL ship. Estraven's presence at the supper matters because Genly thinks he has chosen Orgoreyn freely, while the chapter quietly shows Information and Coordination being managed around him: route, reception, factional timing, and official questions have already shaped what he can say in Mishnory.

9. Estraven the Traitor

Summary: The East Karhidish tale explains the older meaning of Estraven's name through the feud between the Domains of Stok and Estre in Kerm Land. Arek of Estre falls through rotten ice on Icefoot Lake, is rescued by Therem of Stok in a thore-forest hut, and vows kemmering with his enemy before Stok men murder him. Therem later brings the child Therem to Estre, where Lord Sorve raises him as heir; the grown Therem kills attacking hearth-brothers and ends the feud by giving disputed land back to Stok. For this peace-making and kin-blood he is called Estraven the Traitor, yet the name Therem remains honored in Estre. Source anchors: Stok; Estre; Arek; Therem of Stok; Icefoot Lake; Lord Sorve; disputed lands; Estraven the Traitor.

Analysis: Stok, Estre, Arek, and Therem of Stok make "traitor" a word tied to reconciliation rather than simple disloyalty. Icefoot Lake and the disputed lands mirror the later Sinoth Valley dispute, where preventing border violence again looks like betrayal to people invested in feud. The chapter matters because it gives Estraven's political fate a mythic precedent inside his own Domain: he belongs to a lineage in which making peace can cost name, kin, and safety.

10. Conversations in Mishnory

Summary: Estraven visits Genly at Shusgis' house after the first snow of Odarhad Gor, accepts Ashe's money, and warns him that he has become the tool of an Orgota faction. At Yegey's lunch, Obsle identifies Mersen as a spy from Erhenrang and Gaum as an agent of the Sarf, while news arrives that Argaven's child has died. Genly explains the Ekumen as Household or Hearth, not a ruling government, and describes council, consent, the Eighty Worlds, Ollul, Open Trade, the ansible, the NAFAL ship, stasis, and the eleven crew. Slose wants a religious revelation, Gaum wants to expose a hoax, Obsle wants a prestige victory over Karhide, and Shusgis finally explains the Sarf as a Permanent Bureau of Internal Administration. Source anchors: Odarhad Gor; Ashe's money; Mersen; Gaum; Sarf; Eighty Worlds; Ollul; Open Trade; stasis.

Analysis: Ashe's money and Odarhad Gor reopen Genly's debt to Estraven just when Genly wants to keep him at a distance. Mersen, Gaum, and the Sarf make Mishnory's apparent openness dangerous, because every public question is also intelligence work. The Eighty Worlds, Ollul, Open Trade, and stasis give the Orgota officials enough real information to act, but Slose's religion and Obsle's prestige calculus show that Genly's truth will be used for purposes narrower than the Ekumen's.

11. Soliloquies in Mishnory

Summary: Estraven's Mishnory journal follows Obsle, Yegey, Slose, Ithepen, the Thirty-Three, and the Open Trade faction as they try and fail to make Genly's presence public. He records that only seven Commensals are reliable Open Traders, that the Sarf controls radio and printed bulletins, and that the Envoy remains hidden despite being escorted around the city. He works in a plastics factory as a unit, practices dothe and untrance, writes to his son, resents Genly's handling of Ashe's money, and watches Gaum attempt a kemmer seduction on Pyenefen Street. Genly speaks in the Hall of the Thirty-Three, is mocked as a Karhidish agent and sexual deviant by Kaharosile, and Estraven finally urges him to call the ship at once before the Sarf moves. Source anchors: Obsle; Yegey; Ithepen; Thirty-Three; Sarf radio; plastics factory; Gaum; Kaharosile.

Analysis: Obsle, Yegey, Ithepen, and the Thirty-Three show that Orgoreyn's problem is not lack of intelligent officials but their fear of one another. Sarf radio and Kaharosile's attack make concealment more dangerous than disbelief, because Genly cannot gain public protection while every channel is filtered. The plastics factory and Gaum scenes keep Estraven's analysis grounded in personal vulnerability: he understands the machine because he is living as one of its labeled units.

12. On Time and Darkness

Summary: This Yomesh Canon passage from The Sayings of Tuhulme the High Priest presents Meshe as the Center of Time whose Seeing contains all past and future at once. A poor man of Sheney finds treasure in the stone-fields of Tuerresh because Meshe sees a king bury it ten thousand years earlier, but Meshe also sees a future murder and the poor man's grave. The hemmen-tree in Ornen Forest tries to hide one leaf, only for Meshe to pluck it, and the text claims that every raindrop, star, and darkness between stars is bright in Meshe's Eye. Footnotes connect the doctrine to the expanding-universe hypothesis, Rerherek's constant, and the Yomesh polemic against the Handdarata who call on darkness. Source anchors: Meshe; Center of Time; Sheney; Tuerresh; Ornen Forest; Rerherek's constant; Handdarata; darkness.

Analysis: Meshe, the Center of Time, and Ornen Forest provide the religious opposite of Faxe's disciplined ignorance. Sheney and Tuerresh show total knowledge as unbearable because every rescue contains another death already visible. The note on Rerherek's constant is important because Le Guin joins cosmology to theology: the Yomesh hatred of darkness is both a metaphysical claim and a political habit that helps explain Slose's urge to turn the Ekumen into revelation.

13. Down on the Farm

Summary: After Estraven's warning, Genly cannot reach Obsle, Yegey, or Slose because Mishnory is occupied with the Solemnity of the Saints and Throne-Upholders, and Shusgis explains away Estraven's fear. Armed guards arrest Genly at night, take him to Kundershaden Prison, drug him with veridicals, and transfer him naked in a steel caravan-truck with twenty-five other prisoners. During the westward journey across the Sembensyens, two prisoners die, water is passed through a trap once a day, one young Orgota enters kemmer, and the group survives cold by huddling without leadership. At Pulefen Commensality Third Voluntary Farm and Resettlement Agency in District Thirty, Genly works in the thore sawmill, receives anti-kemmer drugs, undergoes examinations, befriends the dying carpenter Asra, and tries to tell him about other worlds before another interrogation erases his memory. Source anchors: Kundershaden; veridicals; caravan-truck; Sembensyens; Pulefen Farm; anti-kemmer drugs; Asra; thore sawmill.

Analysis: Kundershaden, veridicals, and the caravan-truck reveal the real Orgoreyn behind Shusgis' hospitality. Pulefen Farm and anti-kemmer drugs show Coercive Labor operating through bodies, not merely through censorship or paperwork, and the thore sawmill makes the brutality economically productive. Asra matters because Genly's grand Ekumenical story reaches one dying carpenter as a confused tale about flight, kemmer, and another sun, which is both a failure of communication and the most human exchange left to him there.

14. The Escape

Summary: Estraven turns from Obsle and Yegey to Shusgis, blackmailing him with a false story about the Nobles Faction, Tibe's assassination, and a Sarf contact in order to learn where Genly has been sent. He sends Sardon rem ir Chenewich at the Royal Embassy a message for Argaven, leaves Mishnory as a carry-loader on Odstreth Susmy, travels through Ethwen and the Esagel fur trade, joins Mavriva's hunters, and prepares a trapper's cover near Turuf. Under the forged identity Thener Benth, he enters Pulefen Farm as a paroled guard, steals the cook's sonic gun, cuts the fence switch, stuns Genly, carries him out in dothe through sove-snow, and hides him on a sledge in Tarrenpeth. After thangen sleep, broth, cleaned sores, and terror, Genly recognizes Estraven and learns that the Open Traders sold him to the Sarf. Source anchors: Shusgis; Chenewich; Ethwen; Mavriva; Thener Benth; dothe; sove-snow; Tarrenpeth.

Analysis: Shusgis and Chenewich show Estraven using reputation, embassy channels, and deception as tools of rescue rather than power. Ethwen, Mavriva, Thener Benth, and Tarrenpeth make the escape depend on local knowledge of trades, papers, snow, and geography, not on the Ekumen's technology. Dothe and sove-snow matter because Estraven's body becomes the means of political reversal: he literally carries Genly out of the institution that has tried to domesticate him.

15. To the Ice

Summary: Genly wakes in the hidden tent and begins to see Estraven without the court mask of Erhenrang or the stigma of Mishnory. They consider routes west to the coast or east to Karhide and choose the impossible-looking passage north through the Sembensyens, across the Gobrin Ice, and down toward Guthen Bay because roads mean Inspectors and papers. Estraven steals kadik-germ, dried breadapple, tea, and red tuber sugar in Turuf, calculates sixty days of gichy-michy plus extra food, and plans roughly eight hundred miles from Tormenbod Thern to Arhad Anner. Their first days through bessa snow, Tarrenpeth Forest, pesthry migration, rain, mud, and Fire-Hills teach Genly to trust Estraven's competence and to speak with him as Ai and Harth. Source anchors: hidden tent; Karhide route; Gobrin Ice; Turuf; gichy-michy; Tormenbod Thern; Tarrenpeth; pesthry.

Analysis: The hidden tent and Karhide route turn the mission into a survival problem with no institutional cover. Turuf, gichy-michy, Tormenbod Thern, and the Gobrin Ice make Logistics and Throughput intimate rather than industrial: Estraven's politics now depends on pounds, days, distances, and weather rather than declarations. Tarrenpeth and pesthry also change Genly's relation to Estraven: competence replaces suspicion, and the move from titles to Ai and Harth begins a friendship built inside labor rather than diplomacy.

16. Between Drumner and Dremegole

Summary: Estraven's journal records the passage from the Fire-Hills onto the Gobrin through the volcanic ice between Drumner and Dremegole. On Sordny Thanern they camp near firn and dirty crevasses; Drumner erupts with smoke, sulphur, red light, and tremors, while Dremegole grumbles in sympathy. Estraven notes Eskichwe rem ir Her's theory that volcanic CO2 may end or lessen the Ice Age, and Genly compares it to Terran explanations for glacial retreat. The two lose days searching for a route through pressure-blocks, ash, cinders, sove-snow, quakes, frostbite, and ice-cliffs before finally reaching the Gobrin on Harhahad Thanern. Source anchors: Drumner; Dremegole; Sordny Thanern; Eskichwe rem ir Her; CO2; sove-snow; Harhahad Thanern; pressure-blocks.

Analysis: Drumner, Dremegole, and pressure-blocks make the Ice crossing geologically active rather than a blank white ordeal. Eskichwe rem ir Her and CO2 connect Gethenian science to Terran climate theory, so Genly and Estraven can talk across worlds even while hauling a sledge through ash. Harhahad Thanern matters because the achievement is not heroic conquest of nature; it is a lucky passage through a landscape where fire, ice, and human calculation barely tolerate them.

17. An Orgota Creation Myth

Summary: The pre-Yomesh text from the Isenpeth Cave Shrine in the Gobrin Hinterlands begins with only ice and sun, then three ice-shapes who say they bleed, weep, and sweat. One makes earth from the sun's excrement, one makes seas and rivers by breathing on ice, and one makes plants, animals, and thirty-nine sleeping men from soil and seawater. Edondurath wakes first, kills thirty-six of the sleepers, pursues Haharath, and returns to the Birthplace on the Gobrin Ice, where the unnamed youngest brother enters the house of frozen bodies and couples with him. Their children are followed by shadows because death begins in the house of flesh and the end will leave ice and darkness again. Source anchors: Isenpeth Cave Shrine; ice and sun; thirty-nine men; Edondurath; Haharath; Gobrin Ice; house of bodies; shadows.

Analysis: Isenpeth Cave Shrine and Gobrin Ice place creation inside the same frozen geography Genly and Estraven are crossing. Edondurath, Haharath, and the unnamed youngest brother make kinship begin in murder, flight, kemmer, and survival, which echoes the Stok-Estre material without repeating it. The shadows in the house of bodies matter because the myth refuses Meshe's denial of darkness: human life begins with death already following it.

18. On the Ice

Summary: Genly remembers the tent on the Ice as the center of his life: Chabe stove heat, blown snow, Estraven's breathing, hunger, orsh, gichy-michy, facial masks, slit-screen eyeshields, sastrugi, and daily marches of twelve to eighteen miles. The two spend fifty days repeating camp discipline, rationing food, cutting snow walls, and fighting cold that can freeze nostrils, lashes, fingers, and lungs. Genly finally accepts Estraven as both man and woman during kemmer, offers mindspeech, and reaches him only when calling "Therem" in the dead brother Arek's voice. Later they endure blizzards, starvation, shadowless weather, and the calculation that Genly must call the ship quickly from Karhide, because Argaven can use Orgoreyn's lie about Pulefen Farm against the Commensals. Source anchors: Chabe stove; orsh; gichy-michy; sastrugi; kemmer; mindspeech; Arek; shadowless weather; Pulefen lie.

Analysis: The Chabe stove, orsh, gichy-michy, and sastrugi make friendship a repeated material practice before it becomes a declaration. Kemmer, mindspeech, and Arek force Genly to stop treating Estraven's body and reserve as puzzles to be solved from outside; the trust comes through a wound Estraven cannot hide. The Pulefen lie then gives the Ice crossing a political endpoint, but the chapter's deeper Moral Economy is that counsel becomes binding only after shared hunger, labor, exposure, and trust make Genly capable of carrying Therem's claim.

19. Homecoming

Summary: Genly and Estraven leave the Ice after eighty-one days and arrive at Kurkurast Domain near Guthen Bay, where the hot-shop, cook, road-packer, and Hearth hospitality return them to human society. Estraven tells the crossing as a saga, but they must move toward Sassinoth to find a transmitter strong enough to reach Genly's relay satellite and ship. At Thessicher's farm under the Sinoth Valley Administration, Estraven trusts an old friend he once helped resettle east of the Ey, while Genly sells the Chabe stove at the Town Commerce and buys ten minutes of private transmission from the College of the Trades. Thessicher betrays Estraven to Tibe for the price on his head, and Estraven skis for the Orgota border, refuses to wait, and is shot by Karhidish border guards with foray guns before reaching the red-topped fence. Source anchors: Kurkurast; road-packer; Sassinoth; Thessicher; Chabe stove; private transmission; Tibe; foray guns.

Analysis: Kurkurast and the road-packer show Karhide at its most generous, giving food, shelter, and recognition without asking for documents. Sassinoth, Thessicher, and Tibe then show how quickly that hearth-world can become lethal when fear and reward enter the same landscape. The Chabe stove and private transmission complete the mission's practical work, but the foray guns make the cost exact: Estraven gets Genly to the signal and removes himself from the treaty that his survival might endanger.

20. A Fool's Errand

Summary: Genly is held in Sassinoth after Estraven's death but treated by a young physician, fed in the Tower of the Lords-Elect, and asked about Pulefen Farm and the radio message. News of his escape from Orgoreyn and crossing of the Gobrin Ice spreads through Karhide, while Estraven's role remains officially unspoken but widely known. The Sarf faction loses ground in Mishnory, Obsle's Open Trade party takes the presiding offices of the Thirty-Three, and Tibe resigns after receiving Thessicher's warning and confirmation of Estraven's death. Argaven summons Genly to Erhenrang, Faxe receives him in the Round-Tower Dwelling as a councillor of the Hes-kyorremy, the king agrees to receive the ship at Athten Fen, and Lang Heo Hew descends with the Ekumen crew before Genly later visits Estre, Esvans Harth, and young Sorve with Estraven's journals. Analysis: The Sassinoth physician and Tower of the Lords-Elect contrast sharply with Kundershaden and Pulefen Farm, making Karhide's improvised mercy politically decisive. Obsle, the Sarf faction, and Tibe resigns show that Estraven's prediction was accurate: Genly's return exposes Orgoreyn's lie and gives Argaven a prestige victory. Faxe, Athten Fen, Lang Heo Hew, and Estre complete the book by joining public alliance to private mourning; Genly can bring the ship down, but Knowledge Preservation becomes the final obligation because he cannot make Argaven clear Estraven's name on demand, only carry journals and testimony back to the Hearth that made him.

The Gethenian Calendar and Clock

Summary: The appendix explains that Gethen's year is 8401 Terran Standard Hours, or .96 of a Terran year, and that its 23.08-hour rotation produces a 364-day year. Karhide and Orgoreyn count years from the current Year One, making the past "one-ago" and the future "one-to-come," while the Yomeshta use 144-year cycles from the Birth of Meshe. The moon's 26-day period creates fourteen fixed months, with winter names such as Thern, Thanern, Nimmer, and Anner, and day names such as Getheny, Arhad, Odgetheny, and Odeps. The system explains the dates scattered through Estraven's journal and Genly's report as part of a world whose timekeeping is cyclic, lunar, and locally named. Source anchors: 8401 hours; Year One; one-ago; Birth of Meshe; 26-day month; Thern; Getheny; Odgetheny.

Analysis: Year One, one-ago, and Birth of Meshe show that Gethenian history is not numbered with the forward linearity Genly brings from Ekumenical records. The 26-day month, Thern, Getheny, and Odgetheny clarify why kemmer, travel planning, and journal entries feel tied to repeated cycles rather than abstract dates. The appendix matters because it turns the book's many temporal references into a final piece of world logic: politics, sex, weather, and memory all happen inside a calendar that is always recentering itself.
