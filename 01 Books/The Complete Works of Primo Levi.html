The Complete Works of Primo Levi

Buy on Amazon — The Complete Works of Primo Levi

Position in the vault

This note belongs in the vault's book layer as a large collected-work node for Primo Levi's testimony, fiction, essays, poems, scientific imagination, and late reflection on Auschwitz. Use it beside Coercive Labor when reading the Lager's conversion of persons into expendable work material, beside Knowledge Preservation for Levi's long campaign against denial and damaged memory, and beside Informal Institutions for workshops, laboratories, partisan bands, publishing networks, and the small practices by which people preserve or abandon human obligation.

Detailed overview

The Complete Works of Primo Levi gathers a writer who cannot be reduced to survivor testimony, even though Auschwitz remains the center of gravity. Toni Morrison's introduction names the pressure that runs through the collection: Levi uses language, chemistry, classical literature, memory, and ethical precision to reclaim singular human beings from systems that made people into things. Ann Goldstein's editorial introduction stresses that English readers have often known Levi through If This Is a Man, The Truce, The Periodic Table, and The Drowned and the Saved, while this edition restores the scale of his fiction, essays, poems, journalism, and scientific fabulation.

Volume I begins with If This Is a Man, Levi's account of deportation to Auschwitz III-Monowitz, the Buna labor camp, the initiation into the Lager, hunger, the Ka-Be infirmary, chemical examination, Dante's Ulysses, Lorenzo, Charles, Arthur, and the ten days between the Germans' evacuation and liberation. The Truce then shifts from annihilation to wandering return: The Thaw, The Big Camp, The Greek, Katowice, Cesare, Victory Day, Starye Doroghi, Iasi, and The Reawakening turn postwar Europe into an immense waiting room where freedom is real but delayed, improvised, comic, and unstable.

The same volume then shows the other Levi. Natural Histories and Flaw of Form collect speculative stories in which machines, biological oddities, bureaucracies, synthetic substitutes, mimicry, memory devices, and engineered creatures disturb the ordinary world. Their titles alone show the range: The Mnemagogs, Censorship in Bitinia, The Versifier, Angelic Butterfly, Cladonia Rapida, Sleeping Beauty in the Refrigerator, Quaestio de Centauris, Letter 1987, The Synthetics, The Brokers, Knall, Creative Work, and Psychophant. These fictions use science not as escape from Auschwitz but as a way to test what happens when technique outruns judgment.

Volume II contains Levi's most explicitly chemical self-portrait, The Periodic Table. Argon, Hydrogen, Zinc, Iron, Potassium, Nickel, Lead, Mercury, Phosphorus, Gold, Cerium, Chromium, Sulfur, Titanium, Arsenic, Nitrogen, Tin, Uranium, Silver, and Vanadium make elements into occasions for family memory, Piedmontese Jewish identity, apprenticeship, antifascism, work, error, survival, industrial practice, and postwar correspondence. The Wrench then gives voice to Tino Faussone, whose rigging, bridges, bevel gears, offshore work, metalwork, and practical talk let Levi explore work as craft, narration, pride, and moral education.

The collected stories and essays from 1949-1980, Lilith and Other Stories, and If Not Now, When? widen Levi's scope again. He writes memorial pieces on Auschwitz, the deported, Arbeit Macht Frei, resistance in the Lagers, Israel, kibbutzim, Leon Poliakov, Edith Bruck, and technology; he returns to camp figures such as Lilith, Avrom, Cesare, Lorenzo, and the King of the Jews; and he imagines a Jewish partisan band moving through Eastern Europe toward Italy and Palestine. The Author's Note to If Not Now, When? is explicit that the novel is not a true story but a plausible itinerary built from Jewish resistance, Novoselky, Schmulek, Venyamin, Polina, Martin Fontasch, Pirkei Avot, and documentary reading.

Volume III makes the collection still more various. Collected Poems includes Buna, February 25, 1944, Song of the Crow I, Shema, Get Up, Ostjuden, Sunset at Fossoli, and Song of the Crow II, where accusation, memory, and dread become compressed. Other People's Trades gives Levi as essayist on Aldous Huxley, Rabelais, the moon, obscure writing, animals, signs on stone, guncotton stockings, pain, and why people write. Stories and Essays combines late fiction with pieces on Auschwitz, wartime tools, Kafka's The Trial, Emanuele Artom, Leonardo De Benedetti, Hermann Langbein, Jews in Turin, and the difficult journey of truth.

The Drowned and the Saved closes the works by returning to Auschwitz after decades of testimony, correspondence, argument, and historical revision. Its chapters, The Memory of the Offense, The Gray Zone, Shame, Communication, Useless Violence, The Intellectual in Auschwitz, Stereotypes, Letters from Germans, and Conclusion, examine memory's injuries and failures, prisoner functionaries, shame among survivors, language barriers, violence without utility, intellectual habits under camp conditions, simplified images of the Lager, and the German reception of Levi's first book. The collection ends not with catharsis but with a writer repeatedly testing how exact speech can resist denial, myth, self-exculpation, and the collapse of memory.

Section-by-section notes

1. If This Is a Man

Summary: If This Is a Man begins from Levi's deportation to Auschwitz in 1944 and follows the prisoner through the transport, the stripping away of ordinary identity, the bottom of the Lager, initiation, Ka-Be, nights in the barracks, work at Buna, and the fragile accidents that keep a person alive. The contents move through The Journey, On the Bottom, Initiation, Ka-Be, Our Nights, The Work, A Good Day, This Side of Good and Evil, The Drowned and the Saved, Chemistry Examination, The Canto of Ulysses, The Events of the Summer, October 1944, Kraus, Die Drei Leute vom Labor, The Last One, and The Story of Ten Days. Source anchors: If This Is a Man; Auschwitz; The Journey; Ka-Be; Buna; The Canto of Ulysses; The Story of Ten Days.

Analysis: If This Is a Man, Auschwitz, The Journey, Ka-Be, Buna, The Canto of Ulysses, and The Story of Ten Days define Levi's first task: to report an extreme condition without turning it into spectacle or myth. Buna organizes Coercive Labor by stripping prisoners of names, health, bargaining power, and exit before consuming their work; Ka-Be temporarily suspends labor only by classifying damaged bodies for return or disposal. The Canto of Ulysses preserves another language of human aspiration, while The Story of Ten Days tests whether men discarded by the labor system can rebuild reciprocal action.

2. The Truce

Summary: The Truce turns liberation into a long, comic, exhausted, and bewildering route home rather than a simple release. Its visible contents move from The Thaw and The Big Camp through The Greek, Katowice, Cesare, Victory Day, The Dreamers, Heading South, Heading North, A Little Hen, Old Roads, The Forest and the Path, Vacation, Theater, From Starye Doroghi to Iasi, From Iasi to the Line, and The Reawakening. The editorial material notes that Levi wrote it fourteen years after If This Is a Man, after retelling the journey many times to friends and school students, and that he emphasized Russians, odd episodes, travel companions, and the multicolored postwar maelstrom. Source anchors: The Truce; The Thaw; The Greek; Katowice; Cesare; Starye Doroghi; The Reawakening.

Analysis: The Truce, The Thaw, The Greek, Katowice, Cesare, Starye Doroghi, and The Reawakening change the moral weather of the collection without erasing the camp. The Thaw starts where death's machinery has stopped but ordinary life has not resumed, while The Greek and Cesare give Levi figures of cunning, appetite, and verbal energy that belong to survival outside the Lager. Starye Doroghi and The Reawakening make homecoming a geography of delay, showing that liberation produces stories, detours, and performances before it produces return.

3. Natural Histories

Summary: Natural Histories collects Levi's speculative stories under titles that sound scientific, bureaucratic, comic, and ominous at once. The contents include The Mnemagogs, Censorship in Bitinia, The Versifier, Angelic Butterfly, Cladonia Rapida, Order at a Good Price, Man's Friend, Some Applications of the Mimete, Versamine, Sleeping Beauty in the Refrigerator: A Winter's Tale, The Measure of Beauty, Quaestio de Centauris, Full Employment, and The Sixth Day. The book uses invented devices, engineered organisms, mythic hybrids, memory substances, automated writing, commercial services, and biological manipulation to ask what happens when ingenuity loses ethical proportion. Source anchors: Natural Histories; The Mnemagogs; The Versifier; Angelic Butterfly; Cladonia Rapida; Mimete; Quaestio de Centauris.

Analysis: Natural Histories, The Mnemagogs, The Versifier, Angelic Butterfly, Cladonia Rapida, Mimete, and Quaestio de Centauris use fiction to test Technological Change before institutions understand its consequences. The Mnemagogs and Mimete turn memory and copying into capabilities whose diffusion changes identity and responsibility; The Versifier delegates creative work to a machine; Angelic Butterfly makes biological redesign an ethical failure rather than a neutral breakthrough. Levi focuses on adoption and misuse, not gadgets in isolation.

4. Flaw of Form

Summary: Flaw of Form continues Levi's speculative mode but gives it a darker industrial and systems feel. Its source contents list Letter 1987, Protection, Heading West, The Synthetics, Observed from a Distance, The Brokers, Red Lights, Vilmy, With the Best Intentions, Knall, Creative Work, Our Fine Specifications, In the Park, and Psychophant. The titles indicate societies managed through materials, substitute beings, global observation, warning signals, contracts, design specifications, parks, psychological devices, and apparently benevolent interventions whose consequences are not fully controlled by their makers. Source anchors: Flaw of Form; Letter 1987; The Synthetics; The Brokers; Red Lights; Knall; Psychophant.

Analysis: Flaw of Form, Letter 1987, The Synthetics, The Brokers, Red Lights, Knall, and Psychophant matter because they sharpen Levi's suspicion of technical systems that present themselves as rational improvements. The Synthetics and The Brokers point toward artificial substitution and managed exchange, while Red Lights and Knall make warning and rupture part of the book's machinery. Psychophant is a fitting endpoint because Levi's concern is not only what tools do to bodies but what instruments, markets, and expertise can do to perception.

5. The Periodic Table

Summary: The Periodic Table organizes autobiography, chemistry, Jewish family history, wartime danger, industrial labor, and postwar memory through chemical elements. The source contents list Argon, Hydrogen, Zinc, Iron, Potassium, Nickel, Lead, Mercury, Phosphorus, Gold, Cerium, Chromium, Sulfur, Titanium, Arsenic, Nitrogen, Tin, Uranium, Silver, and Vanadium. The editorial notes record its 1975 Einaudi publication, Natalia Ginzburg's praise for Argon, the Prato Prize, its later U.S. publication by Schocken, and the Guardian's 2006 choice of it as the best science book ever written. Source anchors: The Periodic Table; Argon; Hydrogen; Cerium; Vanadium; Natalia Ginzburg; Prato Prize.

Analysis: The Periodic Table, Argon, Hydrogen, Cerium, Vanadium, Natalia Ginzburg, and Prato Prize show Levi joining exact matter to lived time. Argon carries family and Piedmontese Jewish memory, Hydrogen and other early elements belong to apprenticeship and experiment, and Cerium is tied to survival economics inside Auschwitz. Vanadium later makes chemistry into correspondence and reckoning, while Natalia Ginzburg and Prato Prize mark the point where this hybrid of science and memoir becomes recognized as central rather than marginal Levi.

6. The Wrench

Summary: The Wrench is built around skilled work, storytelling, and the voice of the rigger Tino Faussone rather than direct Holocaust testimony. Its contents list With Malice Aforethought, Cloister, The Helper, The Sassy Girl, Tiresias, Offshore, Metalwork, Wine and Water, The Bridge, Without Time, The Bevel Gear, Anchovies I, The Aunts, and Anchovies II, followed by a translator's afterword. The book's machinery includes cranes, rigs, steel, joints, offshore jobs, bridges, gears, worksite accidents, and long spoken accounts in which craft knowledge becomes a moral vocabulary. Source anchors: The Wrench; Tino Faussone; Offshore; Metalwork; The Bridge; The Bevel Gear; Anchovies.

Analysis: The Wrench, Tino Faussone, Offshore, Metalwork, The Bridge, The Bevel Gear, and Anchovies give Levi a way to honor labor without sentimentality. Tino Faussone matters because he narrates work from inside the hand, tool, and job rather than from an abstract theory of labor. Offshore, Metalwork, The Bridge, and The Bevel Gear let Levi treat competence as an ethical habit, while Anchovies shows that practical storytelling can carry comedy, appetite, and self-knowledge alongside engineering detail.

7. Uncollected Stories and Essays: 1949-1980

Summary: The first uncollected section gathers memorial, political, technological, and literary pieces written across the decades after the war. The visible contents include The Death of Marinese, The Deported. Anniversary, Monument at Auschwitz, Arbeit Macht Frei, The Time of the Swastikas, Bear Meat, Preface to the German Edition of If This Is a Man, Resistance in the Lagers, Yitzhak Katzenelson, Note on the Dramatized Version of If This Is a Man, The Deportation of the Jews, Israel Must Live, Encounters in the Kibbutzim, Leon Poliakov's Auschwitz, To the Young, Technographers and Technocrats, A Past We Thought Would Never Return, Edith Bruck, and This Was Auschwitz. Source anchors: Uncollected Stories and Essays; Arbeit Macht Frei; German Edition; Resistance in the Lagers; Yitzhak Katzenelson; Leon Poliakov; Edith Bruck.

Analysis: Uncollected Stories and Essays, Arbeit Macht Frei, German Edition, Resistance in the Lagers, Yitzhak Katzenelson, Leon Poliakov, and Edith Bruck show Levi as a public witness who keeps returning to how Auschwitz is taught, translated, dramatized, remembered, and politically used. German Edition matters because German readers were the audience Levi most urgently wanted to confront. Resistance in the Lagers, Yitzhak Katzenelson, Leon Poliakov, and Edith Bruck place his own testimony inside a wider archive of Jewish memory, partisan action, scholarship, and survivor writing.

8. Lilith and Other Stories

Summary: Lilith and Other Stories is divided in the source contents into Present Perfect and Future Anterior, placing camp memory beside speculative and philosophical fiction. Present Perfect includes Capaneus, The Juggler, Lilith, A Disciple, Our Seal, The Gypsy, The Cantor and the Veteran, The Story of Avrom, Tired of Fictions, The Return of Cesare, The Return of Lorenzo, and The King of the Jews. Future Anterior includes A Tranquil Star, Gladiators, The Beast in the Temple, Disphylaxis, Dizzying Heat, Bridge Builders, Self-Control, Dialogue Between a Poet and a Doctor, Children of the Wind, and The Fugitive. Source anchors: Lilith; Present Perfect; Future Anterior; The Return of Cesare; The Return of Lorenzo; The King of the Jews; A Tranquil Star.

Analysis: Lilith, Present Perfect, Future Anterior, The Return of Cesare, The Return of Lorenzo, The King of the Jews, and A Tranquil Star show Levi arranging memory and invention as paired modes rather than separate careers. The Return of Cesare and The Return of Lorenzo bring figures from the camp and return books back into later narrative space. The King of the Jews and Lilith keep Jewish legend and Lager memory in contact, while A Tranquil Star marks the move toward cosmic or speculative distance that still tests human fragility.

9. If Not Now, When?

Summary: If Not Now, When? is Levi's major novel of Jewish partisans, built from documentary reading and an explicitly invented itinerary. In the Author's Note, Levi names a Milan Aid Office in 1945, refugees returning home, Jewish fighters in Soviet or Polish bands, Venyamin's roving group, Novoselky, Schmulek's catacombs, railroad sabotage, hijacked parachute drops, Polina the pilot, Martin Fontasch the songwriter, Pirkei Avot, Rabbi Hillel, and the title's question. The translator's afterword emphasizes that the book appeared in 1982, that Levi called it a novel after thirty-five years of apprenticeship, and that the group moves through Eastern Europe toward Milan and an imagined Palestine. Source anchors: If Not Now When; Milan Aid Office; Venyamin; Novoselky; Polina; Martin Fontasch; Pirkei Avot.

Analysis: If Not Now When, Milan Aid Office, Venyamin, Novoselky, Polina, Martin Fontasch, and Pirkei Avot make this book Levi's attempt to imagine Jewish armed agency without pretending to write a documentary. Milan Aid Office gives the novel its postwar receiving point, while Venyamin and Novoselky anchor the invented band in the documented world of Eastern European partisan survival. Polina and Martin Fontasch individualize the group, and Pirkei Avot turns the title into a moral summons rather than only a plot question.

10. Collected Poems

Summary: Collected Poems gathers Levi's compressed writing on place, accusation, survival, animal presences, and recurring grief. The contents begin with At an Uncertain Hour and include Crescenzago, Buna, Singing, February 25, 1944, Song of the Crow I, Shema, Get Up, Monday, Another Monday, After R. M. Rilke, Ostjuden, Sunset at Fossoli, February 11, 1946, The Glacier, The Witch, Avigliana, Wait, Epitaph, and Song of the Crow II. Toni Morrison's introduction singles out melancholy and sorrow in the poems, especially insects, accusatory ghosts, sadness of place, and the two Crow poems as voices that sit inside sleep, bread, wine, and the heart. Source anchors: Collected Poems; At an Uncertain Hour; Buna; Shema; Song of the Crow I; Sunset at Fossoli; Song of the Crow II.

Analysis: Collected Poems, At an Uncertain Hour, Buna, Shema, Song of the Crow I, Sunset at Fossoli, and Song of the Crow II put Levi's witness into a more condensed and admonitory form. Buna and Sunset at Fossoli keep the geography of deportation present without the narrative breadth of If This Is a Man. Shema addresses the reader with scriptural pressure, while Song of the Crow I and Song of the Crow II make memory feel like an unwelcome messenger that returns each evening rather than a past event safely archived.

11. Other People's Trades

Summary: Other People's Trades collects Levi's essays on subjects outside his primary professional and testimonial identities, while still showing the chemist's habit of exact attention. The contents include My House, Aldous Huxley, Ex-Chemist, Francois Rabelais, The Moon and Us, Tartarin of Tarascon, Going Back to School, Why Do We Write?, Congested Air, Guncotton Stockings, Against Pain, About Obscure Writing, Leggere la Vita, Signs on Stone, Novels Dictated by Crickets, Domum Servavit, Renzo's Fist, Thirty Hours Aboard the Castoro 6, To Invent an Animal, and The Squirrel. Source anchors: Other People’s Trades; Aldous Huxley; Ex-Chemist; Why Do We Write; About Obscure Writing; Signs on Stone; To Invent an Animal.

Analysis: Other People’s Trades, Aldous Huxley, Ex-Chemist, Why Do We Write, About Obscure Writing, Signs on Stone, and To Invent an Animal show Levi deliberately stepping into other crafts without abandoning precision. Ex-Chemist lets him rethink his own professional identity from outside the factory and laboratory. Why Do We Write and About Obscure Writing make style an ethical matter, while Signs on Stone and To Invent an Animal show his pleasure in deciphering natural forms, human marks, and imagined creatures.

12. Stories and Essays

Summary: Stories and Essays combines late fiction with reflective prose on Auschwitz, memory, language, literature, and wartime artifacts. The source contents include To My Friends, The Thaw, The Interview, Made for Each Other, The Great Mutation, That Quiet Town of Auschwitz, Two Flags, An Erector Set Made with Love, A Wartime Pipette, Frogs on the Moon, The Mirror Maker, The Man Who Squeezed Through Walls, The Ant's Wedding, Force Majeure, A Mystery in the Lager, Time Checkmated, The Machine Gun Under the Bed, A Valley, The Commander of Auschwitz, and later items visible in the same section such as Kafka's The Trial, Emanuele Artom, The Path of a Jewish Writer, Asymmetry and Life, Hermann Langbein, Jews in Turin, and The Difficult Journey of Truth. Source anchors: Stories and Essays; That Quiet Town of Auschwitz; Wartime Pipette; Mystery in the Lager; The Commander of Auschwitz; Kafka’s The Trial; Difficult Journey of Truth.

Analysis: Stories and Essays, That Quiet Town of Auschwitz, Wartime Pipette, Mystery in the Lager, The Commander of Auschwitz, Kafka’s The Trial, and Difficult Journey of Truth show late Levi moving among story, object, witness, and literary judgment. Wartime Pipette and Mystery in the Lager keep material traces small and exact, while That Quiet Town of Auschwitz and The Commander of Auschwitz return to the place and agents of murder without rhetorical inflation. Kafka’s The Trial and Difficult Journey of Truth place Levi's camp memory beside broader questions of law, evidence, distortion, and the effort required to speak accurately.

13. The Drowned and the Saved

Summary: The Drowned and the Saved is Levi's late return to Auschwitz as a problem of memory, responsibility, shame, communication, and distortion. Its contents list Preface, The Memory of the Offense, The Gray Zone, Shame, Communication, Useless Violence, The Intellectual in Auschwitz, Stereotypes, Letters from Germans, Conclusion, Works Cited, and a translator's afterword. The preface recalls 1942 reports of the death camps, Simon Wiesenthal, the SS warning that no one would believe survivors, recurrent dreams of not being listened to, Auschwitz gas chambers and crematoriums, the Warsaw Ghetto archive, Treblinka graves, Stalingrad, death marches, Geheimnistrager, Topf of Wiesbaden, hydrogen cyanide, and civilian silence. Source anchors: The Drowned and the Saved; The Gray Zone; Shame; Letters from Germans; Simon Wiesenthal; Geheimnistrager; Topf.

Analysis: The Drowned and the Saved, The Gray Zone, Shame, Letters from Germans, Simon Wiesenthal, Geheimnistrager, and Topf make Levi's final Auschwitz book an inquiry into Knowledge Preservation under deliberate destruction and damaged recall. The SS kill Geheimnistrager and erase evidence because control of the record extends the crime, while survivor shame and the Gray Zone complicate any clean retrospective narrative. Letters from Germans and Topf force preserved testimony into contact with denial, industrial cooperation, and civilians who knew enough to understand.

Source anchors: Uncollected 1981-1987; Horseshoe Nails; Who Has Courage in Jerusalem; Leonardo De Benedetti; Emanuele Artom; Asymmetry and Life; Jews in Turin.

Analysis: Uncollected 1981-1987, Horseshoe Nails, Who Has Courage in Jerusalem, Leonardo De Benedetti, Emanuele Artom, Asymmetry and Life, and Jews in Turin show the late Levi still refusing a single role. Horseshoe Nails and Asymmetry and Life keep his scientific curiosity alive, while Leonardo De Benedetti and Emanuele Artom return him to named Jewish witnesses and victims. Who Has Courage in Jerusalem and Jews in Turin keep Jewish political and local memory in view, so the collection ends with Levi as chemist, Turinese Jew, reader, memorialist, and public moral voice.
