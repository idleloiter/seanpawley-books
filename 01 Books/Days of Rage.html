Days of Rage

Buy on Amazon — Days of Rage

Position in the vault

Days of Rage is one of the vault's strongest books on underground militancy, revolutionary fantasy, state response, and the gap between ideological intensity and strategic viability. It belongs with Taliban, Autumn in the Heavenly Kingdom, The Tyrannicide Brief, and Seeing Like a State as a study of how political actors imagine legitimate violence and what actually happens when clandestine movements try to sustain it. It is a foundational source for the vault's War, Revolution, and Civilizational Breakdown hub, since its whole subject is a would-be revolutionary war that never reached the state-shattering threshold: the movement collapsed before it ever became a general crisis.

Detailed overview

Bryan Burrough's book is less a narrow organizational history than a reconstruction of a whole American underground ecosystem that many later narratives flattened into a few familiar names. The central claim running through the book is that the 1970s underground was not one coherent revolutionary front, but a shifting overlap of factions, fantasies, grudges, and improvisations. Weather, the Black Liberation Army, the Symbionese Liberation Army, the FALN, and the United Freedom Front all appear in the book not as interchangeable radicals but as distinct formations with different social roots, racial politics, strategic styles, and relationships to mythmaking.

One of the book's major strengths is that it refuses both romanticism and easy dismissal. Burrough takes underground actors seriously enough to reconstruct their motivations, networks, and operating methods, but he also shows how much delusion and improvisation structured their worldview. Many of these militants believed they were midwives to a coming revolution, or at minimum agents who could intensify contradictions until a revolutionary situation emerged. The book demonstrates again and again that this expectation produced both tactical daring and strategic confusion. Bombings, robberies, jailbreaks, propaganda, and clandestine living all created drama and fear, but they did not create mass legitimacy. The underground repeatedly mistook atmosphere for momentum.

The book also matters because it turns state response into part of the story without letting the state become the whole story. FBI pursuit, surveillance, prosecutorial strategy, informants, internal splits, and policing all shaped the underground's trajectory, but the movements were not simply crushed from outside. They also exhausted themselves. Their internal disagreements, paranoid cultures, failed discipline, and inability to bridge from clandestine violence to durable political formation were decisive. This makes the book especially useful for the vault's Crisis Governance, Language and Ideology, Informal Institutions, and War, Crisis, and Collapse notes.

Burrough is also very good on generational atmosphere. The underground did not emerge out of nowhere. It was fed by Vietnam, Black radicalism, the crumbling of New Left optimism, state repression, revolutionary internationalism, and the psychological afterlife of the 1960s. Days of Rage is therefore a strong corrective to simplified stories in which terrorism simply appears as pathology. The book shows a sequence: protest radicalization, strategic despair, clandestine glamour, ideological hardening, factional fragmentation, and eventual organizational decay.

In vault terms, Days of Rage is especially valuable because it shows what happens when a movement decides that symbolism, militancy, and moral urgency can substitute for mass politics, institutional depth, or a realistic theory of power. It is one of the clearest books here on how revolutionary theater and underground violence can become self-sealing worlds.

Major people, societies, and motivations

This is nonfiction: Burrough's "cast of characters" is an interlocking set of underground formations, the state institutions arrayed against them, and the support networks around both. His own argument is that the underground was a real, deadly, but ultimately self-defeating ecology — the "forgotten first decade of a shadow war" that Americans misremember as a hippie antiwar sideshow.

  • Weather Underground (Weatherman): the first and largest nationwide underground bombing group. It wants to accelerate revolution through bombing, clandestinity, and identification with global anti-imperial struggle, but cannot sustain a coherent path from militancy to governable politics. Its leadership core — Bernardine Dohrn, Jeff Jones, Bill Ayers, Mark Rudd, John Jacobs, Terry Robbins — turns protest into "actions" and eventually into an attempted killing program before the Townhouse explosion forces it to soften tactics.
  • Black Liberation Army and adjacent Black underground actors: motivated by repression, racial violence, prison radicalism, and the belief that armed struggle is a necessary response to state and white-supremacist power. Its named actors — Eldridge Cleaver, Dhoruba Moore, Lumumba and Zayd Shakur, Sekou Odinga, Twymon Meyers, Joanne Chesimard (Assata Shakur), Ronald Carter — see gunfire as defensive rather than theatrical, rooted in poverty, discrimination, police violence, and the Panthers' collapse.
  • The second wave (SLA, FALN, United Freedom Front, the Family): distinct new formations that emerge after Weather and the BLA's first peak. The Symbionese Liberation Army is a prison-radicalized, media-saturated cult around escaped convict Donald DeFreeze ("Cinque") that kidnaps Patty Hearst. The FALN is the most determined and most secretive bombing group, tied to Puerto Rican independence. The United Freedom Front is a blue-collar, family-based cell around Ray Levasseur. The Family is a pan-radical, interracial alliance (Mutulu Shakur, Marilyn Buck, Sekou Odinga, Silvia Baraldini) that stages jailbreaks and the Brinks robbery.
  • FBI and law-enforcement actors: want suppression, prosecution, and disruption of underground violence, often through aggressive surveillance and intelligence methods. J. Edgar Hoover, Bill Sullivan, Richard Helms, the NSA and DIA are drawn into a domestic contest that pushes institutions toward illegality by shortcuts.
  • Journalists, lawyers, and historians in Burrough's frame: want to recover how these underground worlds actually operated rather than how they later mythologized themselves. A small network of radical attorneys (Elizabeth Fink, Susan Tipograph, and others) both shields the underground and, by granting access, makes Burrough's reconstruction possible.

Themes and concepts to track

Central threads to follow through the book's chapters:

  • The gap between ideological intensity and strategic viability. Every group believes it is a vanguard on the verge of revolution; every group lacks a theory of how violence converts into mass support or state power. Bombings create fear and spectacle but not legitimacy.
  • Mistaking atmosphere for momentum. Protest radicalization, strategic despair, clandestine glamour, and the "glamour of dealing with the underground" drive escalation; the movements repeatedly misread their own publicity as historical advance.
  • Race as the hidden core. Burrough insists the underground was about Black liberation and white identification with it, not mainly the Vietnam War, which groups used "purely opportunistically."
  • Self-exhaustion versus external repression. The state pursues the groups, but they also collapse from internal purges, paranoia, indiscipline, drug use, and the inability to convert clandestine survival into durable political formation.
  • State overreach and institutional shortcuts. FBI illegal break-ins, the Nixon-era national-security panic, and the treatment of a small underground as the era's supreme threat show institutions distorting themselves against a minor enemy.
  • Media and self-mythology. Communiqués function as "exploding press releases"; the SLA becomes a media event; the underground remembers itself through loyalty and silence. Forgetting and reconstruction structure the whole book.
  • The family and the small group as organizational form. By the end, the underground survives as couples, children, safe houses, and personal networks — informal institutions replacing failed mass organizations.

Core concepts

  • Language and Ideology — communiqués as "exploding press releases"; "self-defense" sliding into "Black Power," "resistance," revolution; "armed revolutionary love" as justification.
  • Informal Institutions — false identities, safe houses, cell structure, radical-lawyer networks, family-based cells that carry organization where formal hierarchy is thin.
  • State Capacity — the FBI and police as the state's coercive arm, both pursuing the underground and failing to coordinate or stay within legal bounds.
  • Intelligence — surveillance, informants, break-ins, airtels; how intelligence pressure both hunted the groups and distorted institutions.
  • Crisis Governance — Nixon summoning the FBI, CIA, NSA, and DIA; treating the underground as a national-security threat and justifying shortcuts.
  • War, Crisis, and Collapse — the underground's collapse from internal decay, factionalism, and failed discipline, not only from external pressure.
  • Information and Coordination — the FBI's inability to separate actionable traces from public fear; the historical problem of reconstructing a deliberately hidden past.
  • Moral Economy — grievances rooted in oppression, poverty, and racial injustice that gave the underground its moral self-image; the SLA's food program as redistributionist spectacle.
  • Legitimacy and Succession — DeFreeze's elevation to "Cinque" as racial authenticity, the succession of command to Harris, and the contest over who could legitimately claim revolutionary authority.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

PROLOGUE

Summary: Burrough opens in a Brooklyn diner with Cathy Wilkerson as a sixty-eight-year-old grandmother, math teacher, Weather Underground veteran, and survivor of the March 6, 1970 Greenwich Village townhouse explosion. The conversation moves from Wilkerson's grandson and Park Slope present life to her hidden San Francisco bomb-making, the unnamed explosives guru who fathered her daughter, the Weather campaign in New York, San Francisco, Chicago, Boston, Pittsburgh, and Washington, and the wider underground of the BLA, SLA, FALN, Family, and United Freedom Front. He then frames the book as the story of a forgotten urban guerrilla "war" — bombings that were widespread (more than 2,500 in an eighteen-month period of 1971-72) yet far less lethal than modern terrorism, a "shadow war" between a half-dozen radical groups and the FBI. Source anchors: Cathy Wilkerson; Brooklyn diner; Greenwich Village; March 6 1970; Park Slope; FALN.

Analysis: Cathy Wilkerson, the Brooklyn diner, and Greenwich Village make the book's historical method intimate before it becomes encyclopedic. March 6 1970 is the hinge because Burrough uses one accidental blast to connect memory, secrecy, and operational detail. The prologue's insistence that the movement has been "forgotten" makes Information and Coordination a governing problem: clandestine politics erases its own record. And because bombs "basically functioned as exploding press releases," the whole underground is impossible to read without Language and Ideology — the movement communicated with dynamite.

Sam Melville and the Birth of the American Underground

Summary: The opening historical chapter follows Sam Melville and Jane Alpert from Woodstock, the East Village, Rat Subterranean News, and Columbia-era Movement politics into actual bomb work. Melville hides FLQ fugitives Alain Allard and Jean-Pierre Charette, learns safe houses, false papers, briefcase bombs, and bomb-threat calls, helps them hijack National Airlines flight 91 to Cuba, then escalates from George Metesky fantasies and dynamite theft to Manhattan bombings that make him Burrough's "Patient Zero" for the underground. Source anchors: Sam Melville; Jane Alpert; Woodstock; FLQ; National Airlines 91; George Metesky.

Analysis: Sam Melville and Jane Alpert give the book a concrete origin for white underground bombing outside campus protest. Woodstock and Rat Subterranean News show the cultural setting, while FLQ fugitives, safe houses, false papers, and National Airlines 91 supply the practical apprenticeship. These Informal Institutions turn fantasy into action before any stable organization exists. George Metesky matters because Melville grafts an older New York bombing tradition onto late-1960s revolutionary language, showing how a new Language and Ideology attaches itself to existing patterns of political violence.

Black Rage and the Road to Revolution

Summary: Burrough corrects the idea that the 1970s underground was simply antiwar violence by tracing it through American political violence, Black radicalization, police brutality, prisons, race riots, Malcolm X, the Panthers, Eldridge Cleaver, George Jackson, and the revolutionary appeal of armed self-defense. The chapter distinguishes Vietnam protest from the Black Liberation Army's roots in repression, poverty, incarceration, and the claim that Black people were already living under state violence. Source anchors: Black Liberation Army; Vietnam War; Eldridge Cleaver; George Jackson; Panthers; police brutality.

Analysis: Black Liberation Army and Panthers keep Burrough from making Weather the whole story. Vietnam War is important mainly as a misleading shorthand, because Eldridge Cleaver, George Jackson, and police brutality explain why many Black militants saw armed struggle as defensive rather than theatrical. The chapter's real subject is how a vocabulary of Language and Ideology — "self-defense" becoming "struggle," "resistance," "Black Power," then revolution — escalates under a perceived failure of State Capacity to protect Black citizens. The grievances anchor the underground in a Moral Economy of oppression, giving later BLA sections their own causal history instead of treating them as an imitation of white student radicalism.

Part One

Summary: Part One is the Weather section, grouping the emergence of Weatherman, the Flint period, the January-to-March 1970 move underground, the March-to-June aftermath of the townhouse explosion, the June-to-October bombing campaign, and the FBI pursuit through April 1971. Its named actors include Bernardine Dohrn, Jeff Jones, Bill Ayers, Terry Robbins, Diana Oughton, Cathy Wilkerson, Kathy Boudin, Ron Fliegelman, J. Edgar Hoover, Bill Sullivan, and Chicago/New York/San Francisco Weather cells. Source anchors: Weatherman; Bernardine Dohrn; Flint; Terry Robbins; FBI; Bill Sullivan.

Analysis: Weatherman, Bernardine Dohrn, and Flint identify the moment when student radical rhetoric becomes clandestine organization. Terry Robbins and the FBI give the part its twin engines: Weather's internal escalation and federal pursuit. Bill Sullivan matters because Burrough treats state illegality and underground illegality together, showing the early 1970s as a competition between revolutionary fantasy and intelligence shortcuts — a contest best read through Crisis Governance and Intelligence, since a tiny group pulls national-security institutions into domestic politics.

The Movement and the Emergence of Weatherman

Summary: Burrough introduces Weatherman as the first and largest nationwide underground bombing group, then roots it in SDS, the Columbia protests, the collapse of Movement optimism, the Weatherman paper, and the leadership circle around Bernardine Dohrn, Jeff Jones, Bill Ayers, Mark Rudd, John Jacobs, and Terry Robbins. The chapter explains how anti-imperialist identification, fantasies of white youth joining Black revolution, and the failure of mass protest fed the move from demonstrations to "actions." Source anchors: Weatherman; SDS; Bernardine Dohrn; Mark Rudd; John Jacobs; anti-imperialist.

Analysis: Weatherman and SDS show that the organization came from the New Left's own institutions, not from an isolated criminal subculture. Bernardine Dohrn, Mark Rudd, and John Jacobs use leadership style and ideological writing to redefine courage as escalation. Anti-imperialist rhetoric makes Language and Ideology operational: it gives Weather a global vocabulary while concealing the group's failure to build a revolutionary base in the United States.

Weatherman, January to March 1970

Summary: After Flint, the Weather leadership tries to turn selected cadres into a combat force, with Dohrn, Jeff Jones, Bill Ayers, Terry Robbins, Cathy Wilkerson, Kathy Boudin, and Diana Oughton moving through safe houses, discipline, weapons talk, and the decision to attack targets such as Fort Dix. Burrough follows the chosen "soldiers" as they imagine themselves as a Castro-like vanguard while shedding ordinary Movement life for clandestine roles. He details the purges ("the consolidation"), the false identities built from dead infants' birth certificates, and the agreed doctrine that policemen and military personnel were legitimate targets. Source anchors: Flint; Dohrn; Jeff Jones; Terry Robbins; Cathy Wilkerson; Fort Dix.

Analysis: Flint, Dohrn, and Jeff Jones show the leadership manufacturing a sense of historic command. Terry Robbins and Fort Dix turn that posture lethal because the intended target is not just property but soldiers and civilians at a dance. The "consolidation" and the network of false documents, safe houses, and radical attorneys are pure Informal Institutions — clandestine infrastructure replacing the formal organization Weather had just dissolved. Cathy Wilkerson matters as both participant and later witness, letting Burrough connect operational choices to the memories of people who survived them. The leaders' belief that they formed a Castro-like vanguard is Language and Ideology doing political work, authorizing killing in the name of a revolution that had no base.

The Townhouse — Weatherman, March to June 1970

Summary: The March chapter centers on the Greenwich Village townhouse at 18 West Eleventh Street, where Terry Robbins, Diana Oughton, Ted Gold, Cathy Wilkerson, and Kathy Boudin prepare bombs for the Fort Dix dance before the subbasement explosion kills Robbins, Oughton, and Gold. Wilkerson and Boudin crawl from the rubble, disappear, and leave Weather to reinterpret a catastrophic accidental detonation as both trauma and warning while police and reporters descend on the ruined townhouse. Source anchors: March 6 1970; 18 West Eleventh; Terry Robbins; Diana Oughton; Ted Gold; Kathy Boudin.

Analysis: March 6 1970 and 18 West Eleventh are the book's most important operational failure because they expose the gap between Weather's self-image and its technical competence. Terry Robbins, Diana Oughton, and Ted Gold become martyrs inside the group, but Burrough keeps the Fort Dix target visible so the reader remembers what the bomb was meant to do. Kathy Boudin and Wilkerson's escape forces a War, Crisis, and Collapse test: Weather must convert a lethal failure into a founding trauma that permits continued action, transforming collapse into a myth of commitment.

Weatherman, June 1970 to October 1970

Summary: Burrough follows Weather after the townhouse disaster as it resumes bombings while Nixon, J. Edgar Hoover, Richard Helms, the NSA, and the DIA discuss stronger domestic-intelligence action. The chapter includes the NYPD headquarters attack (June 9, 1970, at Centre Street), communiqués, federal panic, illegal break-in planning, Weather's effort to avoid casualties, and the way the state began treating the underground as a national-security threat rather than a local policing problem. Source anchors: Nixon; J. Edgar Hoover; Richard Helms; NYPD headquarters; Bill Sullivan; NSA.

Analysis: Nixon, J. Edgar Hoover, and Richard Helms put Weather inside the White House's machinery of fear. NYPD headquarters and Bill Sullivan show reciprocal escalation: Weather bombs symbols of police power, and federal officials reach for covert methods beyond normal criminal investigation. NSA matters because Burrough is showing Crisis Governance distorting Intelligence: a small underground group pulls multiple national-security institutions into domestic politics and encourages shortcuts around legal restraint.

Weatherman and the FBI, October 1970 to April 1971

Summary: The FBI receives floods of tips after Weather bombings, including fantasies about Fort Detrick biological weapons, while agents struggle to find actual fugitives. Burrough follows pressure on Hoover's Bureau, Tucson dynamite leads, construction-supply purchases, Swearingen, safe-house hunting, October attacks, the March 1, 1971 U.S. Capitol bombing, and the gap between public fear and investigative knowledge as Weather survives its first year underground. Source anchors: FBI tips; Fort Detrick; Tucson; fifty pounds; Swearingen; October bombings.

Analysis: FBI tips and Fort Detrick show how rumor inflated Weather's perceived capacity beyond its real organization. Tucson and fifty pounds of dynamite return the pursuit to material evidence, where clandestine politics depends on purchases, storage, and nervous shopkeepers. Swearingen exposes an Information and Coordination problem inside the FBI: agents must distinguish actionable traces from public fear while institutional pressure rewards illegal methods. The chapter also demonstrates State Capacity straining at its own limits — vast resources devoted to catching a core of barely a dozen bombers.

Part Two

Summary: Part Two shifts from Weather to the Black Liberation Army and the FBI's parallel pursuit of Black militants and Weather fugitives in 1971-1973. The section gathers Eldridge Cleaver, Donald Cox, Sekou Odinga, Lumumba Shakur, Zayd Shakur, Dhoruba Moore, Twymon Meyers, Ronald Carter, Joanne Chesimard, the Foster-Laurie murders, New York cells, Panther splits, and the Encirclement of Weather's San Francisco apartment. Source anchors: Black Liberation Army; Eldridge Cleaver; Sekou Odinga; Twymon Meyers; Joanne Chesimard; Encirclement.

Analysis: Black Liberation Army, Eldridge Cleaver, and Sekou Odinga require a different account of underground violence than Weather's student-radical story. Twymon Meyers and Joanne Chesimard make the section more lethal and more deeply tied to police shootings, prison politics, and Panther fragmentation. Encirclement keeps Weather in view, but Part Two's center is the armed Black underground Burrough says previous histories largely neglected. The section repeatedly shows a Moral Economy of racial grievance colliding with State Capacity — an under-resourced and racially charged police pursuit that is both overzealous and ineffective.

The Birth of the Black Liberation Army, Spring 1971

Summary: The BLA birth chapter begins with the claim that Black underground armed struggle grew from poverty, housing, unemployment, medical care, schools, police violence, and the belief that Black people were not free or equal. Burrough places that claim into Riverside Park, May 19, Manhattan, Panther conflicts, Eldridge Cleaver's Algiers influence, Dhoruba Moore, Lumumba and Zayd Shakur, and early police confrontations that turned rhetoric about self-defense into underground cells. Source anchors: Riverside Park; May 19; Eldridge Cleaver; Dhoruba Moore; Lumumba Shakur; Zayd Shakur.

Analysis: Riverside Park and May 19 give the BLA's emergence a precise New York setting rather than a vague atmosphere of rage. Eldridge Cleaver and Dhoruba Moore connect local militancy to Panther ideology and exile politics. Lumumba Shakur and Zayd Shakur show Informal Institutions forming an armed underground: personal networks, prison-inflected theory, and neighborhood loyalties carry organization where formal hierarchy remains thin.

The Black Liberation Army, June 1971 to February 1972

Summary: Burrough separates what the public knew, what police suspected, and what was happening inside the BLA as New York militants consolidated into Bronx and Brooklyn cells divided into subcells. The chapter tracks Eldridge Cleaver's influence, perhaps fifty or sixty underground Panthers, independent operations, police shootings, Ronald Carter, Twymon Meyers, Thomas "Blood" McCreary, and the Foster-Laurie murders that made the BLA a central law-enforcement target. Source anchors: Bronx cell; Brooklyn cell; subcells; Ronald Carter; Twymon Meyers; Foster-Laurie.

Analysis: Bronx cell, Brooklyn cell, and subcells show that the BLA had structure even when outsiders doubted it existed — an instance of Informal Institutions operating beneath the radar of formal recognition. Ronald Carter and Twymon Meyers give Burrough's account named actors whose violence shaped police perception of the whole underground. Foster-Laurie matters because the murders changed the BLA from a rumored formation into a priority enemy for New York law enforcement, redirecting State Capacity toward a group most of the country did not believe existed.

The Weather Underground and the FBI, 1971−72

Summary: Weather's San Francisco "Encirclement" and narrow March 1971 escape become Burrough's evidence that the organization was shrinking after its first underground year. The chapter follows dwindling numbers, FBI break-ins, leadership movement, communiqués, apartment surveillance, San Francisco logistics, Weather lore, and the contrast between the group's public image and the smaller cadre actually available for bombings. Source anchors: Encirclement; March 1971; San Francisco; FBI break-ins; communiqués; Weather lore.

Analysis: Encirclement and March 1971 show Weather surviving through luck and discipline, but San Francisco and Weather lore also reveal how survival became myth. FBI break-ins matter because federal illegality was not incidental to the pursuit; it became part of the contest Burrough is reconstructing. Communiqués turn War, Crisis, and Collapse into a narrative problem: Weather preserves public visibility even as its actual organization contracts, so the public image and the real capacity diverge sharply.

The Black Liberation Army, 1973

Summary: The 1973 BLA chapter places the underground against the end of Vietnam, Watergate, the death of the Movement, Abbie Hoffman's fugitive life, urban crime panic, drugs, and a new conservative mood. Burrough follows the BLA's decline through shootings, arrests, the October 1972 Tombs jailbreak, Twymon Meyers's death, Joanne Chesimard's emergence, New Jersey and New York confrontations, and the way Black armed struggle became isolated as the broader left receded. Source anchors: 1973; Watergate; Abbie Hoffman; Twymon Meyers; Joanne Chesimard; Movement dead.

Analysis: 1973 and Watergate mark the political weather in which radical violence lost the larger audience it imagined. Abbie Hoffman and Movement dead show the old protest culture becoming memory or spectacle. Twymon Meyers and Joanne Chesimard matter because the BLA's story continues after mass radicalism fades. The chapter is also a study of Information and Coordination failure inside the state: multiple police squads and district attorneys hunt the BLA without speaking to one another while mythmaking about Chesimard ("Sister Love") outstrips the evidence — a police construction that State Capacity could neither verify nor escape.

Part Three

Summary: Part Three covers the second wave after Weather and the BLA's first peak: the Symbionese Liberation Army, Patty Hearst, the post-SLA underground, the San Francisco bombers, the death of Weather, the FALN, and Ray Levasseur's early odyssey. It centers Donald DeFreeze, Mizmoon Soltysik, Bill and Emily Harris, Kathleen Soliah, Patty Hearst, Prairie Fire, Squad 47, Oscar López, Willie Morales, and the Sam Melville Jonathan Jackson Unit. Source anchors: SLA; Patty Hearst; Donald DeFreeze; Prairie Fire; FALN; Ray Levasseur.

Analysis: SLA and Patty Hearst make Part Three the book's most media-saturated section, where underground theater and television spectacle feed each other. Donald DeFreeze and Prairie Fire show two different attempts to revive revolutionary authority after earlier defeats. FALN and Ray Levasseur widen the story beyond California celebrity into Puerto Rican nationalism and blue-collar New England militancy. The section depends throughout on Information and Coordination — media and communiqués as the movement's oxygen — and on Language and Ideology as the medium through which these groups manufactured their public selves.

The Rise of the Symbionese Liberation Army, November 1973 to February 1974

Summary: The SLA chapter begins from the claim that prisons such as Folsom, San Quentin, and Soledad produced the most embittered revolutionaries, then follows Donald DeFreeze's transformation into Cinque. Burrough traces Berkeley radicals, California prison politics, Mizmoon Soltysik, Bill and Emily Harris, Kathleen Soliah, Black Cultural Association contacts, escaped-convict charisma, and the small group's path toward kidnapping Patty Hearst, beginning with the November 6, 1973 assassination of Oakland schools superintendent Marcus Foster. Source anchors: Folsom; San Quentin; Soledad; Donald DeFreeze; Cinque; Mizmoon Soltysik.

Analysis: Folsom, San Quentin, and Soledad make the SLA a prison-radicalization story before it becomes a media circus. Donald DeFreeze's transformation into Cinque exposes Legitimacy and Succession inside the cell: white radicals hungry for Black revolutionary authenticity convert an escaped convict into a symbolic commander to borrow his racial legitimacy. Mizmoon Soltysik matters because intimate loyalty and small-group devotion sustain that authority.

The Symbionese Liberation Army, February to May 1974

Summary: Burrough reconstructs the February 4, 1974 kidnapping of Patricia Campbell Hearst from her Benvenue Avenue duplex near Berkeley, where she lived with Steven Weed after Crystal Springs and art-history studies. The chapter follows the SLA's ransom politics, food-distribution demands, communiqués, Hearst-family wealth, Donald DeFreeze, Bill and Emily Harris, the April 15, 1974 Hibernia Bank robbery, Tania, and the group's move from abduction to revolutionary spectacle. Source anchors: February 4 1974; Patty Hearst; Benvenue Avenue; Steven Weed; Hibernia Bank; Tania.

Analysis: February 4 1974 and Benvenue Avenue turn the SLA from obscure cell into national obsession. Patty Hearst and Steven Weed give the kidnapping its class and generational charge, while Hibernia Bank and Tania mark the point where captive, recruit, and propaganda symbol blur. The food-demand program — redistributing groceries to "the oppressed" of the Bay Area — is the book's clearest attempt by a terrorist group to stage a Moral Economy: a theatrical gift economy meant to purchase legitimacy. Burrough uses the episode to show how Information and Coordination, in the form of mass media, could make a tiny group feel historically enormous.

The Rise of the Post-SLA Underground

Summary: After the May 17, 1974 Los Angeles shootout, Patty Hearst and Bill and Emily Harris hear confused radio reports while driving toward Anaheim, then watch the SLA's destruction live on television — the morning raid on the rented house on West Eighty-fourth Street followed by the barricade and conflagration at 1466 East Fifty-fourth Street, where DeFreeze and five others died. The chapter follows the destruction of the original SLA, the surviving trio's shock, Kathleen Soliah's rally and recruitment, Jack Scott's later role, Los Angeles policing, Bay Area radical support, and the openings through which new militants attached themselves to the Hearst fugitives. Source anchors: May 17 1974; Anaheim; East Fifty-fourth Street; Bill Harris; Emily Harris; LAPD.

Analysis: May 17 1974 and East Fifty-fourth Street give the SLA a televised martyrdom that paradoxically helps generate a post-SLA underground. Bill Harris and Emily Harris carry guilt and bravado from the failed rescue fantasy into the next phase. LAPD and Anaheim matter because the geography of escape turns southern California media disaster into a recruitment problem for radicals elsewhere. The episode is a striking case of Information and Coordination working backward: live coverage of a movement's annihilation becomes, for a small remnant, proof that armed struggle still mattered. Kathleen Soliah turning a memorial rally into an "I am a soldier of the SLA" recruitment is Language and Ideology reconstituting a dead group through symbols.

Patty Hearst, the SLA, and the Mad Bombers of San Francisco

Summary: The chapter follows Patty Hearst's "lost year" after June 1974, when she rides with sports writer Jack Scott in a blue Ford LTD driven by his Las Vegas parents while the Harrises evade capture. Burrough connects the surviving SLA to San Francisco bombers (the New World Liberation Front), Prairie Fire's publication, Nixon's resignation, Bay Area safe houses, Jack Scott's sports-left network, the 1975 Crocker National Bank robbery in which Myrna Opsahl was killed, and Hearst's own claim that she had accepted life as an SLA soldier. Source anchors: June 1974; Jack Scott; blue Ford LTD; lost year; Prairie Fire; Myrna Opsahl.

Analysis: June 1974, Jack Scott, and the blue Ford LTD make Patty Hearst's fugitive period oddly domestic and improvised, not cinematic in the way the public imagined. Prairie Fire and San Francisco bombers connect the SLA remnant to the broader radical underground rather than leaving it as celebrity crime. The murder of Myrna Opsahl — Emily Harris dismissing her as "a bourgeois pig anyway" — strips the movement of its Moral Economy pretensions, exposing the dehumanization beneath the revolutionary language. Lost year matters because Burrough treats Hearst's identity during flight as a contested product of coercion, adaptation, and revolutionary performance, a question of Legitimacy and Succession within the cell that turned on racial authenticity (Harris mocked as an illegitimate white general).

The Death of the Weather Underground

Summary: Burrough uses the July 24, 1974 release of Prairie Fire to show Weather trying to reenter radical politics through bookstores, coffeehouses, left-wing hangouts, and a secret plan to regain leadership influence. The chapter follows the Weather Underground Organization, Prairie Fire Organizing Committee, Bernardine Dohrn, Jeff Jones, Bill Ayers, Clayton Van Lydegraf, Squad 47 pressure, ideological purges, and the implosion of clandestine authority. Source anchors: Prairie Fire; July 24 1974; Weather Underground Organization; PFOC; Clayton Van Lydegraf; Squad 47.

Analysis: Prairie Fire and July 24 1974 show Weather replacing bombs with a book while still trying to control the radical left from hiding. Weather Underground Organization and PFOC matter because the group now needs an aboveground political vehicle, but its clandestine habits poison that effort. Clayton Van Lydegraf and Squad 47 expose a War, Crisis, and Collapse failure: internal purification and federal pursuit prevent Weather from converting clandestine survival into durable politics, and the very secrecy that kept it alive also kept it from building a real following.

The FALN, 1976 to 1978

Summary: The FALN chapter begins in spring 1976, after ten anniversary bombings the previous October (including the unexploded device hidden in roses outside Standard Oil) and a lull that made the most lethal remaining group seem to vanish. Burrough follows Chicago FBI agents, the Heron Dam dynamite lead, June 1976 Chicago bombings, Oscar López, Carlos Torres, Marie Haydee Torres, Willie Morales, Puerto Rican independence networks, Mobil Oil, the 1977 Mobil bombing, and the secrecy that kept FALN operations opaque for decades. Source anchors: FALN; spring 1976; Standard Oil; Oscar López; Willie Morales; Puerto Rican independence.

Analysis: FALN and spring 1976 move the book from Weather's overdocumented world to an underground Burrough says remains unusually closed. Standard Oil and Willie Morales keep the story material, focused on bombs and bomb makers rather than communiqués alone. Oscar López and Puerto Rican independence matter because the FALN's cause, discipline, and secrecy differ sharply from Weather's student-left culture. The group's durability rests on Informal Institutions — closed cells and tight personal networks that kept even the state guessing — while its communiqués ("a free and socialist Puerto Rico, if necessary, will be written in red blood") are pure Language and Ideology, tying nationalist grievance to revolutionary rhetoric.

The Odyssey of Ray Levasseur

Summary: Ray Levasseur's first chapter opens with language about armed revolutionary love and moves to April 22, 1976 in downtown Boston. Burrough traces Levasseur's Maine background, prison radicalization, Vietnam-era politics, Cameron and Mary Bishop, courthouse and corporate targets, the Sam Melville Jonathan Jackson Unit, bombings, bank robbery logic, and the blue-collar family structure that would later become the United Freedom Front. Source anchors: Ray Levasseur; April 22 1976; Boston; Sam Melville Jonathan Jackson Unit; Cameron Bishop; armed revolutionary love.

Analysis: Ray Levasseur and April 22 1976 give the late underground a different social profile from Weather or the SLA. Boston and Cameron Bishop locate the story in New England activist and prisoner-support circles, while the Sam Melville Jonathan Jackson Unit ties Levasseur's group back to Burrough's opening genealogy. "Armed revolutionary love" makes Language and Ideology operational by authorizing bombings and robberies as care for oppressed people, showing how language converts a personal, familial ethic into a warrant for violence.

Ray Levasseur's Odyssey, Part II

Summary: The second Levasseur chapter follows Cameron Bishop to Denver on old sabotage charges while Levasseur faces a weapons charge and Mary Bishop asks him to build a defense committee. Burrough tracks Levasseur's westward organizing, legal-defense work, Pat Gros Levasseur, Tom Manning, Carol Manning, children, safe houses, prison-solidarity politics, and the gradual conversion of support work into a durable fugitive cell. Source anchors: Cameron Bishop; Denver; Mary Bishop; Pat Gros Levasseur; Tom Manning; safe houses.

Analysis: Cameron Bishop and Denver show how old cases kept radicals moving through courts and support networks long after the 1960s. Mary Bishop and Pat Gros Levasseur matter because the underground here is domestic and familial, built around spouses, children, and defense work. Tom Manning and safe houses show Informal Institutions converting Levasseur's politics into an operational community rather than a single bomber's campaign — the family cell that would eventually become the United Freedom Front.

Part Four

Summary: Part Four covers the late underground from 1977 to 1984, when Americans were trying to forget the sixties while the Family, FALN, and United Freedom Front continued robberies, jailbreaks, bombings, and killings. It brings together Mutulu Shakur, Marilyn Buck, Sekou Odinga, Silvia Baraldini, Willie Morales, Joanne Chesimard, Ray Levasseur, Tom Manning, Jaan Laaman, Richard Williams, Kazi Toure, Brink's, Nyack, and the murder of New Jersey state trooper Philip Lamonaco. Source anchors: Family; Mutulu Shakur; Marilyn Buck; Brink's; United Freedom Front; Philip Lamonaco.

Analysis: Family, Mutulu Shakur, and Marilyn Buck show the late underground becoming pan-radical, interracial, and connected to prisons, Black nationalism, and white left remnants. Brink's and United Freedom Front make Part Four more violent and more criminally consequential than any narrative of fading protest would imply. Philip Lamonaco matters because the underground's final phase includes murders that cannot be framed as symbolic property attacks. The section as a whole charts a War, Crisis, and Collapse: groups persist through Informal Institutions — interracial cells, safe houses, family networks — even as the larger revolutionary project decays into robbery, drugs, and criminal drift.

The Pan-Radical Alliance, 1977 to 1979

Summary: By 1977, Burrough writes, the SLA was memory, Patty Hearst was in prison, Weather was dying, and much of America wanted disco, cocaine, and amnesia after Vietnam, Nixon, Watergate, Hoover, riots, demonstrations, and bombings. Against that mood, radicals around Mutulu Shakur, Sekou Odinga, Marilyn Buck, Silvia Baraldini, and May 19 networks began forming a pan-radical alliance linking Black liberation, Puerto Rican independence, and white underground veterans. Source anchors: 1977; Patty Hearst; disco; Mutulu Shakur; Marilyn Buck; May 19.

Analysis: 1977 and Patty Hearst mark public exhaustion with the underground era, while disco captures Burrough's sense of cultural flight from politics. Mutulu Shakur and Marilyn Buck show that serious organizing continued after the audience left. May 19 acts as an Informal Institutions bridge: it connects causes and personnel — Black nationalism, Puerto Rican independence, white left veterans — that earlier groups had kept more separate, turning a loose alliance into a functioning conspiracy.

The Family and the FALN, 1979−80

Summary: This chapter centers on the 1979-1980 intersection of the Family and the FALN, especially two jailbreaks: the still-obscure rescue of bomb maker Willie Morales from Bellevue Hospital's prison ward in May 1979, and the November 2, 1979 escape of Joanne Chesimard from New Jersey prison, engineered with Sekou Odinga's smuggled gun. Burrough names Marilyn Buck, Sekou Odinga, Susan Tipograph, Oscar López's FALN world, Morales's injuries, hospital custody, planning over weeks, radical lawyers, and the FBI/police conviction that the escapes joined Puerto Rican militants to Shakur's interracial underground. Source anchors: Willie Morales; Marilyn Buck; Sekou Odinga; Susan Tipograph; FALN; Family; November 1979.

Analysis: Willie Morales and FALN give the chapter its practical trigger: an injured bomb maker whose custody mattered to an entire clandestine network. The Bellevue and Chesimard escapes are feats of Informal Institutions — doctors' visits, smuggled tools, lawyers, staged diversions combining into a jailbreak that the state could not prevent, exposing gaps in State Capacity. Marilyn Buck and Sekou Odinga show the Family functioning as a connector across racial and ideological lines. Susan Tipograph matters because Burrough is careful about accusation, legal representation, and the ambiguous boundary between radical defense work and operational assistance.

Trials, Surrenders, and the Family, 1980−81

Summary: After Joanne Chesimard's jailbreak and the arrival of the 1980s, Burrough follows trials, surrenders, and the Family's internal corruption through cocaine, money, discipline problems, and criminal drift. Mutulu Shakur, Marilyn Buck, Sekou Odinga, Tyrone Rison, Silvia Baraldini, May 19, New York radicals, and law-enforcement pressure appear in a chapter where revolutionary language struggles to contain robbery proceeds, drug use, and personal mistrust, culminating in the armored-car robberies that precede the Brinks "Big Dance." Source anchors: Joanne Chesimard; 1980s; cocaine; Mutulu Shakur; Tyrone Rison; Silvia Baraldini.

Analysis: Joanne Chesimard and 1980s place the Family at the border between legendary jailbreak and decaying clandestine practice. Cocaine and Tyrone Rison turn War, Crisis, and Collapse inward: internal appetites destroy cells as effectively as FBI work. Mutulu Shakur and Silvia Baraldini matter because ideological commitment remains real, but money, secrecy, and suspicion damage the group carrying it — the underground's idealist Language and Ideology now existing alongside outright criminals within the same cells.

The United Freedom Front, 1981 to 1984

Summary: The United Freedom Front chapter begins with an October 4, 1981 Maine Sunday-Telegram article asking where the terrorist bombers were, just before Brink's and before Ray Levasseur's group reemerged larger and more committed. Burrough follows Levasseur, Tom Manning, Pat Gros Levasseur, Carol Manning, Jaan Laaman, Richard Williams, Kazi Toure, bank robberies, bombings, children in clandestine life, Len Cross, and the 1981 murder of New Jersey trooper Philip Lamonaco. Source anchors: October 4 1981; Maine Sunday-Telegram; Ray Levasseur; Tom Manning; Brink's; Philip Lamonaco.

Analysis: October 4 1981 and Maine Sunday-Telegram show how close public curiosity came to the underground just before its last violent resurgence. Ray Levasseur and Tom Manning turn the Sam Melville Jonathan Jackson Unit into the United Freedom Front, a more durable Informal Institutions-based cell built around three couples and nine children — the family as the final organizational form of a movement that began in mass protest. The recruitments of Laaman, Williams, and Toure revive "armed revolutionary love," and Brink's and Philip Lamonaco connect the late underground to deaths that forced Americans to recognize the movement had not vanished, however anachronistic its revolution had become.

EPILOGUE

Summary: The epilogue takes place in November 2010 at the Malcolm X and Dr. Betty Shabazz Center in Harlem, where two hundred people gather for Marilyn Buck's memorial after her cancer death shortly after release from federal prison. Cathy Wilkerson sits with Elizabeth Fink, old Weather, BLA, FALN, Family, and May 19 figures appear, and Burrough uses the room to show how the underground remembered itself through loyalty, grief, silence, and unresolved disagreement. He weighs the movement's legacy — no tangible political victory, only metal detectors and bomb-sniffing dogs — against veterans' insistence that the struggle mattered, and notes the counter-campaign of victims like Joseph Connor, whose father was killed at Fraunces Tavern. Source anchors: November 2010; Shabazz Center; Marilyn Buck; Cathy Wilkerson; Elizabeth Fink; Harlem.

Analysis: November 2010 and the Shabazz Center bring the book's fugitives, lawyers, and survivors into one commemorative room. Marilyn Buck and Cathy Wilkerson connect the late Family world back to Weather's opening memory, while Elizabeth Fink represents the small radical-lawyer network that made Burrough's research possible. Harlem matters because the memorial keeps Black liberation memory central rather than letting the book end with white Weather nostalgia. The epilogue is the fullest statement of Information and Coordination as historical method and as self-deception: a group that never won publicly still sustains itself through shared narrative, while Language and Ideology — the word "armed struggle" spoken or unspoken — determines how the veterans, and their victims, frame what they did.

A NOTE ON SOURCES

Summary: Burrough's source note explains that Days of Rage rests on previously published books, contemporary newspapers and magazines, personal interviews, and the hard limits of FBI files. He discusses Public Enemies, redacted airtels and telegrams, Weather literature, SLA books, the lack of books on the BLA, FALN, and United Freedom Front, John Castellucci's The Big Dance, cold-calling veterans, and the small group of radical lawyers in New York, Chicago, and San Francisco who helped him reach former underground figures. Source anchors: FBI files; airtels; Public Enemies; The Big Dance; radical lawyers; personal interviews.

Analysis: FBI files and airtels explain why Burrough's book is not simply an archival synthesis; the official record is too partial and redacted. Public Enemies and The Big Dance identify the prior models and gaps he had to work around. Radical lawyers and personal interviews expose an Information and Coordination problem for historical reconstruction: Burrough recovers operational detail through negotiated trust with people who had spent decades avoiding exactly this kind of disclosure, which is why so many claims rest on memory rather than documents.

Useful details and retrieval cues

Concrete, extract-anchored markers for recall and cross-referencing:

  • Dates and places to anchor the timeline: Townhouse explosion March 6, 1970, at 18 West Eleventh Street, Greenwich Village (killed Terry Robbins, Diana Oughton, Ted Gold; survivors Cathy Wilkerson and Kathy Boudin; target was the Fort Dix dance). NYPD headquarters bombing June 9, 1970, at Centre Street. U.S. Capitol bombing March 1, 1971. Weather's three "dream" targets: Pentagon (hit), Capitol (hit), White House (only cased).
  • The underground's scale vs. lethality: more than 2,500 U.S. bombings counted by the FBI in an eighteen-month stretch of 1971-72, nearly five a day, yet under 1 percent were fatal — bombs functioned as "exploding press releases." Commuted: the movement's perceived size (hundreds) far exceeded its real core (often barely a dozen).
  • SLA markers: Marcus Foster assassination November 6, 1973. Patty Hearst kidnapped February 4, 1974, from Benvenue Avenue, Berkeley. Hibernia Bank robbery April 15, 1974 (Noriega Street, the "Tania" photo). Los Angeles shootout May 17, 1974, at 1466 East Fifty-fourth Street — six SLA members dead, Patty and the Harrises watching live; the rental house on West Eighty-fourth Street had been raided that morning. April 21, 1975 Crocker National robbery in Sacramento killed Myrna Opsahl.
  • The Family/Brinks: Joanne Chesimard (Assata Shakur) jailbreak November 2, 1979, with Sekou Odinga's smuggled gun. Willie Morales escaped Bellevue's prison ward May 21, 1979. Brinks "Big Dance" October 20, 1981, at the Nanuet Mall killed guards Peter Paige and Joe Trombino; the Nyack shootout killed Waverly Brown and Sgt. Edward O'Grady and captured David Gilbert and Kathy Boudin. William Moroney was killed in an earlier Bronx armored-car job June 2, 1981. Trooper Philip Lamonaco murdered December 21, 1981, by Manning and/or Williams.
  • FALN markers: Fraunces Tavern bombing January 24, 1975 (source of Joseph Connor's father's death); ten "anniversary" bombs October 27, 1975, across New York, Washington, and Chicago (the unexploded Standard Oil device); June 7, 1976 Chicago bombs; late-October "Operation Catch a Bomber" dragnet bust; the Mobil Oil bombing August 3, 1977 (Marie Haydee Torres's fingerprint); the dynamite traced to the Heron Dam in New Mexico.
  • United Freedom Front markers: Levasseur and Manning's Waterbury, Connecticut, bank job March 2, 1978, after following Brinks/Purolator routes; the Mobil Oil office bombing near New York in early 1979; reemergence in 1981 with recruits Richard Williams, Jaan Laaman, and Kazi Toure; first bank of the rebuilt unit June 25, 1981, in New Britain, Connecticut.
  • Recurring imagery: communiqués left in pay phones and hidden niches (e.g., beneath the José Martí statue); bombs in bouquets of roses and umbrellas; false identities built from dead infants' birth records; the family cell as the underground's final form; Cathy Wilkerson's "You were the West Coast bomb maker" as the book's framing intimate reveal.
  • Key retrieval contrast: Burrough's stated thesis — the underground was never mainly anti-Vietnam but mainly about race and Black liberation; it was "race first, always first" — is a constant across nearly every chapter.
