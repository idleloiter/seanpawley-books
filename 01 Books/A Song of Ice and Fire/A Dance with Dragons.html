A Dance with Dragons

Buy on Amazon — A Dance with Dragons

Position in the vault

A Dance with Dragons is the fifth volume of A Song of Ice and Fire. It runs partly in parallel with A Feast For Crows, then overtakes it, rejoining the timeline while concentrating on rule under impossible conditions: Jon at the Wall, Daenerys in Meereen, Tyrion in exile and capture, and the northern and Essosi successor conflicts moving toward fresh collision. As the series hub records, this is the volume where governance, frontier strain, and the delayed consequences of earlier legitimacy breakdown become impossible to postpone.

Detailed overview

This is the series' densest book on governance as burden rather than prize. Many earlier volumes asked who had the right to rule; this one asks what rule actually feels like once the throne, city, castle, or command is in your hands and every available option is poisoned. Jon Snow, Daenerys Targaryen, and to a lesser extent Cersei, Stannis's lieutenants, and the Meereenese factions all face versions of the same problem: Legitimacy and Succession does not solve administration. Oaths, victories, prophecy, bloodline, dragonfire, and acclaim can bring one to power, but they do not eliminate provisioning, faction management, intelligence gaps, hostile elites, divided constituencies, winter, plague, or siege. The book therefore turns Crisis Governance into a sustained test of whether rulers can convert claims into working systems.

Jon's arc is one of the novel's moral and institutional centers. As Lord Commander, he tries to act on the real shape of the northern emergency rather than the fantasies or resentments of his subordinates. He treats the wildlings as necessary political partners because the Others make old distinctions irrelevant, and he tries to make the Night's Watch into a functioning Frontier Systems institution rather than a nostalgic fraternity of grievance. This is exactly why he becomes vulnerable. Jon sees further than many around him, but the organization he leads is full of men whose identities depend on older categories. His tragedy in this book is not that he is wrong about the threat. It is that acting correctly against the real threat destabilizes the symbolic order his own brothers still live inside.

Daenerys's Meereen arc is the other major governance laboratory. Earlier books allowed her to act as liberator and miracle-bearer; this one subjects that identity to administration, insurgency, blockade, disease, marriage politics, and the moral ambiguity of occupation. Meereen is where the series tests whether justice backed by dragons can become durable order. The answer is neither simple success nor total failure. Daenerys repeatedly shows moral seriousness, political patience, and strategic creativity, but she is also trapped by incomplete information, competing loyalties, erotic and emotional pressure, and the sheer inertia of urban social systems built on slavery. Her dragons magnify her symbolic authority while also threatening her ability to govern coherently. Martin uses her to show that emancipatory conquest needs State Capacity, not merely righteous violence.

Tyrion's line becomes a study in deracination, performance, and intellectual survival. Cast out from the House Lannister structure after killing Tywin and Shae, he moves through disguise, slavery, manipulation, and factional transport while continuing to read political structures with extraordinary acuity. His bitterness is deeper than before, but so is his capacity to see how Information and Coordination works when one has no secure place in the system. Tyrion in this book is both degraded and clarified. He no longer has a city to defend or a family role to inhabit, so he becomes even more purely interpretive, though no less compromised.

The northern political lines beyond Jon deepen the series' interest in false kingship, remembered loyalties, and trauma-made identity. Reek/Theon is one of the book's most brutal achievements. Martin takes a character already guilty of betrayal and subjects him to annihilating psychological domination, then slowly reconstitutes him as a traumatized witness. His chapters are painful, but they are also structurally important: they show what rule under Ramsay and Bolton logic looks like from inside. It is Coercive Labor and terror without legitimacy, coercion without stable order, cruelty as atmosphere.

The "secondary" POVs and titled chapters matter because this is a book of distributed instability. Quentyn, Barristan-related Meereen politics, Jon Connington, Aegon/Young Griff, Victarion, Davos in the north, Melisandre at the Wall, and the Ironborn/Martell spillovers all widen the sense that the world is converging on new claimants and new kinds of crisis. The Aegon line is especially important because it reopens the question of Targaryen restoration in a form that is militarily plausible, emotionally ambiguous, and ideologically disruptive. Daenerys is no longer the only possible dragon restoration story, and the Golden Company turns restoration into Financial Infrastructure as much as bloodline.

The book's title is therefore about more than literal dragons. It signals competing Targaryen futures, clashing sovereign imaginations, and unstable coordination around symbols of rule that may no longer be containable. By the end, Jon has been cut down by his own men, Daenerys has vanished from Meereen into a more elemental dragon relation, Tyrion is embedded near a new battlefront, Stannis is still in play in the north, Cersei has partially re-entered the board, and multiple successions remain radically unsettled. A Dance with Dragons ends not by resolving these pressures but by increasing them. It is the series' great deferred-explosion book.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Jon Snow: wants to save the Wall and the realm by acting on the real threat, even when that means violating his brothers' expectations.
  • Daenerys Targaryen: wants to rule justly and hold together liberation, peace, and sovereignty, but keeps facing the limits of dragon-backed idealism under urban complexity.
  • Tyrion Lannister: wants survival, revenge, and some future role worth his intelligence, even as exile strips away stable identity.
  • Theon / Reek: wants relief from terror and eventually some fragment of selfhood after near-total psychological destruction.
  • Davos Seaworth: wants to serve Stannis well while continuing to prefer truth and the living realm over rigid symbolic claims.
  • Melisandre: wants to interpret and direct history through prophecy, sacrifice, and the red god's will.
  • The secondary Essosi and Targaryen-claimant lines (Quentyn, Victarion, Barristan, Jon Connington and Aegon): want position, restoration, vengeance, or advantage amid rapidly changing great-power conditions.

Major linkages

  • A Feast For Crows: the parallel aftermath volume that this book eventually overtakes and rejoins.
  • A Game Of Thrones, A Clash of Kings, A Storm of Swords: the earlier volumes whose legitimacy breakdowns, debts, feuds, and frontier warnings this book reaps.
  • A Song of Ice and Fire: the series hub, whose "included volumes" and "volume-by-volume arc" place this book as the governance-and-frontier strain volume.
  • House Stark: northern loyalty, the false Arya, the search for a surviving Stark, and Jon's blood-stained legitimacy.
  • House Lannister: Cersei's fall, Tyrion's exile, Kevan's regency, and the regime's fiscal survival.
  • Iron Throne Regime: the crown as a strained center losing writ while the Faith, the Iron Bank, Dorne, and the Golden Company pull at its margins.
  • Night's Watch: the frontier institution whose neutrality carries the book's moral and strategic weight.
  • Seeing Like a State: especially useful for Jon and Daenerys, both of whom discover the brutal difference between moral intention and institutional execution.
  • Dune Messiah and Children of Dune: useful for comparing prophecy, legitimacy, messianic burden, and the instability of charismatic rule.
  • Use of Weapons: relevant for Tyrion and Theon where damaged identity, violence, and self-narration dominate.
  • The Expanse: strong linkage on command under impossible logistics, factional fragmentation, and the burden of holding systems together.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Crisis Governance: Jon, Daenerys, Barristan, Stannis's officers, and Kevan all govern amid emergencies that punish delay and turn compromise into visible vulnerability.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: Daenerys, Aegon, Stannis, Tommen, Hizdahr, the Boltons, and the Faith each show that claims must become institutions or they collapse into theater.
  • State Capacity: Meereen, Castle Black, White Harbor, Winterfell, and King's Landing reveal who can feed, police, investigate, transport, tax, and enforce rather than merely command.
  • Information and Coordination: Tyrion's exile, Bran's greenseer training, Davos's mission, Jon's letters and Hardhome decisions, and Varys's epilogue all turn knowledge movement into power.
  • Logistics and Throughput: winter, Slaver's Bay routes, Volantis ships, northern snow, Wall stores, and Ironborn voyages decide which political plans can physically survive.
  • Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy: Reek, Blind Beth, Young Griff, the Lost Lord, and the Queen's Hand show identity as an imposed operating role under institutional pressure.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: Melisandre, the Faith, king's blood, sacrifice, and public penance show belief systems competing with crown and command authority.
  • Coercive Labor: slavery in Slaver's Bay and Volantis, and the psychological captivity of Reek, run as forced-work and forced-identity systems.
  • War, Crisis, and Collapse: the book distributes multiple wars and collapses in parallel rather than resolving them.
  • Elite Formation: marriage alliances, wardship, hostages, and restoration companies show how ruling classes reproduce or fracture.
  • Collapse and Resilience: institutions persist as brittle structures while their capacity to solve problems drains.

Core concepts

  • Legitimacy and Succession: the book advances this concept by testing several claimants (Daenerys, Aegon, Stannis, Tommen, the Boltons, Hizdahr) against the practical work of holding power, showing that a claim is only as durable as the administration behind it.
  • State Capacity: Meereen and the Wall are the two sustained laboratories; both rulers learn that feeding, policing, and coordinating outweigh the symbolic authority that won them office.
  • Crisis Governance: every frontline ruler governs with a short decision horizon under harvest failure, blockade, siege, plague, and internal betrayal.
  • Information and Coordination: rumor, letters, prophecy, espionage, and hidden transport are repeatedly shown to be decisive levers that outrank formal command.
  • Frontier Systems: the Night's Watch and the north beyond the Wall model a neglected frontier whose institutional weakness becomes a civilization-scale threat.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: Melisandre's R'hllor cult, the Faith's penance politics, and king's-blood sacrifice show belief moving into the vacuum left by weak monarchy.
  • Coercive Labor: the anti-slavery experiment in Meereen and the slave economies of Slaver's Bay and Volantis frame abolition, emancipation, and forced-identity systems.
  • War, Crisis, and Collapse: the book shows several simultaneous wars and collapses reinforcing one another rather than resolving into a single settlement.
  • Logistics and Throughput: winter stores, siege supply, ship capacity, and long-distance movement defeat multiple ambitious political plans.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Prologue: Varamyr Sixskins

Summary: Varamyr Sixskins, a powerful skinchanger who once sat beside Mance Rayder, is dying in a hut beyond the Wall after fleeing the battle at the Wall. His pack of wolves, hunting a family of refugees, slaughter them; Varamyr, too weak to stop them, reflects on his past abominations: eating men, stealing Haggon's beasts and his second life, and coveting Jon Snow's direwolf. When Thistle, his last companion, returns to warn of pursuers, Varamyr forces his spirit into her body. She fights him, bites out her own tongue, and claws out her eyes; his true death comes as his spirit flees. In a final vision he sees the risen dead walking with pale blue light in their eye sockets.
Analysis: The prologue widens the series' metaphysical frame by making skinchanging, death, and predatory continuity concrete. Varamyr's arc is a study in the failure of coercive mastery over living things: he treats every bond—Haggon, his beasts, the women he takes—as property, which is why Coercive Labor and predation hollow out his humanity. The rules he breaks (eating human meat, mating wolf with wolf, seizing another man's body) are exactly the rules Bran is also bending. The final vision of blue-eyed wights walking among the snow buries is the clearest statement yet that the true enemy is civilizational: this is War, Crisis, and Collapse at the species level, and the frontier institution meant to face it, the Night's Watch, is elsewhere. The prologue also binds skinchanging to Knowledge Preservation and memory: the dead keep watching, which foreshadows Bran's greenseer training.
Source anchors: Varamyr Sixskins; Haggon; Thistle; skinchanger; wight; weirwood; Jon Snow's direwolf; the Wall

Tyrion I

Summary: Tyrion, having murdered Tywin and Shae, is smuggled out of King's Landing by Varys in a wine cask and drinks his way across the narrow sea, haunted by his father's last words, "Wherever whores go." He is delivered to Illyrio Mopatis's manse in Pentos, where the magister offers him a bath, rich clothes, wine, and a role in a grand restoration scheme built around a three-headed dragon. Tyrion is wary but interested, recognizing that Illyrio and Varys mean to use him for profit.
Analysis: The chapter establishes Tyrion's deracination and his new status as pure intelligence without place. His obsessive refrain about whores and his memory of Tysha's rape show that exile has stripped the Lannister armor without healing the damage. Illyrio's offer turns restoration into a commercial and intelligence transaction, linking succession to Financial Infrastructure rather than blood alone. Tyrion's distrust—"any friend of Varys the Spider is someone I will trust just as far as I can throw him"—frames the hidden coordination layer of Information and Coordination that drives the whole Aegon plot. His fall from the House Lannister structure exemplifies how Elite Formation can unmake a man at the same time it made him.
Source anchors: Illyrio Mopatis; Varys; Tysha; Tywin's death; Pentos; wine cask; "wherever whores go"

Daenerys I

Summary: In Meereen, Daenerys confronts the first sustained phase of anti-slavery governance. She learns that her council is divided, that the freedmen struggle to organize, and that her justice—even when righteous—creates new enemies among the former masters. She hears reports of the Sons of the Harpy's violence and of slavers' machinations across Slaver's Bay, and she tries to rule as a queen rather than a conqueror, weighing mercy against the cost of enforcing it.
Analysis: This chapter establishes Meereen as Daenerys's great State Capacity test: not conquest but rule through investigators, patrols, food distribution, faction management, and credible justice. Martin shows that her symbolic authority as Mother of Dragons does not feed a city or suppress insurgency. The chapter advances Legitimacy and Succession by contrasting her claim with the practical hostility of a slaveholding elite who will not accept her writ. Her slow, morally serious struggle against the War, Crisis, and Collapse already radiating from Slaver's Bay previews the occupation's central tension: justice requires institutions, and institutions require time she may not have.
Source anchors: Meereen; Sons of the Harpy; freedmen; shavepates; Unsullied; Daenerys; slavery

Jon I

Summary: Jon, as the new Lord Commander, assigns hostile brothers to ranging and castle duties, then refuses to give Stannis the Night's Watch castles or place black brothers under the king's knights. Stannis threatens Jon with beheading and gives him a deadline to man the abandoned forts along the Wall, while Melisandre warns of enemies, ice, and daggers around him.
Analysis: The chapter makes institutional independence inseparable from personal exposure. Jon's refusal to bend the Watch to Stannis defends the order's neutrality, but it also sets him against the only ruler currently helping the frontier. This is Frontier Systems in its starkest form: the Night's Watch must hold the Wall for all of Westeros while lacking the resources and political protection to do so. Jon begins converting State Capacity problems—manning forts, keeping order, resisting capture by an allied king—into decisions that accumulate enemies. Melisandre's warning of "daggers in the dark" foreshadows his fate and ties the chapter to Religion and Legitimacy and prophecy.
Source anchors: Jon Snow; Stannis; Melisandre; Night's Watch; the Wall; abandoned castles; neutrality

Bran I

Summary: Bran, Meera, Jojen, Hodor, and Summer shelter in an abandoned wildling village while wights close in. Coldhands and his ravens rescue them; Bran notices that Coldhands's hands are dead and black, confirming that their guide is one of the monsters Old Nan warned about. Jojen insists they must follow him anyway to reach the last greenseer.
Analysis: The chapter deepens the moral uncertainty of Bran's quest. To reach the source of knowledge and sight, Bran must trust a supernatural protector whom the Wall's own categories mark as an enemy. This is the series' recurring theme that survival beyond the frontier requires breaking the very distinctions (living/dead, friend/enemy) that civilization depends on. It connects to Knowledge Preservation, since the greenseer's cave is a reservoir of the world's memory, and to Frontier Systems, since Bran's party is moving beyond the reach of any institution. The bond with Coldhands foreshadows the larger revelation that the "enemy" is not a simple other.

Epilogue

Summary: Ser Kevan Lannister, governing King's Landing as regent, is killed by Varys, who reveals his loyalty to Aegon Targaryen and explains that he has been deliberately mismanaging the realm to widen the field for a perfect Targaryen restoration. Analysis: The epilogue clarifies that hidden actors have been shaping the realm toward a new succession crisis rather than any settlement. Varys's cold speech—that he killed Kevan because Kevan was too competent, too likely to actually heal the realm, and that chaos is a ladder to Aegon's throne—reframes the whole book's governance as a stage managed from the shadows. This makes Information and Coordination the decisive lever: assassination and misinformation serve a restoration strategy that prefers a wounded, desperate realm over a stable one. It ties the book's themes of Legitimacy and Succession, Iron Throne Regime, and War, Crisis, and Collapse into a single ominous resolution.
