Detailed overview

Richard J. Reid's Political Power in Pre-Colonial Buganda is an economic and military history of Ganda state power before British rule. Its core claim is that Buganda's nineteenth-century strength rested on the kingdom's ability to mobilize land, crops, labour, roads, slaves, cloth, iron, ivory, cattle, canoes, and armed men for three connected purposes: profit, internal cohesion, and external security. The kabaka matters throughout, but Reid does not reduce the kingdom to royal biography. He asks how material systems made kingship effective and how those systems began to fail before colonial occupation.

The book begins by pushing back against accounts that foreground the late nineteenth-century "wars of religion" as if Christian and Muslim factionalism were the primary explanation for Buganda's transformation. Reid treats those wars as important, but as symptoms of deeper tensions in commerce, military command, succession politics, public labour, food supply, cattle disease, and the arrival of long-distance trade. The secondary literature is useful to him, but he repeatedly returns to indigenous traditions, missionary observations, travellers such as Speke and Stanley, colonial archives, clan histories, and material practices.

Part One grounds Ganda power in livelihood. Bananas, sweet potatoes, yams, beans, famine foods such as kigomba, cattle, Hima herdsmen, hunting, ivory, fishing, barkcloth, iron, pottery, and specialized clans all appear as political facts. Reid's Buganda is not a simple "banana kingdom" whose ecology automatically produces centralization. Plantations, estates, pastoral imports, cattle distributions, barkcloth production in Buddu, blacksmith privileges, and lake or river economies are mechanisms through which wealth and authority are made visible and transferable.

Part Two turns to labour and coercion. Taxation in local produce, state-organized labour, road building, the highways converging on the capital, Mwanga's abuse of older obligations, slave acquisition, women in slavery, and slave hierarchies explain how the kingdom turned human mobility into state capacity. Reid stresses both centralization and decentralization: chiefs, saza authorities, messengers, local producers, and enslaved foreigners all participate in systems that bind the capital to the countryside without making every initiative purely royal.

Part Three examines commerce from local markets to coastal connections. Cowry shells, barkcloth, cotton cloth, iron goods, foodstuffs, cattle, ivory, slaves, Arab and Swahili merchants, Zanzibar, Tabora, Bunyoro, Busoga, and lake-shore exchange show Buganda's commercial position changing after 1844. Reid's point is not that foreign trade created Ganda power from outside. Rather, imported goods and coastal merchants intensified older structures, gave the kabaka new opportunities to intervene, and exposed the kingdom to dependence when ivory declined and slaves became the export most available.

Part Four connects war, organization, weaponry, and Lake Victoria. Reid reconstructs military ethos, coronation regalia, the memory of Nakibinge, Kamanya's expansion, Suna's and Mutesa's campaigns, command structures, pages and bodyguards, guns, powder shortages, the myasi, canoe construction, Sesse labour, the gabunga, ports, and the fleet. The conclusion is deliberately ambivalent: Buganda innovated impressively, especially on the lake, but by the 1880s food shortage, rinderpest, trypanosomiasis, ivory depletion, slave trading, military overreach, and religious-political factionalism had weakened the material base on which the pre-colonial state depended.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter One: Introduction

Summary: Reid introduces Buganda as a political and territorial kingdom on the northern shore of Lake Victoria, organized around the kabaka, saza chiefs, clan traditions, a substantial kinglist, and expansion at the expense of neighbours such as Bunyoro. He sets the late nineteenth-century wars of religion and the establishment of colonial rule inside a longer history rather than treating Protestant, Catholic, Muslim, and British interventions as the whole story. The chapter reviews secondary literature, missionary and traveller sources, Kagwa, Kalibala's translation, IBEAC material, and the need to examine how the Ganda state used human and material resources for profit, cohesion, and security. Source anchors: kabaka; Lake Victoria; Bunyoro; wars of religion; Kagwa; Kalibala; IBEAC; human and material resources.

Analysis: The kabaka, Lake Victoria, Bunyoro, and wars of religion define the scale of Reid's correction. Kagwa, Kalibala, and IBEAC show why the book must read sources critically rather than simply repeat either colonial or nationalist narratives. The human and material resources formula supplies the organizing mechanism for the whole monograph: politics is to be explained through crops, labour, trade, and war.

Chapter Two: Land and Cultivation

Summary: The chapter examines Buganda's agricultural base, especially the banana, sweet potato, yams, beans, peas, plantations on hill slopes, streams, swamps, Stanley's 1875 observations, famine foods such as kigomba, and the agricultural collapse of the 1880s and 1890s. Reid treats the "kingdom of the banana" carefully: the banana facilitated surplus, permanent settlement, dense population, and chieftaincy, but it did not make the state automatic. He ties Kintu traditions, hill estates, local foresight, food shortage, political turmoil, and plantation destruction to the changing capacity of the state. Source anchors: banana; sweet potato; Stanley 1875; kigomba; Kintu; hill estates; food shortage; 1880s.

Analysis: Banana, sweet potato, Kintu, and 1880s food shortage show agriculture doing political work. Stanley 1875 and hill estates provide evidence for both abundance and hierarchy, while kigomba reveals planning for scarcity. Reid uses crop history to show that Buganda's state capacity rested on a productive base that could expand, but also one that religious war, labour disruption, and famine could damage.

Chapter Three: Herdsmen and Hunters

Summary: Reid reconstructs pastoralism, Hima herdsmen, cattle in politics and society, livestock consumption, rinderpest, trypanosomiasis, hunting, ivory, fishing, crocodiles, Lake Victoria, river economies, and the "waters of Ganda history." Cattle were not as central to Buganda as to some East African societies, but herds mattered for chiefs, the kabaka, food, trade, and symbols of wealth. Hunting and fishing link the kingdom to elephants, ivory exports, dogs, buffalo, antelope, Sesse islanders, riverine knowledge, and ecological crises when elephants disappeared and disease struck cattle. Source anchors: Hima; cattle; rinderpest; trypanosomiasis; ivory; elephants; Lake Victoria; Sesse.

Analysis: Hima, cattle, ivory, and Lake Victoria widen the material base beyond bananas. Rinderpest and trypanosomiasis explain why late nineteenth-century crisis cannot be read only through religion or British intrusion. Elephants and Sesse fishing also connect ecology to commerce: the loss of ivory and the management of water resources changed the kingdom's external reach.

Chapter Four: Crafts and Craftsmen

Summary: This chapter covers barkcloth production, fig trees, bark-stripping, mallets, the ngonge clan, Kimera traditions, Buddu, iron, the kkobe and ngabi clans, blacksmiths, firearm repair, pottery, and the uneven decline of barkcloth after imported textiles and British rule. Reid treats craft skill as a political resource: barkcloth gave Buganda commercial reach, while iron and pottery carried unusual cultural prestige. The chapter argues that territorial expansion toward Buddu and the west was tied to access to barkcloth, metal, and skilled producers, not simply to abstract conquest. Source anchors: barkcloth; fig trees; ngonge clan; Kimera; Buddu; kkobe; ngabi; firearm repair.

Analysis: Barkcloth, Buddu, ngabi, and firearm repair show how craft economies underwrote power. The ngonge and kkobe traditions are not quaint origin stories; Reid uses them to trace how specialist production became state wealth. Firearm repair is especially important because older blacksmith prestige adapted to imported military technology without losing its local social meaning.

Chapter Five: Labour and Taxation

Summary: Reid turns to the state's human resources through taxation, saza chiefs such as the Sekibobo of Chagwe, royal messengers, local produce, state-organized labour, road construction, the highways of Buganda, and Mwanga's abuse of older obligations. Taxes in produce and labour linked governed and governing, while roads connected the capital to frontiers, markets, and military routes. The chapter also shows that public labour could produce cohesion when recognized as legitimate, but could become politically destructive when exploited under Mwanga. Source anchors: Sekibobo; Chagwe; taxation; royal messengers; public labour; highways; Mwanga; roads.

Analysis: Sekibobo, taxation, highways, and Mwanga make the chapter a case for State Capacity, because Reid treats Ganda power as the practical conversion of produce and labour into roads, messengers, and military reach rather than as sovereignty in the abstract. Roads move goods and armies, but they also demonstrate that the kabaka and saza chiefs can command labour across territory, which is exactly the gap between proclamation and execution that state capacity names. Mwanga's abuse matters because it reveals the limit of this system: obligations that once expressed cohesion could become evidence of royal predation, so the same channels that carried capacity could carry collapse when reciprocity broke.

Chapter Six: Slavery

Summary: The chapter analyzes slavery as an old and elaborate institution in Buganda, including acquisition of foreign captives, women in slavery, slave hierarchies, domestic service, missionary reports by Pilkington and C. T. Wilson, slave communities, and the increasing complexity caused by long-distance slave exports. Reid emphasizes that most slaves were foreign, which made slavery part of Buganda's effort to internalize external control. The chapter connects slaves to labour, households, status, markets, and the late nineteenth-century scramble for trade goods. Source anchors: foreign captives; women; slave hierarchies; Pilkington; C. T. Wilson; domestic service; slave exports; trade goods.

Analysis: Foreign captives, women, Pilkington, and slave exports make slavery central to Reid's political economy, and the chapter is a case for Coercive Labor because Reid reads bondage through constrained exit rather than through legal category. Most slaves were foreign captives, which let the kingdom internalize external control by turning outsiders into usable human resources whose refusal cost them place, protection, and survival. Women in slavery and slave hierarchies show that the institution organized households and status as well as fields and trade. Its expansion into export commerce mattered because it weakened internal cohesion at the same time that it supplied the goods coastal traders desired, so coercion became the channel through which Buganda converted captive bodies into both labour and import purchasing power.

Chapter Seven: Domestic and Regional Trade

Summary: Reid rejects the European claim that coastal merchants introduced meaningful commerce to Buganda. Emin Pasha instead finds Ganda barkcloth, mats, copper wire, and services circulating through Rubaga and Kabarega's capital; Buddu traders later carry enough barkcloth to Ankole that a 1904 restriction damages the district's prosperity. Dried fish, salt from Kibiro, iron and imported hoes, gun repair, cattle, and spearheads move through overlapping domestic and regional routes. Markets remain locally supervised: chiefs authorize sites and collect dues, while the kabaka takes revenue and periodically tries to tighten control. Cowries coexist with barter and lose value as imports increase. Source anchors: Emin Pasha; Rubaga; Buddu; Kibiro; market dues; cowries.

Analysis: The commodity evidence changes the model of Ganda power from royal extraction alone to commercially productive decentralization. Buddu's barkcloth producers and the traders using Kibiro salt routes create wealth without direct metropolitan management; market dues then allow landholding chiefs and the kabaka to capture part of activity they did not organize. Specialized gun repair and the import of harder Busoga hoes also show trade transmitting skills and productive capacity, not merely luxuries. Mutesa's late attempt to monopolize ivory and force exchange into the capital market raises food prices, demonstrating the limit of royal control: Buganda's strength came from letting older local networks operate, then taxing their movement.

Chapter Eight: The Growth of Long-Distance Trade

Summary: The chapter treats 1844 as a landmark, when Arab and Swahili traders reached Buganda and intensified demand for imported cloth, guns, beads, luxury goods, ivory, and slaves. Reid discusses coastal merchants, Zanzibar, Tabora, Bunyoro, Busoga, O'Flaherty, Burton's "Isa bin Hosayn," Speke's "Eseau," and the kabaka's attempts to control favourable trading conditions. As ivory inside Buganda declined, slaves became more prominent, and the kingdom's leverage over merchants weakened. Source anchors: 1844; Arab traders; Zanzibar; Tabora; ivory; slaves; O'Flaherty; Busoga.

Analysis: 1844, Zanzibar, ivory, and slaves mark a change in scale rather than a brand-new economy. Reid uses Arab traders and Tabora to show Buganda entering wider circuits from a position of strength, but the decline of ivory exposes a vulnerability. Busoga and O'Flaherty matter because tribute claims and export pressures reveal how commerce pushed the state to assert authority beyond its core.

Chapter Nine: The Rise and Decline of Ganda Military Power

Summary: Reid reconstructs military ethos, regalia, coronation rituals, Nakibinge's remembered struggle, Kamanya's "restless warfare," Suna, Mutesa, frontier posts, regional conquest, and military decline before colonial rule. The chapter argues that Buganda built militarism into kingship and culture, but that nineteenth-century campaigns increasingly overreached. It sets Ganda war-making beside wider East African conflicts involving figures such as Zwangendaba, Mirambo, and the Ngoni to show both regional ambition and limits. Source anchors: Nakibinge; Kamanya; Suna; Mutesa; regalia; Zwangendaba; Mirambo; Ngoni.

Analysis: Nakibinge, Kamanya, Suna, and Mutesa give military power a chronology. Regalia and coronation rituals show that war was symbolic as well as practical, while Mirambo and the Ngoni situate Buganda in a wider militarized region. The chapter's causal role is to show that the same martial culture that supported expansion could become brittle when campaigns, command, and commerce pulled in different directions.

Chapter Ten: Developments in Organization, Tactics and Weaponry

Summary: This chapter looks inside the army: structures, hierarchy, tactics, command selection, peasant armies, capital-based soldiers, the new elite of bodyguards and pages, ebitongole, bagalagala, the myasi, spears, guns, powder shortages, firearm repair, and foreign assistance. Reid argues that the kabaka's attempt to strengthen his own position through favoured young men and armed retainers created rivalry with older chiefs. Guns mattered culturally and politically even when their battlefield effect was limited by scarcity, ammunition, and uneven training. Source anchors: ebitongole; bagalagala; myasi; bodyguards; older chiefs; guns; powder shortages; firearm repair.

Analysis: Ebitongole, bagalagala, guns, and older chiefs make the chapter a case for Elite Formation, because Reid shows the kabaka manufacturing a new ruling cadre out of bodyguards, pages, and favoured young men rather than inheriting one from the older chief class. Firearms gave the capital a new prestige culture and new factions, while powder shortages limited practical effectiveness, so the gun's political value as a marker of royal favour outpaced its battlefield value. The myasi and bodyguards show the kabaka reshaping command, but that reshaping could weaken battlefield coordination because it built a rival elite whose loyalty to the throne threatened the older chiefs whose cooperation the army still needed.

Chapter Eleven: Lake Victoria, the Final Frontier

Summary: Reid analyzes canoe construction, naval developments from about 1700 to 1840, the growth of naval power after 1840, Sesse labour, the Vuma, the gabunga, ports, fishing and canoe industries, Arab dhows, Said ibn Habib, lake trade routes, and the organization of the fleet. The Ganda fleet was meant to overcome limits of land warfare, control Lake Victoria routes, project influence, and protect commercial interests. It extended power but also exposed overconfidence, especially when Vuma resistance and shoreline raids undercut grand plans for Buzinza and Mirambo. Source anchors: Lake Victoria; canoe construction; Sesse labour; Vuma; gabunga; Arab dhow; Said ibn Habib; ports.

Analysis: Lake Victoria, Sesse labour, the gabunga, and Vuma resistance make naval power Reid's clearest example of innovation under pressure, and the chapter is a case for Empire and Periphery because the lake turns Buganda's power into a problem of projecting authority across water rather than occupying solid territory. Canoes joined local craft skill to state strategy, but the fleet depended on coerced or specialized Sesse island labour, so the kingdom ruled the lake through nodal control of ports, fishing industries, and canoe-building communities rather than through uniform occupation. Arab dhows and Said ibn Habib show why the lake mattered commercially, while Vuma resistance shows that technology did not erase the political limits of projecting force onto a shoreline the fleet could reach but could not hold.

Chapter Twelve: Conclusions

Summary: Reid concludes that Buganda's pre-colonial state used human and material resources to pursue profit, internal cohesion, and external security, but that this system was under strain by the eve of colonial rule. He recapitulates the importance of bananas, roads, taxation, slavery, barkcloth, iron, pottery, cowries, ivory, slaves, guns, canoes, the fleet, food shortage, rinderpest, trypanosomiasis, elephant depletion, Mwanga's mismanagement, and religious factionalism. British arrival found not a static kingdom but a resilient system in crisis, able to act as British "sub-colonists" partly because pre-colonial organization had already trained elites to mobilize resources. Source anchors: profit; internal cohesion; external security; barkcloth; rinderpest; ivory; Mwanga; sub-colonists.

Analysis: Profit, internal cohesion, external security, and barkcloth state the book's synthesis. Rinderpest, ivory decline, and Mwanga explain why late nineteenth-century Buganda was vulnerable before formal colonial rule. The "sub-colonists" point is crucial: Reid's Buganda enters colonial history with damaged but real capacities, not as a passive object suddenly made political by Europeans.
