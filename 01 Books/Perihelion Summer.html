Detailed overview

Greg Egan's Perihelion Summer turns an astronomical near miss into a story about logistics, denial, improvisation, and unequal survival. The immediate hazard is not an impact: the primordial black holes Taraxippus A and B pass tens of millions of kilometers away. The catastrophe comes from the way their gravity changes Earth's orbit, making June colder and December much hotter in the southern hemisphere. Egan tracks that shift through exact mechanisms: Einstein rings, JPL trajectory models, inverse-cube tides, the Stefan-Boltzmann rule of thumb, black-hole lensing, and the slow recognition that a non-apocalyptic tide can still destroy crops, reefs, fisheries, cities, and states.

The novel's main stage is the Mandjet, Matt's circular aquaculture vessel built with Arun, Yuki, Jožka, and others as a wave-powered fish farm. Before the encounter, it looks like an eccentric hedge against disaster, mocked by relatives, online commenters, and latecomers such as Aaron. After the encounter, its mundane systems become life-support infrastructure: cobia in the central net, CRISPR-modified flies turning waste into feed, solar panels, wave-power links, freshwater tanks, gardens, compost heaps, maggots, runabouts, VHF calls, pontoons, pressure sensors, and spare water-purifying units.

Egan is especially interested in the gap between knowing a danger and acting on it. Matt can explain Taraxippus to Leo, Selena, Aaron, Mr. Carmichael, and Eduardo, but each audience has different reasons not to move. Australian relatives choose inland camping grounds, Japanese families go to mountain villages, Timorese families weigh Antarctica against China and smugglers, coffee farmers fear squatters, and officials deploy Border Force drones to police empty northern cities. The book repeatedly asks what a rational plan looks like when the information is good enough to scare you but not good enough to make everyone accept the same tradeoff.

The middle of the book changes the Mandjet from refuge into a tool for others. Matt's failed pitch for the Seasonal Sharing Scheme, his insulation work, the Broome meeting, Eduardo's Dili contacts, the stolen surveillance drone, and the bargaining with coffee growers all build toward a southern loop: keep people fed while they move between intolerable heat and Antarctic summer. This is not presented as salvation. It is a least-bad route for people whose alternatives include heat death in Dili, pirates near Mindanao, hostile northern borders, or camps that cannot scale.

The final part strips the Mandjet of any fantasy of self-contained safety. Sri Lankan passengers arrive dehydrated; Malagasy boats trade bananas for potatoes; the Sophia needs underwater welding after ice damages its propeller; Yuki wishes she were in Japan; Perth burns while Matt cannot reach Selena; armed men seize the Mandjet; and Matt, Thiru, Aaron, Luís, Eduardo, Yuki, Jožka, and Arun improvise a rescue that almost becomes murder but ends through negotiation and the transfer of breeding stock. The vessel survives by becoming less private, not more fortified.

The ending in Antarctica is deliberately unfinished. Rayah's seaweed from Zanzibar, Jožka's krill-oil biodiesel idea, Yuki's possible bacteria, the crowded anchorages near the Mawson River, the moon's altered face, and Matt's mother asking what will be left to return to all point to a world that cannot go back to pre-Taraxippus normality. Matt's collapse beside his mother folds together his father's death, the bodies in Perth, the Mandjet hijacking, and the burden of having survived by making choices that saved some people and abandoned others.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Part One

Summary: Part One follows Matt, Arun, Yuki, Jožka, Aaron, Selena, Leo, and the Mandjet crew through the days before and immediately after the Taraxippoi trajectory becomes clear. It moves from a New Year's Eve party in Perth to the Mandjet's deck, satellite quotas, maggots, cobia, the binary-black-hole discovery, JPL predictions, HIP 33008, and the final realization that Earth will not drown from tides but will enter a radically more eccentric orbit. The part is structured around uncertainty shrinking from ridicule and vague risk into a dated orbital change: Taraxippus A on 16 March, Taraxippus B three days later, and southern summers made lethally hotter. Source anchors: Matt; Mandjet; Taraxippoi; JPL; HIP 33008; cobia; satellite quota; 16 March.

Analysis: Matt, the Mandjet, JPL, and HIP 33008 make Part One a study of evidence becoming actionable in stages. The satellite quota and cobia systems keep the risk practical even before the models settle, while the Taraxippoi and 16 March date turn preparation from eccentricity into necessity. The part's mechanism is narrowing uncertainty: each lensing reduces the space for denial but also reveals a different kind of damage than the one people feared.

Chapter 1

Summary: At his parents' New Year's Eve party in Perth, Matt endures heat, floodlights, relatives, and Leo's mockery about having spent five years of Greater Sunrise wages on the Mandjet instead of real estate. Selena rescues him from the argument, and Matt asks her again to bring their parents aboard if she can persuade them. He frames the Mandjet as self-sufficient in food and mostly immune to sea-level change, while admitting that nothing would make him happier than returning after six months to find the sky had not fallen. Source anchors: New Year's Eve; Matt; Leo; Greater Sunrise; Selena; Mandjet; real estate; sea-level change.

Analysis: Matt, Leo, Greater Sunrise, and Selena establish the emotional cost of preparedness before any black-hole calculations appear. Leo's real-estate anger shows the social pressure to perform normal confidence, while Selena's calm question lets Matt state the Mandjet's purpose without bravado. The chapter matters because Matt's refuge begins as a family embarrassment, not a heroic ark.

Chapter 2

Summary: Matt and Arun reach the Mandjet by runabout, talking about hangovers, Arun's crying mother, Taraxippus crackpots, the vessel's orange pontoons, wave-power links, solar panels, cobia pond, and Yuki's unexpected guest Aaron. Jožka is skeptical of Aaron, who blogs about TV shows and is seasick, but Matt insists that anyone aboard must be folded into the group if he completes safety induction and helps with the maggots. In the control room, the freshwater tanks, batteries, compressed-air stores, garden sensors, CRISPR flies, fish carcasses, and rotting seaweed all look healthy. Source anchors: Arun; runabout; Mandjet; Yuki; Aaron; Jožka; CRISPR flies; maggots.

Analysis: Arun, Yuki, Aaron, and Jožka define the Mandjet's first social test. The CRISPR flies and maggots make survival unpleasant and technical, while Aaron's arrival tests whether the crew can avoid creating insiders and outsiders before real pressure starts. Matt's insistence on fairness is pragmatic because later the vessel will have to absorb strangers far less prepared than Aaron.

Chapter 3

Summary: At three in the morning, Matt discovers that Aaron has exhausted 95 percent and then all of the Mandjet's satellite quota by streaming the Lilliput season finale on an iPad. Aaron had not understood the limit, Yuki was asleep, and Matt has to decide whether to treat the mistake as a crisis or a newcomer error. He returns to bed thinking about offshore rigs, trained workers, eight-person rooms, six-week rotations, and the impossibility of rehearsing indefinite communal life at sea. Source anchors: satellite quota; Aaron; Lilliput; Yuki; iPad; offshore rigs; eight people; six-week rotations.

Analysis: Aaron, Lilliput, the satellite quota, and offshore rigs turn a comic annoyance into a warning about social infrastructure. The lost data can be fixed, but Aaron's assumption that connectivity is ordinary reveals how little the Mandjet resembles land. Matt's memory of rigs matters because those cramped workplaces had training, hierarchy, and exit schedules, while the Mandjet may soon have none.

Chapter 4

Summary: While feeding CRISPR fly larvae to the cobia, Matt learns from Yuki that a new gravitational lensing has shown the Einstein ring radius is far too small for the original Taraxippus model. Jožka, Arun, Yuki, Aaron, and Matt consider hacking, a second black hole, primordial binaries, Hubble confirmation, Chilean observations, New Scientist wormhole chatter, Reddit naming polls, and media blame games. The independent analysis forces them to accept that Taraxippus is a binary and that all earlier single-object predictions are void. Source anchors: CRISPR larvae; cobia; Yuki; Einstein ring; Jožka; Arun; Hubble; binary black hole.

Analysis: Yuki, Einstein ring, Hubble, and binary black hole shift the problem from preparation to radical uncertainty. The cobia feeding just before the news keeps the vessel's biology beside the astronomy. Aaron's media feed and Arun's binary guess also show the difference between noise and insight: speculation is everywhere, but only some of it connects to the measurements.

Chapter 5

Summary: After new models suggest a serious risk, the crew contacts family and tries to persuade people inland or aboard: Selena takes Matt's parents toward Leonora, Aaron's mother finds a cousin in Cobar, Arun's family goes to Roma, Yuki's family heads for a mountain village near Osaka, and Jožka's family returns to Brno. Matt considers calling Aaron's parents himself with marine-engineering talk about tides and amphidromic systems. The chapter ends with the crew trying to present evacuation as ordinary prudence rather than panic. Source anchors: Selena; Leonora; Cobar; Roma; Osaka; Brno; Aaron's parents; amphidromic system.

Analysis: Selena, Leonora, Cobar, and Osaka show why preparedness fragments by family, geography, and trust. The Mandjet's unused berths become a reproach because the people Matt loves choose different hedges. The amphidromic-system pitch matters because technical authority only works when it can be translated into a story people can act on without feeling they have joined a cult.

Chapter 6

Summary: The crew waits through successive lensings as Siding Spring, Hubble, Chile, Mauna Kea, JPL, and finally HIP 33008 refine the Taraxippoi tracks. They watch Taraxippus A brighten a magnitude-seven star through binoculars and with the naked eye, then learn that A will pass about seventy-two million kilometers away and B about seventy-five million kilometers away. The tides will be less than feared, but the new orbit puts Earth 7 percent farther from the sun in June and 10 percent closer in December, implying cold winters, deadly southern summers, crop failures, forest die-offs, reef loss, and collapsing fisheries. Source anchors: Siding Spring; Mauna Kea; HIP 33008; Taraxippus A; seventy-two million kilometers; 7 percent; 10 percent; December.

Analysis: HIP 33008, Taraxippus A, seventy-two million kilometers, and December provide the scientific turn that governs the rest of the novella, and the chapter turns the Taraxippoi from a flood scare into a case of Ecological Constraint. The binocular scene gives the crew direct contact with curved space, but the JPL map converts that awe into an orbit that puts Earth 7 percent farther from the sun in June and 10 percent closer in December, so the catastrophe is not a single impact but a durable reshaping of growing seasons, fisheries, reefs, and habitable latitude. The chapter's key mechanism is delayed harm: avoiding the tidal flood leaves a slower, more durable catastrophe, which means every later political and logistical choice in the book runs inside an ecological budget set by the new orbit rather than by any government.

Part Two

Summary: Part Two jumps into the altered climate regime, with Matt back in Australia installing insulation during blizzards, pitching the Seasonal Sharing Scheme, tending a greenhouse, and deciding that Perth will not be enough when summer returns. He rejoins the Mandjet near Broome, proposes the southern loop, calls Eduardo in Dili, negotiates with Timorese representatives, steals a Border Force drone to reassure coffee growers, and accepts Antarctica as the route the Mandjet can actually support. This part changes the vessel from a private fallback into a hub for a flotilla. Source anchors: blizzard; Seasonal Sharing Scheme; Broome; Eduardo; southern loop; Dili; Border Force drone; Antarctica.

Analysis: Broome, Eduardo, the southern loop, and the Border Force drone make Part Two about conversion from survivalism to logistics. The blizzard and Seasonal Sharing Scheme show formal adaptation failing socially, while Dili and Antarctica force Matt to ask where the Mandjet is useful rather than where it is safest for him. The stolen drone is a small crime pressed into service as trust-building infrastructure.

Chapter 7

Summary: Matt installs ceiling insulation for Mr. Carmichael during a blizzard, tries to explain the Seasonal Sharing Scheme for tropical families from PNG, East Timor, and Indonesia, and is suspended by the Insulation Army app after Carmichael complains about offensive language. At home, he tends a heated greenhouse, watches Border Force drone propaganda about empty northern cities, eats with Selena and his parents, and realizes that if summer brings 60-degree Perth, desalination failure, and unaffordable imports, he needs to be aboard the Mandjet before it becomes impossible to retrieve them. Source anchors: Mr. Carmichael; ceiling insulation; Seasonal Sharing Scheme; Insulation Army; greenhouse; Border Force drones; 60 degrees; desalination.

Analysis: Mr. Carmichael, the Seasonal Sharing Scheme, Border Force drones, and 60 degrees show Australian adaptation splitting between mutual aid and border policing. Matt's suspension proves how brittle volunteer systems are when politics enters a house call. The greenhouse and desalination fears push him back to the Mandjet because a suburban home cannot be patched into a closed life-support system quickly enough.

Chapter 8

Summary: Matt hitchhikes to Broome and rejoins Arun, Yuki, Jožka, Aaron, and the Mandjet, then argues for a southern loop rather than smuggling people toward Alaska, Siberia, or the North Pacific. The crew debates gunboats, China, Emperor seamount islands, Arctic farmland fantasies, Indonesian fishing boats, flotilla risk, and the Mandjet as a support vessel with food and water. Matt calls Eduardo in Dili, who says the north is full of smugglers, conspiracy claims, heat denial, and fear of pirates near Mindanao, and suggests meeting near Melville Island instead of entering Indonesian waters. Source anchors: Broome; Arun; southern loop; Alaska; Emperor seamounts; Eduardo; Dili; Melville Island.

Analysis: Broome, Eduardo, Dili, and Melville Island transform the Mandjet's route into a political choice. The Arctic and seamount proposals show that every "solution" has borders, navies, or years of engineering attached. The southern loop is not ideal, but it matches the Mandjet's actual strengths: slow, visible, food-producing, and useful where no navy is trying to defend a beachhead.

Chapter 9

Summary: Eduardo brings José, Martinho, and João to inspect the Mandjet by fishing boat, and the crew demonstrates the control room, garden, maggots, cobia, flies, generators, electronics, propulsion, and structure. João dives into the central enclosure to confirm that the fish are real, and later Eduardo explains that coffee-growing families will not leave unless their land is protected and a doctor will not join unless the flotilla reaches about a thousand people. Matt, Arun, Jožka, Yuki, and Aaron then lure a Border Force drone off course with fake GPS, capture it with runabouts, ropes, ballast, and seawater, and give it to Eduardo as a scarecrow for the coffee plantations. Source anchors: Eduardo; José; Martinho; João; coffee growers; doctor; fake GPS; Border Force drone.

Analysis: Eduardo, João, the coffee growers, and fake GPS make trust material, and the chapter is a case for Logistics and Throughput because the Timorese elders do not bargain over speeches or rights; they bargain over capacity thresholds. The doctor will not join until the flotilla reaches about a thousand people, the coffee growers will not leave unless their land is protected, and João dives into the central enclosure to confirm that the cobia are real before any agreement can hold. Capturing the Border Force drone with fake GPS, runabouts, ropes, and seawater then reverses Australian border technology: a tool meant to exclude migrants becomes a prop that helps them risk leaving home, and the Mandjet earns its place in the southern loop only by proving it can move food, water, and medical scale at the rate the evacuation requires.

Part Three

Summary: Part Three follows the flotilla south through heat, crowding, storms, boat damage, refugee exchanges, armed seizure, and eventual Antarctic landfall. The Mandjet becomes school, hospital-adjacent water source, fish farm, repair platform, bargaining chip, and contested prize while Matt tries to keep contact with Selena and his mother. The section's route runs through Sri Lankan dehydration, Malagasy banana trade, the Sophia's damaged propeller, Perth's fire, the Mandjet hijacking, and the improvised camp near the Mawson River. Source anchors: flotilla; Sri Lankan boat; Malagasy bananas; Sophia; Perth fire; hijacking; Mawson River; Antarctic camp.

Analysis: The Sri Lankan boat, Sophia, Perth fire, and Mawson River show Part Three dismantling the idea that the Mandjet can simply outrun catastrophe. Every gain creates a new obligation: water, repairs, passengers, fish stock, or rescue. The Antarctic camp is not a destination that ends the story; it is a new logistics problem under midnight sun.

Chapter 10

Summary: On the Mandjet, Timorese children arrive by yellow "onibus escolar," Hélia teaches on deck, and Matt deals with compost before reading news of Singapore's photovoltaic-glass enclosure, Dubai's similar plan, Shanghai's domes, Mediterranean drownings, South China Sea deaths, East African fleets, South African camps, Australian debt downgraded to junk, and the Australian dollar's 60 percent drop. A Sri Lankan trawler arrives with sixty people needing drinking water and medical attention from Rosa. The chapter contrasts the children's routine with global systems breaking around them. Source anchors: onibus escolar; Hélia; Singapore glass; Shanghai domes; Mediterranean drownings; Australian dollar; Sri Lankan trawler; Rosa.

Analysis: Hélia, Singapore glass, the Sri Lankan trawler, and the Australian dollar show the new world operating at multiple scales. Singapore can enclose itself, while a trawler with sixty people needs water immediately. The children learning on the deck matter because the flotilla is trying to remain a society, not just a chain of emergency cases.

Chapter 11

Summary: In heavy seas, Matt wakes judging seven-meter waves by the Mandjet's motion, then sees Sri Lankan children, Hélia's classes, Indonesians passing through, Thiru's nephew Sundara, bananas traded from Madagascans for potatoes, and the Sophia asking for mechanical help. Matt reaches Luís's boat by runabout, diagnoses a propeller problem, prepares for underwater welding, and spends the night aboard while Jorge worries that friends left in Timor-Leste are dead. The next day, with Rosa nearby, Matt welds the damaged propeller under the hull in rough water. Source anchors: seven-meter waves; Hélia; Sundara; Madagascans; Sophia; Luís; Jorge; underwater welding.

Analysis: Luís, the Sophia, underwater welding, and Jorge make the flotilla's fragility visceral. A single damaged propeller can strand thirty people, and a single repair depends on Matt's old offshore skills plus Rosa's medical backup. The bananas from Madagascans also show an emerging exchange economy, but one still vulnerable to ice, waves, and missing news from home.

Chapter 12

Summary: Matt and Thiru travel by trawler toward a crisis, discussing Thiru's village, failed cold storage, missing air-conditioning, Chinese conspiracy rumors, and why the Mandjet began as a fish farm rather than a prophecy. They spot and recover a small iceberg, estimate its volume and weight, use Thiru's net and winch to haul it into the hold, and joke that they now have air-conditioning. The ice also explains how the Sophia's propeller might have been damaged in water far north of Antarctica. Source anchors: Thiru; trawler; cold storage; Chinese government; iceberg; winch; Sophia; air-conditioning.

Analysis: Thiru, cold storage, iceberg, and winch show adaptation as salvage. The iceberg is both hazard and resource: it can wreck the Sophia and cool Thiru's boat. Thiru's account of his village also strips away any easy comfort Matt might take from the Mandjet's origin story, because no amount of foresight could make air-conditioning appear once supply chains failed.

Chapter 13

Summary: Eduardo warns Matt through coded coordinates that four armed men have seized the Mandjet, locked people in cabins, and forced Aaron to escape by diving into the water. Matt, Thiru, Aaron, Luís, and Eduardo plan an underwater return through the pontoon access hatch, using snorkels, ropes, crane hooks, X-shaped lines, bolts, and a garrote in the toilet compartment. Yuki interrupts the ambush, explaining that she and Jožka have convinced the hijackers to leave with fish stock, maggots, and a solar water-purifying unit; the next morning the armed men depart, and Yuki proposes making breeding-stock transfers official to reduce future attacks. Source anchors: Eduardo; coded coordinates; armed men; Aaron; pontoon hatch; garrote; Yuki; breeding stock.

Analysis: Eduardo, Aaron, the garrote, and Yuki make this the book's moral crisis, and the chapter is a case for Crisis Governance because the decision is triage under pressure rather than ordinary policing. Matt is ready to kill because the Mandjet's cobia, maggots, solar water-purifiers, and breeding stock are too valuable to lose, but Yuki's negotiation converts armed theft into a rough distribution of capacity, accepting partial loss to keep the vessel and its people intact. The breeding-stock proposal matters because defense by secrecy has failed; the vessel becomes safer by giving other boats a stake in its survival, which is the crisis-governance move of trading autonomy for distributed resilience when no single refuge can be defended alone.

Chapter 14

Summary: In Antarctica under the midnight sun, Matt walks near crowded anchorages, moss, meltwater, the Mawson River, crumbling ice cliffs, and camps full of insomniac arrivals. He meets Rayah from Zanzibar, who brought seaweed grown in nets, then hears Jožka thinking about krill-oil biodiesel and Yuki engineering bacteria so ships can return north. In the tent, Matt's mother smokes and asks what will be left to go back to; Matt breaks down over his father's death, the Perth bodies, the hijacking, and the life that remains on water. Source anchors: midnight sun; Rayah; Zanzibar; seaweed; Jožka; krill oil; Mawson River; Matt's mother.

Analysis: Rayah, seaweed, Jožka, and Matt's mother make the ending provisional and unsentimental. Zanzibar's seaweed and krill oil are not utopian answers; they are the next set of tools in a damaged supply chain. Matt's collapse matters because the plot's competence has carried him far past grief, and Antarctica finally gives him enough stillness to feel what survival has cost.
