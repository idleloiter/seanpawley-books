Survival in Auschwitz

Buy on Amazon — Survival in Auschwitz

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer.

Detailed overview

Primo Levi frames Survival in Auschwitz as documentation for a quiet study of the human mind under the Lager, not as a new indictment that adds unknown atrocities. The preface matters because Levi immediately narrows the book's purpose: he was deported in 1944, after labor scarcity slightly lengthened some prisoners' lives, and he writes as one witness to a system designed to destroy persons before killing bodies. The poem beginning "You who live safe" turns the reader's domestic safety into an obligation to remember.

The memoir follows Levi from capture by the Fascist Militia on 13 December 1943 through Fossoli, the transport, Buna-Monowitz, the Auschwitz number, initiation into camp rules, work, hunger, sickness, selection, laboratory labor, and the final ten days after the Germans evacuate the camp. The structure is not simply chronological. Levi repeatedly pauses to analyze camp economy, language, hierarchy, trade, shame, useless labor, the distinction between "the drowned and the saved," and the moral distortion produced by hunger and terror.

Levi's most concrete subject is the making of the Häftling. Names, clothes, shoes, spoons, bowls, soup, bread, bunks, latrines, Kommando assignments, the Ka-Be hospital, roll call, selections, tattooed numbers, and the German commands all become technologies of reduction. Yet the book is never only about degradation. Levi records Lorenzo's gifts of soup, Alberto's intelligence and friendship, Jean the Pikolo listening to Dante, Resnyk sharing labor, and Charles and Arthur keeping life going in the abandoned infirmary.

The camp is shown as an economy as much as a prison. "This Side of Good and Evil" describes the Exchange Market, theft, shirt trading, Greek prisoners from Salonika, and the way value detaches from ordinary morality. "Chemical Examination" and "Die drei Leute vom Labor" show Levi's training as a chemist becoming a narrow survival advantage, though even the laboratory does not abolish fear, hunger, or the camp's permanent contingency.

The strongest chapters are those where Levi refuses false uplift. "The Canto of Ulysses" gives a moment of human recovery through Dante, but it occurs between soup errands and forced labor. "October 1944" shows selections returning with winter. "The Last One" ends with prisoners watching a hanging and feeling ashamed because the condemned man still has the force to declare defiance while they have been reduced to spectators. The book's ethical pressure comes from these exact contrasts.

The final ten days after evacuation do not become liberation fantasy. Levi, sick with scarlet fever, remains in Ka-Be with other abandoned prisoners while Germans flee, Russians approach, disease spreads, and men scavenge potatoes, stoves, and water. Survival depends on practical cooperation with Charles and Arthur, not on a sudden restoration of dignity. When the Russians arrive, the dead are still present, and the saved are left with the work of memory.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Coercive Labor: the Lager turns prisoners into numbered, hungry, sortable labor bodies through Kommandos, Kapos, Buna work, hospital triage, selections, and the permanent threat of uselessness.
  • Knowledge Preservation: Levi writes the book as witness and documentation, preserving names, routines, camp language, Dante fragments, and the moral facts of survival against disbelief and forgetting.
  • Legibility: tattooed numbers, work classifications, blocks, ration systems, selections, Ka-Be sorting, and prisoner taxonomies make people readable to the camp as labor capacity or waste.
  • Moral Economy: bread, spoons, soup, theft, exchange, Lorenzo's gifts, and the distinction between the drowned and the saved show moral judgment distorted but not abolished by engineered scarcity.
  • Language and Ideology: German commands, camp jargon, Levi's poem, the language of "stranger as enemy," and Dante's Ulysses episode show how words can either enforce degradation or recover human inheritance.
  • Ecological Constraint: cold, rain, mud, winter, disease, hunger, and the material thresholds of warmth and calories make survival depend on bodily conditions as much as on decisions.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Author's Preface

Summary: Levi explains that he was deported to Auschwitz in 1944, when German labor shortages had temporarily lengthened the average life of some prisoners destined for elimination. He says the book is not written to add unknown accusations about death camps, but to furnish documentation for a quiet study of aspects of the human mind. The preface identifies Auschwitz, German policy, the scarcity of labor, camp routine, individual killings, memory, judgment, and the need for measured witness. Levi also warns that many facts may seem unbelievable because they describe a world whose moral rules were deliberately inverted. Source anchors: 1944; Auschwitz; German Government; scarcity of labour; camp routine; quiet study; human mind; witness.

Analysis: 1944, Auschwitz, German Government, scarcity of labour, camp routine, quiet study, human mind, and witness define Levi's authority and restraint. Scarcity of labour explains why his survival is historically contingent, while camp routine signals that destruction operated through ordinary procedures. Quiet study and human mind matter because Levi wants the reader to examine mechanisms, not consume horror as spectacle. The preface establishes the book as Knowledge Preservation because Levi frames testimony as documentation against disbelief rather than as retrospective ornament.

Chapter 1: The Journey

Summary: Levi begins with his capture by the Fascist Militia on 13 December 1943, when he is twenty-four and part of an inexperienced Justice and Liberty partisan group in the mountains. He is transferred to Fossoli, joins Italian Jews marked for deportation, and boards a sealed train with men, women, children, old people, water shortages, fear, and ignorance of the destination. The transport moves toward Auschwitz, where the selection on arrival separates those sent directly away from those chosen for labor. The chapter names Italy, racial laws, Fossoli, Carpi, the German convoy, the cattle cars, and the first lesson that ordinary categories of home and travel have ended. Source anchors: 13 December 1943; Fascist Militia; Justice and Liberty; Fossoli; Italian Jews; cattle cars; Auschwitz; selection.

Analysis: 13 December 1943, Fascist Militia, Justice and Liberty, Fossoli, Italian Jews, cattle cars, Auschwitz, and selection move Levi from political naivete into the machinery of extermination. Fossoli and cattle cars show the transition from Italian imprisonment to German deportation. Selection is the first Auschwitz procedure that turns persons into categories, while Justice and Liberty marks the moral world Levi has already lost.

Chapter 2: On the Bottom

Summary: "On the Bottom" describes arrival in the Lager and the stripping away of personhood. Levi receives a number, loses clothes, hair, shoes, possessions, name, and ordinary modesty, and learns that the camp contains Häftlinge, Blocks, Kapos, Kommandos, soup, bread, bunks, washrooms, and endless orders in German and camp jargon. He explains the difference between new arrivals and old prisoners, the meaning of the tattoo, the need for a spoon and bowl, and the instant pressure of hunger and cold. The chapter's title names the social and moral floor on which a man becomes a specimen in an experiment. Source anchors: Lager; Häftlinge; tattoo; number; Kapos; Kommandos; soup; spoon.

Analysis: Lager, Häftlinge, tattoo, number, Kapos, Kommandos, soup, and spoon show how Auschwitz remakes identity through objects and ranks, and the chapter is a case for Coercive Labor because the camp converts persons into expendable labor material before it kills them. Tattoo and number replace name, while Kapos and Kommandos organize domination through prisoner intermediaries rather than SS men alone, so the system narrows refusal until survival itself becomes compliance with the work order. Soup and spoon matter because survival immediately depends on the smallest utensils and routines; Levi’s descent to the bottom is measured by learning that a bowl or spoon can count for more than the moral vocabulary he brought from outside, which is the coercive-labor logic in which the conditions for bargaining, dignity, and ordinary moral judgment are systematically destroyed.

Chapter 3: Initiation

Summary: Levi is assigned to Block 30 after transfers from hut to hut and Kommando to Kommando, sharing a bunk with Diena and learning practical rules from Steinlauf and others. The chapter teaches that washing, shoes, bowls, bread, theft, latrines, roll call, and bed assignments are not trivial but matters of survival. Steinlauf insists on washing not because it produces cleanliness in any normal sense, but because it preserves a residue of discipline against the camp's effort to reduce prisoners to beasts. Levi records his own confusion as a newcomer who still wants explanations in a place where explanations rarely help. Source anchors: Block 30; Diena; Steinlauf; washing; shoes; bread; roll call; newcomer.

Analysis: Block 30, Diena, Steinlauf, washing, shoes, bread, roll call, and newcomer make initiation a counter-education. Diena and Steinlauf give Levi human instruction, while washing and shoes show that dignity and survival are both practical. Bread and roll call reveal how every hour is governed by scarcity and exposure, leaving the newcomer to learn by danger.

Chapter 4: Ka-Be

Summary: "Ka-Be" follows Levi into the camp infirmary after days of labor carrying loads from the railway to the store over thawing ground. His injured foot brings him into the Krankenbau, where entry procedures, naked waiting, medical sorting, bunks, lice, fever, and rumours of selection create a different but still dangerous zone. Ka-Be offers rest from work and a chance to observe the camp, but it also threatens disappearance for those judged useless. Levi sees how sickness changes status without restoring safety. Source anchors: Ka-Be; railway; thawing ground; injured foot; Krankenbau; lice; fever; selection.

Analysis: Ka-Be, railway, thawing ground, injured foot, Krankenbau, lice, fever, and selection show that even the hospital is part of the camp's sorting apparatus. Railway labor and thawing ground produce the injury, while Krankenbau and lice make rest inseparable from degradation. Selection matters because medical care can become the path to death if a prisoner is no longer useful. Ka-Be is also Legibility under murderous conditions: the sick are sorted according to perceived labor value, not restored as patients.

Chapter 5: Our Nights

Summary: After leaving Ka-Be, Levi is not returned to his former Block or Kommando but reassigned by opaque criteria. "Our Nights" describes the readjustment to bunks, overcrowding, dreams, hunger, night noises, latrine trips, shoes, belts, bowls, and the constant fear of losing one's place or possessions. Levi analyzes the astonishing human capacity to dig in, secrete a shell, and build a tenuous defensive barrier even in desperate circumstances. Dreams of food, home, and telling the story to people who do not listen reveal how the camp enters sleep as well as waking life. Source anchors: Ka-Be; Block; Kommando; bunks; dreams; hunger; latrine; defensive barrier.

Analysis: Ka-Be, Block, Kommando, bunks, dreams, hunger, latrine, and defensive barrier show survival as adaptation without safety. Block and Kommando reassignment remove continuity, while bunks and latrine routines reduce night to competition for space and bodily control. Dreams and defensive barrier show the mind trying to preserve a self that the camp attacks even during sleep.

Chapter 6: The Work

Summary: Levi describes work with Resnyk, the red-haired prisoner from Drancy who shares his bunk and later helps him carry heavy loads. The chapter shows the Buna work site, civilian workers, Polish and French prisoners, mud, commands, guards, and the exhausting rhythm of carrying, lifting, and marching. Resnyk's strength and practical kindness briefly change Levi's day, but the work remains purposeless from the prisoner's point of view except as a mechanism for consuming bodies. Levi records how weakness, cold, and labor make thought narrow to the next load and the next chance not to collapse. Source anchors: Resnyk; Drancy; Buna; civilian workers; mud; guards; carrying; collapse.

Analysis: Resnyk, Drancy, Buna, civilian workers, mud, guards, carrying, and collapse make labor both social and destructive. Resnyk's help shows that solidarity can exist inside forced work, while Buna and civilian workers place the camp inside German industry. Mud, guards, carrying, and collapse reduce labor to bodily attrition rather than productive meaning.

Chapter 7: A Good Day

Summary: "A Good Day" begins with the claim that free men give many names to life's purpose, while in the Lager the only purpose is to reach spring. The weather improves, the sun appears, and the prisoners feel the difference that warmth can make to bodies reduced by hunger. The chapter turns on soup, Buna, the Chemical Kommando, Lorenzo-like gifts of food elsewhere in the memoir's world, and the moment when one ration or one warm hour can make the day seem good. Levi shows how the scale of desire has contracted until survival through the season is enough. Source anchors: spring; sun; Lager; soup; Buna; Chemical Kommando; hunger; warm hour.

Analysis: spring, sun, Lager, soup, Buna, Chemical Kommando, hunger, and warm hour explain the title without sentimentality. Spring and sun are not decorative; they change the survival odds of hungry men whose bodies have been worn down by winter. Soup and warm hour mark the reduced scale of happiness, while Buna and Chemical Kommando keep that fragile relief tied to forced labor. The chapter matters because Levi shows purpose shrinking to warmth, calories, and another day without pretending that such shrinkage is spiritual wisdom.

Chapter 8: This Side of Good and Evil

Summary: Levi analyzes the camp economy through the Exchange Market, theft, bread, shirts, bowls, spoons, tobacco, soup, and the experienced Greek Jews from Salonika. The chapter explains how ordinary moral categories are distorted when every object has survival value and when official rules coexist with tolerated illegal trade. Prisoners steal from Buna, trade in the camp, and calculate value under hunger, while the administration benefits from confusion and scarcity. Levi does not romanticize the market; he treats it as an adaptation to a system where need has been engineered. Source anchors: Exchange Market; theft; bread; shirts; spoons; tobacco; Salonika; scarcity.

Analysis: Exchange Market, theft, bread, shirts, spoons, tobacco, Salonika, and scarcity show why Levi calls the chapter this side of good and evil. Bread and spoons have moral weight because they preserve life, while theft and trade cannot be judged by peacetime standards alone. Salonika prisoners matter because their experience reveals skills and codes newcomers lack. The market is a damaged Moral Economy: scarcity rewrites value, but Levi still tracks obligations, predation, skill, and shame rather than treating all acts as equivalent.

Chapter 9: The Drowned and the Saved

Summary: Levi distinguishes between the "drowned," who sink rapidly in the Lager, and the "saved," who find positions, skills, patrons, or adaptations that delay destruction. He describes Musselmänner, Schepschel, Alfred L., Elias Lindzin, Henri, Prominents, and the many ways prisoners survive by strength, cunning, labor position, exchange, or manipulation. The chapter is analytic rather than self-congratulatory: being saved does not mean being morally better. Levi studies how the camp selects for certain capacities and erodes the social assumptions by which outsiders would normally judge conduct. Source anchors: drowned; saved; Musselmänner; Schepschel; Alfred L.; Elias Lindzin; Henri; Prominents.

Analysis: drowned, saved, Musselmänner, Schepschel, Alfred L., Elias Lindzin, Henri, and Prominents form Levi's most important camp taxonomy. Musselmänner name those already near social death, while Schepschel, Alfred L., Elias Lindzin, and Henri show different survival techniques. Prominents matter because camp privilege is produced inside the prisoner society, not only imposed from SS authority. Levi's taxonomy is Legibility turned back against the camp: he classifies the social mechanisms of survival without accepting the camp's own judgment of human worth.

Chapter 10: Chemical Examination

Summary: Levi is assigned to the Chemical Kommando, whose Kapo is a green triangle professional delinquent rather than a chemist. The chapter follows the Arbeitsdienst, the roll-call square, the group of fifteen Häftlinge, the hope attached to Levi's chemistry training, and the examination by Doktor Pannwitz. Levi crosses from the camp world into an office where scientific credentials are tested under radically unequal conditions. The encounter with Pannwitz, in which Levi feels looked at as if through aquarium glass, shows knowledge becoming a possible survival asset while humiliation remains total. Source anchors: Chemical Kommando; green triangle; Arbeitsdienst; fifteen Häftlinge; chemistry; Doktor Pannwitz; aquarium glass; survival asset.

Analysis: Chemical Kommando, green triangle, Arbeitsdienst, fifteen Häftlinge, chemistry, Doktor Pannwitz, aquarium glass, and survival asset show the camp's grotesque use of expertise. Chemistry gives Levi a chance, but green triangle and Arbeitsdienst remind him that professional logic is subordinated to camp hierarchy. Doktor Pannwitz and aquarium glass make the examination a scene of dehumanizing observation.

Chapter 11: The Canto of Ulysses

Summary: Levi walks with Jean the Pikolo to fetch soup and tries to teach him Italian through Dante's canto of Ulysses. He remembers fragments from the Inferno, struggles to reconstruct lines, translates for Jean, and is pierced by the passage about human beings made not to live like brutes but to pursue virtue and knowledge. The chapter occurs amid Buna, soup, Kommando routines, French-Italian exchange, and the urgency of memory under hunger. The episode is not escape from Auschwitz; it is a brief recovery of language and human inheritance inside Auschwitz. Source anchors: Jean the Pikolo; soup; Dante; Ulysses; Inferno; virtue and knowledge; Buna; memory.

Analysis: Jean the Pikolo, soup, Dante, Ulysses, Inferno, virtue and knowledge, Buna, and memory make this chapter the memoir’s central act of cultural rescue, and it is a case for Knowledge Preservation because Levi recovers a language of aspiration inside the camp rather than outside it. Soup keeps the errand material, while Dante and Ulysses restore a language of aspiration that the Lager has tried to strip from the Häftling. Memory matters because Levi’s incomplete recall is itself evidence of what the camp has damaged and what it has not fully taken, so preservation here is the active work of reconstructing a human inheritance from fragments under conditions designed to make inheritance impossible.

Chapter 12: The Events of the Summer

Summary: This chapter records changes during summer 1944: rumours from the war, bombings, work disruptions, the arrival of Hungarian Jews, selections, and shifting expectations as the front moves. Levi describes Buna, air raids, the black market, the camp's nervous atmosphere, and the tension between hope and the knowledge that hope can be dangerous. The summer brings news that Germany may lose, but it also brings intensified killing and the awareness that prisoners may die before any army reaches them. Source anchors: summer 1944; rumours; bombings; Hungarian Jews; selections; Buna; black market; front.

Analysis: summer 1944, rumours, bombings, Hungarian Jews, selections, Buna, black market, and front place the camp inside the wider war without making liberation imminent. Bombings and front news suggest German vulnerability, while Hungarian Jews and selections show extermination continuing. Buna and black market keep survival tied to daily calculation even when history outside begins to move.

Chapter 13: October 1944

Summary: "October 1944" begins with prisoners fighting emotionally against the arrival of winter, knowing from the previous year that October to April can kill seven out of ten. The chapter centers on cold, rain, selections, the fear of being chosen, the Ka-Be, the Chemical Kommando, and Levi's effort to remain useful enough to live. Winter changes every calculation: clothing, shoes, calories, labor, illness, and the length of roll call become more lethal. Levi shows that the season itself is an executioner added to hunger, work, and SS power. Source anchors: October 1944; winter; seven out of ten; selections; Ka-Be; Chemical Kommando; clothing; roll call.

Analysis: October 1944, winter, seven out of ten, selections, Ka-Be, Chemical Kommando, clothing, and roll call make time biological. Winter and seven out of ten translate calendar change into expected death. Selections, Ka-Be, and Chemical Kommando show Levi trying to remain on the labor side of the camp's ledger, while clothing and roll call become survival variables.

Chapter 14: Kraus

Summary: "Kraus" takes place in November rain, with mud like a swamp and prisoners longing for a dry rag, a sack, shelter, or fire. Levi works beside Kraus, a Hungarian who has not yet learned the camp's economy of effort and who works too eagerly, endangering himself and irritating others. Levi invents a dream in which Kraus visits him at home in Naples or Turin, momentarily giving the man a human future he likely will not have. The chapter shows pity, irritation, lying kindness, and the way rain reduces happiness to dryness. Source anchors: November; rain; dry rag; Kraus; Hungarian; shelter; fire; invented dream.

Analysis: November, rain, dry rag, Kraus, Hungarian, shelter, fire, and invented dream make the chapter intimate and cruel. Dry rag, shelter, and fire define happiness at the lowest material level, lower even than freedom or family in that wet hour. Kraus as a Hungarian newcomer shows a man not yet adapted to the camp's conservation of effort, while the invented dream is kindness precisely because it cannot change his probable fate. Levi's pity is mixed with irritation, which keeps the scene honest about what hunger and rain do to compassion. The chapter makes Ecological Constraint morally visible because weather and wet clothing directly alter the capacity to feel, help, or endure.

Chapter 15: Die drei Leute vom Labor

Summary: As winter begins and the front approaches, Levi expects the weakened Chemical Kommando to die hauling 125-pound phenylbeta sacks. Instead Doktor Pannwitz selects prisoner 174517 with Brackier and Kandel for the laboratory in Bau 939. Its 65-degree room, precision balance, Heraeus oven, Hoppler thermostat, notebook, soap, and petrol offer warmth, lighter work, and material for exchange, substantially improving their chances of surviving the winter. Yet the reprieve is conditional on error-free performance. The German and Polish civilian women treat the three filthy prisoners with disgust; Fraulein Liczba's antisemitic insult makes ordinary laboratory life another measure of their exclusion. Source anchors: phenylbeta; prisoner 174517; Bau 939; Heraeus oven; Herr Stawinoga; Fraulein Liczba.

Analysis: The laboratory protects Levi's body by partially restoring the professional identity the camp has tried to erase. Familiar equipment and Herr Stawinoga's formal Monsieur awaken university memory, but that recovery also revives the capacity to suffer as a man rather than endure in the Lager's narrowed consciousness. Fraulein Liczba and the other women sharpen the contradiction: their bread, clothes, songs, and Christmas plans belong to the civilian world whose chemistry Levi can still practice but whose recognition he cannot regain. Selection as prisoner 174517 is therefore neither liberation nor symbolic redemption; it is a precarious material advantage that may end with one broken glass or failed measurement.

Chapter 16: The Last One

Summary: Near Christmas, Levi and Alberto walk in the grey file, discussing work, bread, cold, and laboratory news. The prisoners witness the hanging of a man connected with the Sonderkommando revolt, and the condemned prisoner cries out before death. Levi and Alberto return to their bowl of soup ashamed, because the last man has shown a force and defiance that the surviving prisoners feel they no longer possess. The chapter includes Alberto, the Laboratory, the march, Christmas, public execution, the SS, and the moral shock of seeing resistance from someone about to die. Source anchors: Christmas; Alberto; Laboratory; bread; public execution; Sonderkommando; defiance; shame.

Analysis: Christmas, Alberto, Laboratory, bread, public execution, Sonderkommando, defiance, and shame make this chapter a judgment on survival itself. Alberto and bread keep Levi among the living, while public execution and Sonderkommando recall open resistance. Defiance produces shame because survival has required habits of submission, and the condemned man momentarily reveals what has been lost. The execution also exposes Language and Ideology at its starkest, because a final spoken declaration briefly breaks the camp's vocabulary of obedience.

Chapter 17: The Story of Ten Days

Summary: The final chapter begins as Levi falls ill with scarlet fever and enters Ka-Be just before the Germans evacuate the camp in January 1945. Alberto leaves on the death march and disappears, while Levi remains with other sick prisoners including Charles and Arthur. The abandoned prisoners scavenge potatoes, light a stove, fetch water, care for the sick, watch men die, and wait as the Russians approach. The ten days after German departure are filled with cold, infection, corpses, practical cooperation, and the slow realization that survival is possible, but not cleanly separable from the dead left in the camp. Source anchors: scarlet fever; Ka-Be; January 1945; Alberto; death march; Charles; Arthur; Russians.

Analysis: scarlet fever, Ka-Be, January 1945, Alberto, death march, Charles, Arthur, and Russians make liberation contingent and incomplete. Scarlet fever and Ka-Be save Levi by preventing evacuation, while Alberto and death march mark the cost of that chance. Charles, Arthur, and Russians show survival depending first on prisoner cooperation and only later on military arrival.
