Detailed overview

Arthur Keaveney's Sulla is a political biography of Lucius Cornelius Sulla that tries to restore the man behind the later image of the grim dictator. The book begins from the unprecedented fact that in 88 BC a Roman consul led his own army against Rome, then asks how a poor patrician from the Cornelian gens became the soldier, diplomat, civil-war commander, dictator, lawgiver, and retiree whose career permanently damaged the republic he claimed to restore.

Keaveney insists on Sulla's contradictions. He was an aristocrat with fierce pride in noble ancestry, but poverty pushed him into the company of actors, singers, and women outside senatorial respectability. He was charming, loyal to friends, generous to soldiers, and capable of real warmth; he was also ambitious, vengeful, and willing to make enemies pay in blood, confiscation, exile, and humiliation. The biography repeatedly uses Plutarch, Appian, Sallust, Cicero, coins, offices, marriages, and military episodes to hold those traits together rather than choosing either monster or misunderstood conservative.

The narrative gives Sulla's early career a clear military and diplomatic logic. His service under Marius in the Jugurthine War, his handling of Bocchus, his rivalry with Marius over credit for Jugurtha's capture, his eastern contact with Mithridates and Parthian envoys, and his Social War victories all build the reputation that made the Mithridatic command worth fighting over. Keaveney's Sulla is not a politician who accidentally acquired an army; he is a commander whose rapport with soldiers became a political weapon after Roman institutions failed to contain elite competition.

The year 88 BC is the book's hinge. Sulpicius, Marius, the Mithridatic command, the tribunate, the assemblies, and the Senate's weakness create the crisis in which Sulla chooses to march on Rome. Keaveney does not deny the illegality or destructive precedent of the march, but he presents Sulla as convinced that Sulpicius's measures were themselves tyrannical and that his own dignitas and public settlement were being stolen. The book is strongest when showing how restorationist language and emergency action begin to feed each other.

The dictatorship chapters describe Sulla's settlement as both systematic and morally compromised. The proscriptions eliminate enemies and raise money; the laws strengthen the Senate, weaken the tribunate, regulate the courts, enlarge magistracies, and restructure provincial careers; the colonies and confiscations reward veterans while punishing Italian communities. Sulla's constitution is not random cruelty. It is an attempt to rebuild senatorial government by using methods that make future constitutional government less plausible.

The final chapters treat Sulla's abdication and death as part of his self-understanding. Keaveney takes seriously Sulla's belief in felicitas, Apollo, Venus, Ma-Bellona, omens, dreams, and his own role as lawgiver. Sulla retires because he believes the work is complete and because his confidence in divine favor lets him imagine a restored order surviving without him. The irony is that Pompey, Lepidus, Sertorius, Caesar, and later armed strongmen inherit the precedents Sulla made more than the restraints he wrote into law.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter 1: The World of Sulla

Summary: Keaveney opens with the day in 88 BC when Lucius Cornelius Sulla, as consul, led an army against Rome for the first time in Roman history. He then sketches the republic into which Sulla was born: Rome after Carthage's destruction in 146 BC, Mediterranean empire, consuls, assemblies, Senate, nobiles, client networks, Optimates, Populares, tribunes, the Gracchi, equestrian juries, res repetundae courts, the Italian allies, the Social War, and Marius's army reforms. The chapter argues that Sulla's world was already strained by senatorial dominance, popular challenges, provincial exploitation, Italian exclusion, and competition among aristocratic families before Sulla added military coercion to the mix. Source anchors: 88 BC; Carthage; Senate; nobiles; Optimates; Populares; Italian problem; Social War.

Analysis: 88 BC, Carthage, Senate, nobiles, Optimates, Populares, Italian problem, and Social War make Sulla's march intelligible without excusing it. Carthage marks Rome's imperial scale, while Senate and nobiles show who controlled that empire. Optimates, Populares, Italian problem, and Social War explain why ordinary republican competition had become explosive before Sulla touched Rome with an army.

Chapter 2: The Early Years: 138-105BC

Summary: The second chapter reconstructs Sulla's patrician but impoverished beginnings in the Cornelian gens. Keaveney discusses P. Cornelius Rufinus, the Flamen Dialis, the Ludi Apollinares, Sulla's father, his wealthy stepmother, the false infancy prophecy, Sulla's Greek and Latin education, his poverty after his father's death, the ground-floor apartment, and his exclusion from normal aristocratic office-seeking. Sulla's friendships with actors, his Atellan farces, his first wives Ilia or Julia and Aelia, his relationships with Metrobius and Nicopolis, his golden-red hair, pale face, blue eyes, charm, ambition, and eventual inheritance from his stepmother and Nicopolis lead to the quaestorship under Marius in 107. Source anchors: Cornelian gens; Rufinus; Flamen Dialis; Ludi Apollinares; Nicopolis; Metrobius; quaestorship; Marius.

Analysis: Cornelian gens, Rufinus, Flamen Dialis, Ludi Apollinares, Nicopolis, Metrobius, quaestorship, and Marius show why Keaveney refuses a flat tyrant portrait. Rufinus and Flamen Dialis give Sulla ancestral dignity without current power, while Nicopolis and Metrobius explain the social world that formed his charm and outsider habits. Quaestorship and Marius then move him from delayed ambition into public life.

Chapter 3: The Long Road: 104-89BC

Summary: This chapter follows Sulla's rise after the Jugurthine War, especially the long rivalry with Marius over credit for the capture of Jugurtha through Bocchus. Keaveney traces the Cimbric and Teutonic crises, Sulla's service under Marius and Catulus, his cultivation of soldiers, his dealings with foreign peoples, his praetorship after earlier electoral frustration, and his eastern assignment in Cilicia and Cappadocia. The chapter includes Mithridates, Ariobarzanes, the Parthian ambassador Orobazus, the Chaldean prophecy of felicitas, and the Social War, where Sulla's campaigns at Pompeii, Aesernia, and elsewhere made him a first-rank Roman commander. Source anchors: Jugurtha; Bocchus; Marius; Catulus; Mithridates; Orobazus; felicitas; Social War.

Analysis: Jugurtha, Bocchus, Marius, Catulus, Mithridates, Orobazus, felicitas, and Social War show Sulla accumulating the assets he later spends in crisis. Bocchus gives him diplomatic fame, Marius gives him rivalry, and Catulus helps detach him from Marian dependence. Mithridates and Orobazus introduce the eastern theater, while felicitas and Social War victories let Sulla present success as both skill and divine favor.

Chapter 4: Triumph and Disaster: the Year 88BC

Summary: Chapter 4 concentrates on Sulla's consulship, the Mithridatic command, and the first march on Rome. Keaveney explains the rivalry with Marius, Sulpicius's tribunate, debt politics, the enrolment of new citizens, the transfer of Sulla's command to Marius, the Senate's paralysis, the officers who deserted, and Sulla's appeal to the legions at Nola. Sulla violates the pomerium, fights in the streets and the Esquiline Forum, drives out Marius and Sulpicius, passes laws meant to strengthen senatorial procedure, and then leaves for the East, having set a precedent no Roman could erase. Source anchors: consulship; Mithridatic command; Sulpicius; new citizens; Nola; pomerium; Esquiline Forum; Marius.

Analysis: consulship, Mithridatic command, Sulpicius, new citizens, Nola, pomerium, Esquiline Forum, and Marius make 88 BC the book’s central institutional rupture, and the chapter is a case for Legitimacy and Succession because Sulla turns a constitutional dispute into armed occupation of the city whose authority he claims to restore. Sulpicius and new citizens show the political dispute, while Mithridatic command gives Sulla the personal prize at risk, so dignitas and public settlement become the language in which a magistrate justifies marching on his own capital. Nola, pomerium, and Esquiline Forum turn constitutional conflict into armed occupation, and Marius becomes both rival and warning of what personal military prestige can do, which is the legitimacy-and-succession breakdown in which restorationist language and emergency action begin to feed each other.

Chapter 5: Rome's Proconsul: The War with Mithridates

Summary: Keaveney next follows Sulla east against Mithridates while his position in Rome deteriorates. The chapter covers Mithridates's massacre of Romans and Italians in Asia, Archelaus, Athens, the Piraeus, Chaeronea, Orchomenus, Lucullus, Greek finance, temple plunder, naval shortages, and Sulla's refusal to abandon the eastern war despite enemies at home. Sulla besieges Athens, defeats Mithridatic forces in Greece, negotiates with Archelaus, and at Dardanus imposes peace on Mithridates because he needs to return west to fight the Cinnan regime. Source anchors: Mithridates; Asia; Archelaus; Athens; Piraeus; Chaeronea; Orchomenus; Dardanus.

Analysis: Mithridates, Asia, Archelaus, Athens, Piraeus, Chaeronea, Orchomenus, and Dardanus show Sulla as both Roman proconsul and outlawed commander. Asia and Athens justify the war in Roman terms, while Piraeus, Chaeronea, and Orchomenus display persistence under pressure. Dardanus matters because Sulla chooses a pragmatic settlement with Mithridates so he can turn against Roman enemies.

Chapter 6: Settling Scores: Asia and the Cinnans

Summary: Chapter 6 describes Sulla after the Mithridatic settlement as he punishes Asia and prepares to return to Italy. Keaveney discusses indemnities, tax burdens, confiscations, the punishment of communities that backed Mithridates, Sulla's negotiations with Fimbria's army, the suicide of Fimbria, and the Cinnan control of Rome under Cinna, Carbo, and the younger Marius. Sulla tries to detach governors such as Perperna, manages Lucullus and other loyalists, gathers money and ships, and frames his return as both revenge for proscription and restoration of lawful order. Source anchors: Asia; indemnities; Fimbria; Cinna; Carbo; younger Marius; Perperna; Lucullus.

Analysis: Asia, indemnities, Fimbria, Cinna, Carbo, younger Marius, Perperna, and Lucullus link eastern victory to civil-war preparation. Indemnities make Asian punishment a fiscal weapon, while Fimbria shows how rival Roman armies could dissolve and transfer allegiance. Cinna, Carbo, and younger Marius define the regime Sulla means to overthrow; Lucullus and Perperna show his reliance on personal loyalty and diplomatic pressure.

Chapter 7: Rome's First Civil War

Summary: This chapter follows Sulla's return to Italy in 83 BC and the civil war against the Marian-Cinnan coalition. Keaveney tracks the landing at Brundisium, the defections of Metellus Pius, Pompey, Crassus, and other nobles, the campaigns against Norbanus, Scipio, Carbo, and the younger Marius, the battles around Sacriportus and Praeneste, Samnite resistance, and the decisive fighting at the Colline Gate. The chapter shows Sulla using clemency, intimidation, battlefield skill, and veterans' loyalty until Rome, Italy, and the remaining Marian centers are forced under his control. Source anchors: Brundisium; Metellus Pius; Pompey; Crassus; Norbanus; Praeneste; Samnites; Colline Gate.

Analysis: Brundisium, Metellus Pius, Pompey, Crassus, Norbanus, Praeneste, Samnites, and Colline Gate make the civil war a coalition-building exercise as much as a campaign. Metellus Pius, Pompey, and Crassus show ambitious allies attaching themselves to Sulla, while Norbanus and Praeneste reveal the enemy's fragmentation. Samnites and Colline Gate bring the war to Rome's edge, letting Sulla treat victory as salvation from annihilation.

Chapter 8: Sulla Dictator: The Proscriptions

Summary: Chapter 8 examines Sulla's dictatorship through the proscriptions, the most notorious instrument of his victory. Keaveney discusses the revival of dictatorship, Valerius Flaccus's law, published death lists, rewards for killers, confiscated property, freedmen, informers, equestrian victims, senatorial enemies, and the terror that followed civil war into private households. The proscriptions let Sulla kill opponents, finance settlement, reward followers, and reorder the elite, but they also gave personal vengeance, greed, and political reconstruction a single legal form. Source anchors: dictatorship; Valerius Flaccus; proscriptions; death lists; confiscated property; freedmen; equestrian victims; informers.

Analysis: dictatorship, Valerius Flaccus, proscriptions, death lists, confiscated property, freedmen, equestrian victims, and informers show how Sulla made revenge administrative, and the chapter is a case for State Capacity because the dictatorship converts political vengeance into executable procedure. Valerius Flaccus gives legality to dictatorship, while death lists and rewards for informers turn killing into procedure, so the state’s capacity to count, list, and confiscate is pointed at elimination rather than at governance. Confiscated property and freedmen reveal the social engineering beneath the bloodshed, because punishment also created new dependents and redistributed wealth, which is capacity used to rebuild a senatorial order by methods that make future constitutional government less plausible.

Chapter 9: Sulla Dictator: The Law and the Land

Summary: Keaveney turns from killing to legislation and land. The chapter covers Sulla's enlargement of the Senate, restoration of senatorial juries, regulation of the cursus honorum, limits on the tribunate, rules for provincial commands, expansion of quaestors and praetors, colonies for veterans, confiscations in Italian communities, and the settlement of soldiers on seized land. Sulla's measures are presented as a coherent attempt to make the Senate dominant again and to prevent tribunes, popular assemblies, and ambitious commanders from repeating the disorders that had brought him to power. Source anchors: Senate; senatorial juries; cursus honorum; tribunate; provincial commands; quaestors; veteran colonies; confiscations.

Analysis: Senate, senatorial juries, cursus honorum, tribunate, provincial commands, quaestors, veteran colonies, and confiscations show the double nature of Sulla's settlement. Senate, senatorial juries, cursus honorum, and tribunate express constitutional design. Veteran colonies and confiscations reveal the coercive social base that design required, tying legal restoration to the dispossession of Italian communities.

Chapter 10: Sulla Dictator: The New Age

Summary: Chapter 10 studies the ideological and religious presentation of Sulla's new order. Keaveney discusses felicitas, the title Felix, Apollo, Venus, Ma-Bellona, omens, dreams, the Chaldean prophecy, coins, public ceremonies, and Sulla's claim to be a divinely favored lawgiver. The chapter also treats cultural politics, games, spectacles, and the way Sulla's reforms were meant to inaugurate a renewed age of senatorial rule. Keaveney takes Sulla's religion seriously while noting that the Memoirs likely arranged signs and prophecies to make his life appear crowned by divine approval. Source anchors: felicitas; Felix; Apollo; Venus; Ma-Bellona; omens; Memoirs; lawgiver.

Analysis: felicitas, Felix, Apollo, Venus, Ma-Bellona, omens, Memoirs, and lawgiver explain why Sulla did not see himself merely as a successful general. Apollo, Venus, and Ma-Bellona locate his career in divine patronage, while Felix and felicitas make success a public identity. Memoirs and lawgiver matter because Sulla shaped the evidence of his own life to prove that his constitution had heavenly sanction.

Chapter 11: The Last Years: 79-78BC

Summary: Chapter 11 follows Sulla's retirement, private life, final conflicts, and death. Keaveney describes his resignation of the dictatorship, withdrawal to Campania, continued relations with actors, marriage to Valeria, divorce of the dying Metella for religious reasons, memoir writing, illness, Lepidus's challenge, Pompey's growing power, and the funeral honors after Sulla's death in 78 BC. The chapter presents retirement as both astonishing confidence and a miscalculation: Sulla believed his settlement could stand without him, but the men and precedents he left behind were already straining it. Source anchors: resignation; Campania; actors; Valeria; Metella; Memoirs; Lepidus; Pompey.

Analysis: resignation, Campania, actors, Valeria, Metella, Memoirs, Lepidus, and Pompey show Sulla trying to become private again after remaking the state. Campania and actors reconnect him to the pleasures of youth, while Metella and Memoirs show religion and self-fashioning persisting to the end. Lepidus and Pompey expose the weakness of retirement: Sulla can leave office, but he cannot remove the ambitions his methods empowered.

Chapter 12: Qualis Fuit Sulla?

Summary: The final chapter asks what sort of man Sulla was. Keaveney weighs the warm friend of actors, soldiers, Lucullus, and comrades against the vengeful enemy of Athenians, Asiatics, Norbanus, and proscribed Romans. He analyzes ambition, loyalty, hatred, justice as Sulla understood it, forgiveness when enemies submitted, the abandonment of Marius and Cloelia when politically useful, and the belief in felicitas that fused personal ambition with divine mission. The chapter concludes that Sulla was not a fraud in religion or a mere bloodless reactionary, but a passionate Roman aristocrat whose virtues and vices both helped destroy the republican order he meant to save. Source anchors: actors; soldiers; Lucullus; Norbanus; Marius; Cloelia; felicitas; republican order.

Analysis: actors, soldiers, Lucullus, Norbanus, Marius, Cloelia, felicitas, and republican order gather the biography's evidence into a character judgment. Actors and soldiers support Keaveney's claim that Sulla's warmth was real, while Norbanus, Marius, and Cloelia show revenge and ambition overriding loyalty. Felicitas is the bridge between temperament and politics, because Sulla's confidence in divine favor let him treat his own dominance as a remedy for the republican order.

Appendix: Asia in the Time of Sulla

Summary: The appendix addresses technical problems concerning Asia during Sulla's career, drawing on Badian, Rome-Athens-Mithridates scholarship, provincial boundaries, Cilicia, tax arrangements, and the condition of Greek cities under Mithridates and Roman recovery. It is less a narrative chapter than a source-control section, clarifying disputed geography, chronology, administrative status, and evidence that affect how Sulla's eastern command should be interpreted. The appendix matters because Asia is where Sulla raises money, punishes communities, negotiates with Mithridates, and returns from foreign war to Roman civil war. Source anchors: Asia; Badian; Mithridates; Cilicia; provincial boundaries; tax arrangements; Greek cities; eastern command.

Analysis: Asia, Badian, Mithridates, Cilicia, provincial boundaries, tax arrangements, Greek cities, and eastern command show Keaveney doing the groundwork behind the main narrative. Provincial boundaries and Cilicia affect what authority Sulla actually held, while tax arrangements and Greek cities affect the meaning of his punishments. Badian and Mithridates signal that the appendix is adjudicating scholarly disputes rather than adding a separate life episode.
