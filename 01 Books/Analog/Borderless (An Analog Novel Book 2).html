Borderless (An Analog Novel Book 2)

Buy on Amazon — Borderless (An Analog Novel Book 2)

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer. Its strongest vault role is the series' second-stage account of covert networks, infrastructure dependency, and the moment when anti-feed subculture must become emergency governance.

Detailed overview

Borderless shifts the Analog series center of gravity from Dag Calhoun to Diana, and that matters immediately because the book is organized around secrecy, concealment, and professional masks rather than around Dag's earlier oscillation between cynicism and conscience. Diana is already a practiced infiltrator when the novel opens: she thinks of secrets as the dark matter shaping dreams, fear, and selfhood, and she moves through Analog, Commonwealth, and a string of shell companies and cutouts with the reflexes of someone who has spent years turning intimacy into access. Peper uses her point of view to make espionage feel less like glamorous improvisation than like layered compartmentalization, where every alias, referral, cache, and meeting request leaves another residue of guilt.

The plot itself is an escalation of the conflict that Bandwidth only began. Commonwealth is pushing to extend its network into the territories of the "Prideful Seven," with Mexico already taken, Rachel Leibovitz's defenses under pressure, Lowell scheming across Arctic infrastructure, and Helen offering Diana a grander and more poisonous destiny than she has previously allowed herself to imagine. Haruki hires Diana to investigate Commonwealth through fronts like Leviathan Partners and Hoffman and Associates, but the book quickly makes clear that no one is merely investigating. Everyone is trying to steer the coming collapse in a direction favorable to their own side, whether that side is Commonwealth's board, Analog's feedless counterculture, Rachel's corporate empire, or Lopez's White House.

Dag remains central, but now he is seen through Diana's half-trust, half-need. Peper keeps returning to the damage she did to him and the damage she fears he has done to her. Berkeley rocks, La Jolla ruins, Telegraph Hill, the Hay-Adams, the Ranch, the Lomonosov Ridge, West Potomac Park, and the Oval Office become successive pressure chambers in which Diana must decide whether she is still merely a cutout or whether she is becoming an actor with her own line of causation. That decision is not abstract. It shapes whether she weaponizes Rachel's vulnerability, how she uses Haruki, whether she takes Helen's seductive appeals to "history" seriously, and whether she can get Dag to help without simply lying to him again.

What the novel is doing specifically is staging a border crisis across several scales at once. The title points to geopolitics, because states, corporations, and intelligence networks are colliding over climate, infrastructure, and feed dependency; but it also points to the breakdown of personal borders. Diana's professional discretion is repeatedly exposed as fear. Analog is both fetish and logistics platform. Commonwealth's borderless network ambitions are mirrored by Helen's wish to dissolve the limits that constrain elite action. Even the emergency at the end, when Rachel is forced to contemplate turning everything off, treats connectivity as a border that can either be defended or severed. By the time Diana is bicycling through a San Francisco full of dead cars, flying a P-38 under threat from F-16s, bluffing past White House hierarchies, and working in soil again in the last chapter, Peper has turned a data-thriller premise into a study of crisis governance when platform systems fail.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Diana (birth name Maria): freelance spy and former CIA case officer, protagonist of the novel. Her motivation is professional secrecy itself — she collects secrets as identity and refuge — until the book forces her to spend that capital on trust. Her arc runs from compartmentalized operator to involuntary defector, then to Commonwealth's chief intelligence officer, ending with her choosing trust as "the currency of life."
  • Dag Calhoun: retired Apex lobbyist, Diana's live-in partner, and an artist whose charcoal drawings externalize his guilt over the global carbon-trade and climate disasters he once helped profit from. He becomes Helen's hostage in order to coerce Diana, survives psychological interrogation, and turns back into a strategic operator on the flight south from the Arctic.
  • Helen: senior adviser to President Lopez, once Diana's CIA mentor who took her under her wing, and the mastermind of the whole operation. She plans to nationalize Commonwealth and use the feed's blackout power to seize the first planetary empire. Her maxims ("Espionage requires sacrifice"; "Love if you can't help it. But trust? Never.") frame the moral debate of the book.
  • Rachel Leibovitz: octogenarian chairwoman of Commonwealth, the company that runs the feed. A builder rather than a power-seeker, she is the book's strategic counterweight to Helen; Diana ultimately persuades her to turn the feed off and then to transform Commonwealth into a sovereign, distributed, user-bound geopolitical actor.
  • Lowell Harding: Texas-born oil baron whose empire the carbon tax destroyed; secretly the origin of the plan to nationalize Commonwealth. He is flamboyant, ruthless, and driven by boredom and revenge, and he owns the Ranch in Idaho where the conspiracy is consummated.
  • Haruki: rookie analyst for Hoffman and Associates, a cutout who hires Diana on Lowell's behalf. His amateurish espionage-novel enthusiasm makes him disposable, but Diana recruits him into her counter-plan, and he later helps ferry the group through the crisis.
  • Sofia: Italian refugee and now a top Commonwealth network engineer; Diana's oldest surviving informant, bound by a debt for the asylum Diana arranged for her family. Diana finally releases her from that debt in exchange for one last act.
  • Nell: receptionist of Analog and heir apparent to its owner's role (the owner always serves as receptionist before passing the club on). She is the custodian of Analog's feedless ethos, can read Diana's tradecraft-fogged character better than anyone, and loans Diana her prized Lockheed P-38 to reach Washington before Helen can.
  • Kendrick LaGrange: SEC regulator and Diana's friend and agent inside Washington, who warns the group that Helen has already persuaded the president to approve the raid.
  • Eddie Hsu: Commonwealth board member and architect of Taiwan's political rise, who distrusts the plan but eventually offers Taipei as a refuge and helps legitimate the new sovereign Commonwealth.
  • Javier Flores: activist shareholder and former Commonwealth software architect who once hacked the feed with Emily; he becomes the champion who presses for turning Commonwealth into a transparent, user-accountable institution.
  • Sean Bancroft: Apex lobbying partner, Dag's mentor, and the dying cutout through whom Lowell and Helen's orders flow; Diana breaks him open to find the principal.
  • President Lopez: moderate progressive president who reluctantly signs off on the raid, only to learn from Diana that Helen murdered his predecessor, President Freeman, and seizes the chance to move against her.
  • Freja: Lowell's Danish chief of staff and the operational engine of the Ranch; she represents the coercive machinery behind Lowell's improvisation.

Major linkages

  • Series: This book sits inside Analog and should be read both as a standalone volume and as part of that larger series-scale structure. It is the second volume of the Analog series, following Bandwidth (in which Dag trades Emily and Javier's feed exploit to Rachel for the global carbon tax) and preceding Breach, the third volume that is not locally represented in this vault.
  • Concepts: The novel is the series' fullest working-out of platform governance — Commonwealth's terms-of-service carbon tax is the inciting political fact, and the feed's blackout-power drives the climax. It develops state capacity under strain (Helen's attempted nationalization, Lopez's response), crisis governance when the feed dies, speculative governance in Rachel's sovereign-Commonwealth gambit, intelligence as professional identity and moral burden, and information as the substrate of both addiction and coordination. The club itself grounds chokepoints and gateways and collapse and resilience.
  • Cluster: Thematically it belongs with vault materials on speculative governance, platforms and sovereignty, intelligence and warning, collapse and resilience, and private frontier capital; within the MoC layer it feeds the Science Fiction and Speculative Orders and State, Order, and Legibility maps.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Borders, physical and psychological: the title names national and corporate borders (climate, infrastructure, feed dependency) while the plot dissolves personal borders — Diana's professional discretion repeatedly collapses into fear, and Helen offers to dissolve the limits constraining elite action.
  • Secrecy versus trust: the book pits Helen's doctrine ("trust? Never") against Diana's late discovery that trust is the "currency of life"; every operation converts an intimate bond into leverage.
  • Platform dependency as both superpower and hostage system: the feed is infrastructure for all of civilization in the novel, which means whoever controls it can blackmail states and dissolve ordinary life — and can also be brought down by the same dependence.
  • Coercion disguised as recruitment: Helen, Lowell, and Haruki each recruit through staged intimacy, fabricated evidence, and obligation; the reader should track who is a cutout, who a principal, and who is being groomed.
  • Empire versus republic / bastion: Helen argues for planetary empire to outrun decayed national institutions; Diana counters with the "bastion" ideal of American self-determination, and Rachel chooses a third path — a sovereign, non-state, borderless political institution.
  • The moral status of espionage: Diana's self-justifications ("I do what I do for my country") are dismantled across the novel until she can no longer outsource her conscience to Helen or any agency.
  • Embodied practice and resilience: gardening, drawing, bicycle escape, the P-38, Analog's feedless rituals — practices that survive the death of the feed are repeatedly privileged over platform-enabling technologies.

Core concepts

  • Platform Governance: Commonwealth enacts the global carbon tax through its terms of service, making the feed's operator a de facto legislator; Helen's nationalization plan and Rachel's re-founding show both capture and reform of a platform as a governing institution.
  • State Capacity: the novel tests state power at the breaking point — a fabricated treason case lets Helen seize Commonwealth, while Lopez's eventual investigation shows the state reasserting itself once the principal is exposed.
  • Crisis Governance: with the feed turned off, emergency landings, stalled cars, frozen drones, and ICUs without power become the real stakes; crisis governance means choosing who starves and who survives.
  • Speculative Governance: Rachel's plan — coequal headquarters on every continent, a share buyback, declared sovereignty, algorithmic user benefits — is a speculative model of borderless, non-state governance in direct competition with nations.
  • Intelligence: espionage appears as layered compartmentalization (caches, cutouts, aliases, referrals) and as the moral damage done to the people who carry secrets; Diana's professional identity is itself the book's central problem.
  • Information and Coordination: the feed organizes attention, deceptions, and interlocking systems; Diana's tracker coiled inside her own report and her fabricated geotagged activity show information warfare operating beneath every political move.
  • Chokepoints and Gateways: Analog is a gateway that selects and measures people while stripping their feed; Commonwealth's control of the feed is the ultimate chokepoint, and Lowell's "death by a thousand small conveniences" names it directly.
  • Collapse and Resilience: the dead-car San Francisco and the blacked-out world stage collapse, while Analog's embodied practices and low-tech logistics stage resilience.
  • Coercive Labor: Diana's debts with Sofia, Sean, and Kendrick, and Dag's hostage interrogation, all show information extracted through obligation, threat, and staged care rather than consent.
  • Language and Ideology: Helen's maxims ("take your place in history"; "America is our only soul mate") recruit through destiny-talk and ideology dressed as duty.
  • Ecological Constraint: climate change is everywhere — LA's fire, DC's levees, the Arctic's melted routes, Bulgarian floods — and it is the pressure that made the carbon tax both possible and contested.
  • Elite Formation: the Ranch, the board, the federal boarding house, and the secret service circle reproduce elite formation through status contests, staged intimacy, and shared secrets.
  • Informal Institutions: cutouts, referrals, caches, client chains, and personal debts form the informal architecture on which formal power actually runs.
  • Commercial Society: Leviathan Partners shorting the market, Hoffman's white-paper cover, and Commonwealth's algorithmic user dividends show commercial institutions running alongside and into politics.
  • Technology, Platforms, and Speculative Systems: the P-38, the sunflower recorder, the brick-like pre-feed hardware, and the seasteaders converting oil rigs into tiny sovereigns instantiate the speculative systems the series tracks.
  • Artificial Intelligence: implicit in the feed's algorithms that "pilot" vehicles, score users, and generate learning materials — automated cognition is the substrate the human plot manipulates and resists.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

CHAPTER 1

Summary: The novel opens in Diana's head with the claim that secrets define people, and Peper immediately roots that idea in tradecraft rather than abstraction. Diana receives a meeting request routed through a Bogotá-based referral, thinks in terms of caches and compartmentalization, and treats secrets not as shameful leftovers but as the premium fuel of her professional life. Her appetite for them is described almost chemically, as though once she learned the taste nothing else could satisfy. The chapter introduces Diana's habit of self-concealment, her disciplined feel for covert work, and the moral cost of making secrecy into identity. Source anchors: Diana; secrets define us; dark matter; Bogotá referral; caches; appetite for secrets.

Analysis: Diana and the Bogotá referral matter because the book wants secrecy to arrive as a working environment, not a slogan. The caches and the appetite for secrets tell you how deeply trained she already is: concealment is not something she occasionally deploys but the medium she breathes. By making "secrets define us" the opening proposition and tying it to Diana's body rather than to a manifesto, Peper turns intelligence work into an identity problem: she can collect, compartmentalize, and route secrets, but she may no longer know how to live outside their dark matter.

CHAPTER 2

Summary: Diana moves through Analog, appreciating how the club stands apart from the glistening towers and manicured Victorians around it, and the chapter carefully rebuilds Analog as a physical place with red satin curtains, Nell's supervision, and feedless disorientation. Diana circles a room, calibrates space, and notices how the club's architecture supports surveillance and staging while still pretending to be leisure. She is not there socially; she is waiting for a newcomer. When Nell brings in a man and pauses to let him adjust, Diana reads his arrival not as casual nightlife but as the beginning of an operation. Source anchors: Analog; red satin curtains; Nell; feedlessness; surveillance; newcomer.

Analysis: Analog and Nell matter because the club is both sanctuary and machine, and Diana is alert to both sides. The red satin curtains and feedlessness are not atmospheric trim; they are the mechanisms by which people are destabilized and reset before being handled. By making Diana study the newcomer as he enters Nell's territory, Peper shows that Analog is no longer only a symbol of resistance to feeds but a gateway through which people are selected, measured, and moved.

CHAPTER 3

Summary: Haruki hires Diana to look into Commonwealth, and she immediately starts pressing through the cover story. He arrives in a tailored suit with all the polish of a man used to appearing indispensable, but Diana forces specifics: Leviathan Partners, Hoffman and Associates, shadowing Commonwealth executives, and the difference between "differentiated market intelligence" and the real assignment. Haruki eventually confirms enough to let her see the card pattern even if he does not reveal every layer. Diana listens less for confession than for self-incrimination, because knowing what he is willing to say tells her what hand he is actually holding. Source anchors: Haruki; Commonwealth; Leviathan Partners; Hoffman and Associates; shadow Commonwealth execs; differentiated market intelligence.

Analysis: Haruki and Commonwealth matter because the novel's corporate-intelligence conflict has to be established through actual front organizations, not just vague mutual suspicion. Leviathan Partners and Hoffman and Associates are the chapter's concrete proof that this job is being laundered through layers of plausible deniability. Diana's insistence on hearing Haruki say the arrangement aloud matters because she catches people by mapping the distance between their script and their fear, exposing informal institutions of cutouts, referrals, and deniable client chains.

CHAPTER 4

Summary: Diana follows Sofia up a hard trail under manzanita branches, sweat and blood pounding in her ears, and the chapter becomes a coercive field interview rather than a desk briefing. Sofia's bouncing ponytail, the exposed terrain, and the physical climb keep the conversation from becoming polite. Diana asks what she wants to know even while already suspecting the answer, because this is about extracting a debt as much as gathering information. By the end Sofia gives in with exhausted resignation, and Diana recognizes the hollow quality of that victory. Source anchors: Sofia; manzanita branch; trail climb; debt; resignation; hollow victory.

Analysis: Sofia and the trail climb matter because Peper wants this interrogation to feel bodily costly for both women. The manzanita branch, the sweat, and the debt make the scene specific: Diana is not magically reading a dossier but squeezing an answer out of a relationship already warped by obligation. The hollow victory matters because it reveals a limit in Diana's method early in the novel; she can get information, but coercive obligation keeps staining the result.

CHAPTER 5

Summary: Walking alone on the levee above West Potomac Park, Diana takes in a Washington reshaped by rising water and remembers her years stationed in Amsterdam, the European country that survives sea-level rise by marketing hydrology expertise to the world. Her feed announces that Kendrick is approaching, and the chapter becomes a reunion between two old friends: he is a big, quietly impressive SEC regulator newly tasked with overseeing Commonwealth, and they smoke a joint of California kush she has brought, trading nostalgia for vanished dive bars while Kendrick shows off his life with his husband Rob, their toddler daughter Dena, and their new golden retriever. The warmth curdles when Diana turns to business. First she establishes that Leviathan Partners is a short-selling hedge fund that makes its fortune by exposing dirty secrets. Then, switching gears over the shared joint, she asks what is really going on inside Commonwealth. Kendrick's face drains and his eyelid twitches: someone else has already been asking the same questions, the datasets have been requisitioned, and he refuses to back-channel with every branch of the government.
