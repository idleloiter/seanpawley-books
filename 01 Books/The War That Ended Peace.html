The War That Ended Peace

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer.

Detailed overview

Margaret MacMillan writes the road to 1914 as an accumulation of choices made inside a Europe that had grown used to peace but kept building institutions, habits, plans, and expectations that made a general war easier to start. The book opens with Louvain in August 1914 because the burned university library, the German reprisals, the 1,100 destroyed houses, and the deported civilians make the war visible as an assault on the civilization Europeans believed they had perfected. From that image, MacMillan works backward to ask how a continent with the Concert of Europe, peace congresses, dense trade, shared aristocratic culture, and memories of earlier crises nevertheless reached mobilization and invasion within five weeks of Sarajevo.

The early chapters map the powers and their self-images around 1900. Paris's Exposition, Queen Victoria's Jubilee, the Boer War, Theodore Roosevelt's United States, Kaiser Wilhelm II's court, Weltpolitik, and the Anglo-German naval rivalry show a world full of confidence but also status anxiety. Britain still imagines splendid isolation, Germany wants a place on the world stage, France wants security and recovery after 1870, Russia seeks prestige after humiliation, Austria-Hungary fears Slavic nationalism, and the Ottoman Empire appears as both weakening neighbor and object of great-power calculation. MacMillan keeps returning to the way prestige, fear of decline, and public symbolism shape practical policy.

The alliance chapters are especially attentive to contingency. The Entente Cordiale is not presented as inevitable friendship but as a settlement of colonial irritants around Egypt, Morocco, and Fashoda; the Anglo-Russian understanding grows from Central Asian, Persian, Afghan, and Tibetan calculations as much as European ones; and the Dual Alliance binds Germany to Austria-Hungary through loyalty, need, and narrowing options. The book's political geography is not a static map. Morocco, Bosnia-Herzegovina, the Straits, Serbia, Bulgaria, Tripoli, Albania, Macedonia, and the North Sea repeatedly force leaders to decide whether a local quarrel is worth a European confrontation.

MacMillan also spends long sections on ideas, peace movements, and war planning because she does not reduce 1914 to diplomacy alone. Social Darwinism, nationalism, military honor, masculinity, fear of socialism, anarchist violence, The Hague conferences, Bertha von Suttner, Jean de Bloch, Alfred von Schlieffen, Helmuth von Moltke, Joseph Joffre, Conrad von Hotzendorf, and Russian mobilization timetables all matter because leaders inhabited a world of concepts as well as telegrams. Peace was imaginable and actively pursued, but war was also imagined as cleansing, inevitable, short, or manageable; those assumptions made brinkmanship more dangerous.

The crisis chapters show repeated rehearsals before the final disaster. The First Moroccan Crisis, Algeciras, the Bosnian annexation, Agadir, the Italo-Turkish War, the Balkan Wars, and the arguments over Serbia train governments to use threats and then expect someone else to contain the damage. In July 1914, that pattern fails. Austria-Hungary uses the murder of Franz Ferdinand and Sophie at Sarajevo to confront Serbia; Germany gives Vienna support; Russia cannot accept another humiliation over Serbia; France stands by Russia; Britain hesitates over Belgium, France, and the fleet; and mobilization schedules convert diplomatic pressure into military action.

The book's title is literal: the war ended a peace that had been maintained by habits, restraint, shared assumptions, and luck rather than by any automatic law of progress. MacMillan's strongest claim is that the Great War was not inevitable, but neither was it accidental. She treats individuals such as Wilhelm II, Edward VII, Sir Edward Grey, Raymond Poincare, Nicholas II, Bethmann-Hollweg, Berchtold, Conrad, Sazonov, and Princip as people whose decisions mattered, while showing that those decisions were constrained by crisis-governance habits, alliances, railway plans, parliaments, newspapers, military staffs, and inherited memories of previous crises.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Introduction: War or Peace?

Summary: MacMillan begins at Louvain, where German troops entered Belgium in August 1914, panicked after rumors of Belgian and British attacks, shot civilians including the mayor and university officials, deported 1,500 inhabitants, and burned the university library with its 200,000 books. She then connects Louvain to Rheims Cathedral, Ypres, Treviso, the Lusitania, the 9 million dead soldiers, the fall of Russia, Germany, Austria-Hungary, and the Ottoman Empire, and the later memory of the war in family medals, unexploded ordnance, memoirs, and paintings. The introduction sets out possible causes: arms races, military plans, economic rivalry, imperialism, alliances, nationalism, Social Darwinism, honor, domestic politics, and the decisions of monarchs and ministers during the five weeks from June 28 to August 4, 1914. Source anchors: Louvain; August 25; 200,000 books; Rheims Cathedral; June 28; August 4.

Analysis: Louvain, August 25, 200,000 books, Rheims Cathedral, June 28, and August 4 define the book's scale and method. Louvain turns an abstract European war into a concrete civilizational wound, while Rheims Cathedral shows that symbolic destruction quickly became part of the war's political meaning. June 28 and August 4 let MacMillan hold two time spans together: the long accumulation before 1914 and the compressed decision period after Sarajevo. The 200,000 books make knowledge preservation part of the war's meaning because the loss tests Europe's belief that culture, education, and progress had made such violence obsolete.

1. Europe in 1900

Summary: The first chapter uses the Paris Exposition of 1900 to show Europe at the height of confidence, with exhibits, moving pavements, peace congresses, colonial displays, military technology, the Alexander III bridge, and the newly visible Franco-Russian alliance. MacMillan places that confidence beside doubts raised by Einstein, Freud's Interpretation of Dreams, Gustave Le Bon on crowds, Durkheim on social dislocation, neurasthenia, Japanese art, African sculpture, Harry Kessler's reflections on Japan, and the Palace of Armies and Navies. She also introduces Britain after the Boer War, Kitchener's concentration camps, the Transvaal pavilion, Theodore Roosevelt's expanding United States, the Spanish-American War, the Philippines, Puerto Rico, Cuba, Hawaii, and the Concert of Europe as both achievement and fragile habit. Source anchors: Paris Exposition; Alexander III bridge; Boer War; Theodore Roosevelt; Concert of Europe; Palace of Armies.

Analysis: Paris Exposition, Alexander III bridge, Boer War, Theodore Roosevelt, Concert of Europe, and Palace of Armies make 1900 look prosperous but unstable. The Paris Exposition celebrates progress while the Palace of Armies advertises improved killing power, so MacMillan can show optimism and danger in the same fairground. The Alexander III bridge makes the Franco-Russian alliance visible in stone, while the Boer War exposes British vulnerability and pushes London toward partners. Theodore Roosevelt and the Concert of Europe widen the story beyond the old continental powers: the United States is rising, and Europe's old consultation habits depend on leaders continuing to value them.

2. Great Britain and Splendid Isolation

Summary: MacMillan presents Britain as the greatest empire in 1900 but also as a power uneasy after Queen Victoria's Jubilee, the Boer War, criticism over concentration camps, and the discovery that the army and imperial prestige were less secure than assumed. Salisbury, Balfour, Chamberlain, Curzon, Kipling, the Royal Navy, the Indian Empire, Egypt, South Africa, and the dominions appear in a system that prized freedom of action and distrusted permanent continental commitments. The chapter follows Britain's search for room to maneuver through the Venezuelan boundary dispute, accommodation with the United States, the Anglo-Japanese Alliance of 1902, and the recognition that Germany, Russia, France, and imperial overstretch could no longer be handled from isolation alone. Source anchors: Queen Victoria's Jubilee; Salisbury; Boer War; Anglo-Japanese Alliance; Royal Navy; splendid isolation.

Analysis: Queen Victoria's Jubilee, Salisbury, Boer War, Anglo-Japanese Alliance, Royal Navy, and splendid isolation explain why British policy changes without Britain thinking of itself as joining a fixed camp. Salisbury's caution and the Royal Navy support splendid isolation when Britain feels strong, but the Boer War damages that confidence. The Anglo-Japanese Alliance shows London solving an Asian problem with a non-European partner and admitting that naval power alone cannot cover every imperial exposure. This chapter matters because Britain does not enter the later Ententes from sentimental friendship with France or Russia; it enters because isolation has become expensive and risky.

3. "Woe to the Country That Has a Child for King!": Wilhelm II and Germany

Summary: This chapter centers on Kaiser Wilhelm II as ruler, performer, grandson of Queen Victoria, admirer of uniforms and ships, and a man whose insecurity shaped German diplomacy after Bismarck. MacMillan follows Wilhelm's accession, the dismissal of Bismarck, the court atmosphere, the Kruger telegram, the Daily Telegraph affair, his relations with Edward VII, Nicholas II, and his own ministers, and the way his speeches and impulses interacted with Germany's federal constitution, Prussian militarism, and industrial expansion. Germany's strength is real: population, steel, coal, science, universities, railways, and the army give it weight, but Wilhelm's need for recognition makes policy noisy and unpredictable. Source anchors: Wilhelm II; Bismarck; Kruger telegram; Daily Telegraph; Edward VII; Prussian army.

Analysis: Wilhelm II, Bismarck, Kruger telegram, Daily Telegraph, Edward VII, and Prussian army let MacMillan connect personality to institutions without making the Kaiser the sole cause of war. Bismarck's dismissal removes a cautious manager, while the Kruger telegram and Daily Telegraph affair turn Wilhelm's words into international incidents. Edward VII matters because Wilhelm reads family and diplomacy through rivalry and slight. The Prussian army gives Germany formidable strength, but it also reinforces a political culture in which military solutions receive deference, especially when civilian direction is confused by the Kaiser's interventions.

4. Weltpolitik: Germany's Place on the World Stage

Summary: MacMillan explains Weltpolitik as Germany's bid for status, colonies, naval power, and global recognition after unification had made it Europe's strongest land power. Bernhard von Bulow, Alfred von Tirpitz, Wilhelm II, German nationalists, colonial leagues, the Pan-German League, the Berlin-Baghdad railway, Kiaochow, Samoa, Morocco, and Mahan's theory of sea power appear as signs of a country seeking "a place in the sun." Tirpitz's naval laws and risk theory are especially important because they promise Germany a fleet strong enough to deter Britain even if it cannot defeat the Royal Navy outright. The chapter shows how prestige politics, domestic pressure, industrial strength, and fear of encirclement become mutually reinforcing. Source anchors: Weltpolitik; Bulow; Tirpitz; place in the sun; Berlin-Baghdad railway; risk theory.

Analysis: Weltpolitik, Bulow, Tirpitz, place in the sun, Berlin-Baghdad railway, and risk theory explain why Germany's ambitions alarmed other powers even when German leaders described them as defensive or normal. Bulow gives the policy a public language of dignity, Tirpitz gives it ships, and risk theory turns British naval security into a German bargaining instrument. The Berlin-Baghdad railway shows that German reach is not limited to the North Sea. The phrase place in the sun matters because status becomes a policy objective in itself, making compromise harder when symbolic recognition is treated as national necessity.

5. Dreadnought: The Anglo-German Naval Rivalry

Summary: The chapter follows the naval race from British invasion scares and popular press agitation to Fisher's reforms, HMS Dreadnought, German naval laws, Tirpitz's building program, and the British fear that command of the sea could be threatened close to home. MacMillan includes Northcliffe's newspapers, William Le Queux's invasion fiction, the Naval Scare, the Two-Power Standard, the cost of battleships, dominion contributions, the Kiel Canal, and the technical leap that made older ships obsolete. Britain responds with more building, budget fights, and closer attention to Germany, while Germany treats the fleet as both shield and symbol. Source anchors: HMS Dreadnought; Fisher; Tirpitz; Northcliffe; Two-Power Standard; Kiel Canal.

Analysis: HMS Dreadnought, Fisher, Tirpitz, Northcliffe, Two-Power Standard, and Kiel Canal show how technology, public fear, and strategic geography produce mistrust. Fisher's reforms are meant to preserve British superiority, but HMS Dreadnought resets the count and gives Tirpitz a chance to narrow the visible gap. Northcliffe turns naval numbers into mass politics, while the Two-Power Standard makes every German hull feel like a direct challenge. The Kiel Canal matters because it gives German ships operational flexibility between the Baltic and North Sea, making British fears more concrete than a general dislike of Germany.

6. Unlikely Friends: The Entente Cordiale Between France and Britain

Summary: MacMillan traces the Entente Cordiale through old Anglo-French hostility, Fashoda, Egypt, Morocco, colonial bargains, Edward VII's Paris visit, Theophile Delcasse's diplomacy, and the French need for security after defeat by Prussia. The chapter emphasizes that the Entente was not an alliance at first; it settled disputes over Egypt, Morocco, Newfoundland, West Africa, and Siam while leaving military commitments ambiguous. Yet German reactions, French expectations, British conversations between staffs, and repeated crises gradually gave the arrangement strategic meaning beyond its written terms. Source anchors: Fashoda; Entente Cordiale; Egypt; Morocco; Edward VII; Delcasse.

Analysis: Fashoda, Entente Cordiale, Egypt, Morocco, Edward VII, and Delcasse reveal how colonial settlement can become European alignment. Fashoda had nearly brought Britain and France to war, so resolving Egypt and Morocco removes a major source of friction. Edward VII's Paris visit and Delcasse's work add public warmth to practical bargaining, which makes the Entente politically usable in both countries. Morocco becomes the test: once Germany challenges French influence there, Britain begins to treat the colonial settlement as coordination infrastructure against German pressure.

7. The Bear and the Whale: Russia and Great Britain

Summary: This chapter follows the long Anglo-Russian rivalry across Central Asia, Persia, Afghanistan, Tibet, the Ottoman Straits, and India, then shows how defeat in the Russo-Japanese War and revolution in 1905 weakened Russia and encouraged accommodation. MacMillan discusses Nicholas II, Sergei Witte, the Portsmouth peace mediated by Theodore Roosevelt, Bloody Sunday, the Duma, Stolypin, British concern for India, and the 1907 Anglo-Russian Convention dividing interests in Persia and clarifying Afghanistan and Tibet. The agreement does not make Britain and Russia natural friends, but it reduces imperial friction and helps create the Triple Entente alongside France. Source anchors: Russo-Japanese War; Nicholas II; Sergei Witte; Bloody Sunday; Persia; Anglo-Russian Convention.

Analysis: Russo-Japanese War, Nicholas II, Sergei Witte, Bloody Sunday, Persia, and Anglo-Russian Convention show a rivalry being managed after both sides find the old contest too costly. The Russo-Japanese War and Bloody Sunday weaken Nicholas II's room for adventure, while Witte's role at Portsmouth shows Russia accepting mediation by a rising United States. Persia is the key imperial space where British India and Russian expansion had collided. The Anglo-Russian Convention matters because it closes one more open quarrel among Germany's potential opponents, even though it remains a pragmatic bargain rather than deep trust.

8. The Loyalty of the Nibelungs: The Dual Alliance of Austria-Hungary and Germany

Summary: MacMillan examines Austria-Hungary's dependence on Germany through Franz Joseph, the Habsburg monarchy's nationalities, Hungary, Bohemia, Galicia, South Slavs, the Balkans, and the army's fear of internal disintegration. The Dual Alliance promises Germany an ally and Austria-Hungary protection, but it also binds Berlin to a weakening empire whose quarrels with Serbia and Russia grow more dangerous. Figures such as Conrad von Hotzendorf, Aehrenthal, Berchtold, Franz Ferdinand, and Hungarian leader Istvan Tisza appear inside a monarchy trying to manage Slavic nationalism while preserving imperial authority. The Nibelung language captures loyalty as romance, obligation, and trap. Source anchors: Franz Joseph; Dual Alliance; Conrad; Franz Ferdinand; South Slavs; Istvan Tisza.

Analysis: Franz Joseph, Dual Alliance, Conrad, Franz Ferdinand, South Slavs, and Istvan Tisza explain why Austria-Hungary becomes both Germany's asset and Germany's liability. Franz Joseph personifies continuity, but the South Slavs and other nationalities make that continuity brittle. Conrad repeatedly imagines preventive war as a cure for imperial weakness, while Istvan Tisza often worries about the consequences for Hungary. Franz Ferdinand's reform interests complicate the picture because his death removes one possible, if uncertain, alternative. The Dual Alliance matters because Germany's fear of losing its only dependable ally turns alliance coordination into the blank check of 1914.

9. What Were They Thinking? Hopes, Fears, Ideas, and Unspoken Assumptions

Summary: This chapter surveys the mental world before 1914: nationalism, Social Darwinism, masculinity, honor, fears of decadence, class conflict, anarchism, socialism, anti-Semitism, imperial racism, and the belief that struggle could strengthen nations. MacMillan uses writers, soldiers, politicians, artists, and public controversies such as Zabern to show how violence and discipline were discussed long before the war. Nietzsche, Treitschke, Bernhardi, Cubism, Boy Scouts, school curricula, conscription, press campaigns, and fears of revolution all enter as evidence that ideas about vitality and decline influenced policy. She does not claim everyone wanted war, but she shows how many educated Europeans had language ready to justify it. Source anchors: Social Darwinism; honor; Zabern; Nietzsche; Bernhardi; Boy Scouts.

Analysis: Social Darwinism, honor, Zabern, Nietzsche, Bernhardi, and Boy Scouts give MacMillan a vocabulary for the pressures beneath formal diplomacy. Social Darwinism and Bernhardi make war appear natural or even healthy, while honor makes retreat from a crisis look morally shameful. Zabern shows militarism clashing with civilian society inside Germany, not merely between states. Nietzsche's reception and Boy Scouts training reveal how ideological language can bend cultural material toward hardness, discipline, and national readiness. These ideas do not mechanically cause 1914, but they make violent choices easier to defend when leaders face humiliation or uncertainty.

10. Dreaming of Peace

Summary: MacMillan balances the war-minded material with the prewar peace movement, international law, arbitration, The Hague conferences, Bertha von Suttner, Jean de Bloch, Norman Angell, socialist internationalism, free trade arguments, and hopes that finance and interdependence made major war irrational. She shows peace activism in congresses, pamphlets, parliamentary efforts, women's organizations, religious groups, and the work of Andrew Carnegie and others who funded institutions meant to replace war with law. Yet the chapter also notes the limits of these efforts: governments refused binding arbitration on vital interests, militaries resisted constraints, and socialists split when national war came. Source anchors: Hague conferences; Bertha von Suttner; Jean de Bloch; Norman Angell; arbitration; Carnegie.

Analysis: Hague conferences, Bertha von Suttner, Jean de Bloch, Norman Angell, arbitration, and Carnegie prevent the book from treating prewar Europe as simply eager for war. The Hague conferences and arbitration show real institutional attempts to civilize disputes, while Bertha von Suttner and Carnegie give the peace movement public names and money. Jean de Bloch and Norman Angell argue that modern war would be economically and militarily self-defeating, which many readers found persuasive. The failure lies not in absence of peace thinking but in weak crisis governance: these mechanisms cannot bind governments when honor, alliances, and mobilization timetables take command.

11. Thinking About War

Summary: This chapter turns to soldiers and civilians who imagined what a European war would be like, including debates over offense, defense, morale, firepower, conscription, reserves, railways, and the lessons drawn from the Franco-Prussian War, the Russo-Japanese War, and colonial fighting. MacMillan discusses French faith in elan, German confidence in staff work, Russian manpower, British expeditionary limits, Austro-Hungarian worries, and the assumption in many capitals that war would be short. Joseph Joffre, Ferdinand Foch, Moltke, Conrad, and military writers all appear in an atmosphere where doctrine often translated political fear into offensive planning. Source anchors: elan; Franco-Prussian War; Russo-Japanese War; Joffre; Moltke; Conrad.

Analysis: Elan, Franco-Prussian War, Russo-Japanese War, Joffre, Moltke, and Conrad show why military professionals could see evidence of modern firepower yet still favor attack. The Franco-Prussian War encouraged hopes for decisive campaigns, while the Russo-Japanese War offered mixed lessons that armies interpreted selectively. Joffre and elan give France a doctrine tied to morale and national recovery. Moltke and Conrad embody staff systems under pressure to solve political problems quickly. The chapter matters because leaders in 1914 did not improvise war from nothing; they activated ideas already built into doctrine, training, and railway assumptions.

12. Making the Plans

Summary: MacMillan details how general staffs converted fears into timetables, especially the Schlieffen Plan, German assumptions about Belgium, French Plan XVII, Russian mobilization schedules, Austro-Hungarian plans against Serbia and Russia, and British expeditionary planning. Railways, mobilization zones, reserve call-ups, frontier deployments, and staff secrecy turn diplomacy into a race once orders begin. Schlieffen, Moltke, Joffre, Sukhomlinov, Conrad, and the British Committee of Imperial Defence appear as planners who sought certainty in scenarios that left little flexibility. The chapter shows that plans did not cause the crisis, but they shaped what leaders believed they could still control after the crisis began. Source anchors: Schlieffen Plan; Belgium; Plan XVII; railways; Sukhomlinov; Committee of Imperial Defence.

Analysis: Schlieffen Plan, Belgium, Plan XVII, railways, Sukhomlinov, and Committee of Imperial Defence are the machinery behind July 1914. Belgium matters because German operational logic collides with British treaty and public commitments. Plan XVII reveals French willingness to attack into Alsace-Lorraine, while the Schlieffen Plan makes speed through Belgium central to German hopes. Railways and Sukhomlinov's Russian mobilization work turn logistics and throughput into a political constraint by reducing time for reconsideration. The Committee of Imperial Defence shows Britain planning without making an open alliance, which leaves ambiguity that later complicates deterrence and reassurance.

13. The Crises Start: Germany, France and Morocco

Summary: The Moroccan crises begin when Germany challenges French influence, with Wilhelm II's Tangier visit in 1905, Delcasse's fall, the Algeciras Conference, French ambitions in Morocco, British support for France, and German frustration at diplomatic isolation. MacMillan shows Morocco as colonial terrain that becomes a European test of prestige and alignment. The crisis hardens the Entente because Britain sees German pressure as bullying and France sees British backing as useful, while Germany learns that dramatic gestures can produce conferences but not necessarily gains. Source anchors: Tangier; Algeciras Conference; Delcasse; Morocco; Wilhelm II; Entente.

Analysis: Tangier, Algeciras Conference, Delcasse, Morocco, Wilhelm II, and Entente show a recurring pattern in the book: a colonial issue becomes a European measuring stick. Wilhelm II's Tangier performance is meant to defend German status, but the Algeciras Conference exposes Germany's limited support. Delcasse's removal gives Berlin a tactical success without changing the underlying French-British connection. Morocco matters because it lets Britain and France practice cooperation under pressure, while Germany experiences diplomatic disappointment that feeds later suspicion of encirclement.

14. The Bosnian Crisis: Confrontation Between Russia and Austria-Hungary in the Balkans

Summary: MacMillan moves to the Balkans, where Austria-Hungary's annexation of Bosnia-Herzegovina in 1908 enrages Serbia and humiliates Russia, which had expected compensation through the Straits after talks involving Aehrenthal and Izvolsky. Bosnia, Herzegovina, Serbia, Russia, Austria-Hungary, Germany, the Ottoman Empire, and the Young Turk revolution all intersect in a crisis that ends without war because Russia backs down under German pressure. Serbia is forced to accept the annexation, but resentment deepens, Russian leaders remember the humiliation, and Austria-Hungary concludes that firmness can work if Germany stands behind it. Source anchors: Bosnia-Herzegovina; 1908; Aehrenthal; Izvolsky; Serbia; Russian humiliation.

Analysis: Bosnia-Herzegovina, 1908, Aehrenthal, Izvolsky, Serbia, and Russian humiliation make this chapter one of the clearest rehearsals for 1914. Aehrenthal's move succeeds because Russia is too weak after 1905 to fight, and Izvolsky becomes associated with diplomatic failure. Serbia's anger is not resolved; it is stored as national humiliation. Russian humiliation matters because later Russian leaders use it as a warning against backing down again. Austria-Hungary and Germany also learn a dangerous lesson: if Berlin applies pressure and Vienna holds firm, the other side may yield.

15. 1911: The Year of Discords: Morocco Again

Summary: The second Moroccan crisis centers on Agadir, where the German gunboat Panther appears after French moves in Morocco, raising fears of a German Atlantic naval base and provoking British alarm. MacMillan follows Bethmann-Hollweg, Kiderlen-Waechter, Lloyd George's Mansion House speech, French and German bargaining, Congo compensation, and the way public opinion in Britain, France, and Germany hardened. The crisis ends in settlement, with France's position in Morocco confirmed and Germany compensated in Africa, but the episode strengthens British suspicion and convinces many Germans that they had again been denied respect. Source anchors: Agadir; Panther; Bethmann-Hollweg; Lloyd George; Mansion House; Congo.

Analysis: Agadir, Panther, Bethmann-Hollweg, Lloyd George, Mansion House, and Congo show brinkmanship becoming more theatrical and less controllable. The Panther makes a colonial dispute look like a naval threat to Britain, while Lloyd George's Mansion House speech signals that Britain will not ignore German pressure on France. Bethmann-Hollweg's government gains Congo territory but loses politically because German nationalists see the outcome as insufficient. Agadir matters because it pushes military conversations between Britain and France further and feeds German beliefs that prestige cannot be secured through diplomacy alone.

16. The First Balkan Wars

Summary: MacMillan explains how the Balkan League of Serbia, Bulgaria, Greece, and Montenegro attacked the Ottoman Empire in 1912, winning unexpectedly large victories and creating new disputes over Macedonia, Albania, Adrianople, and access to the Adriatic. Russia hopes to guide Slavic clients, Austria-Hungary fears a stronger Serbia, Germany watches its ally, Britain tries conference diplomacy, and the Ottoman retreat destabilizes the region. The Second Balkan War then pits Bulgaria against former allies and leaves Serbia enlarged, resentful over Albania, and more important to Russia, while Austria-Hungary feels increasingly threatened by Serbian ambition. Source anchors: Balkan League; Serbia; Bulgaria; Ottoman Empire; Albania; Adriatic.

Analysis: Balkan League, Serbia, Bulgaria, Ottoman Empire, Albania, and Adriatic show why the Balkans were not a peripheral nuisance but a pressure point in the alliance system. The Ottoman Empire's defeats create opportunities that Serbia and Bulgaria fight to divide. Albania blocks Serbia's Adriatic hopes largely because Austria-Hungary insists on it, so Serbian nationalism turns more sharply toward Habsburg South Slav lands. Russia's support for Serbia becomes more valuable after the Balkan Wars, while Austria-Hungary sees delay as dangerous. The chapter prepares the reader for why Sarajevo matters beyond one assassination.

17. Preparing for War or Peace: Europe's Last Months of Peace

Summary: This chapter surveys 1913 and early 1914, when governments still debated budgets, elections, reforms, alliances, and crises while Europe appeared outwardly peaceful. MacMillan discusses Poincare, Jaures, Asquith, Grey, Churchill, Nicholas II, Sazonov, Berchtold, Conrad, Bethmann-Hollweg, the arms race, French three-year service, Russian military expansion, German army increases, Irish Home Rule, labor unrest, suffrage agitation, and continued Balkan anxiety. The chapter shows leaders managing ordinary politics while military and diplomatic timetables grew less forgiving. Source anchors: Poincare; Jaures; Grey; three-year service; Russian military expansion; Irish Home Rule.

Analysis: Poincare, Jaures, Grey, three-year service, Russian military expansion, and Irish Home Rule make the last months of peace feel crowded rather than calm. Poincare and the three-year service law reflect French concern about Germany and Russian readiness. Russian military expansion worries German planners who fear the future balance will worsen. Grey and Irish Home Rule show Britain absorbed by domestic crisis, which affects how clear British warnings can be. Jaures represents the antiwar left, but his presence also highlights how fragile socialist resistance proves when national crises arrive.

18. Assassination at Sarajevo

Summary: MacMillan reconstructs June 28, 1914, when Archduke Franz Ferdinand and Sophie visited Sarajevo, encountered failed conspirators, survived an initial bomb attack, and were then shot by Gavrilo Princip after a mistaken turn near the Latin Bridge. The chapter explains the Serbian nationalist world around Young Bosnia, the Black Hand, Apis or Dragutin Dimitrijevic, Bosnia-Herzegovina, Habsburg rule, Serbian officials, and the ambiguous connections between Belgrade and the assassins. Franz Ferdinand's reform possibilities, Sophie's presence, the symbolism of St. Vitus Day, and the incompetence of security all heighten the event's political charge. Source anchors: June 28; Franz Ferdinand; Sophie; Gavrilo Princip; Black Hand; Apis.

Analysis: June 28, Franz Ferdinand, Sophie, Gavrilo Princip, Black Hand, and Apis show how a contingent killing becomes a great-power crisis. Franz Ferdinand matters because his death removes a Habsburg figure associated with possible reform and with caution about war. Sophie matters because the murder is also personal and dynastic, not only diplomatic. Gavrilo Princip gives the act a visible agent, while Black Hand and Apis create the unresolved question of Serbian state responsibility. June 28 becomes the trigger Austria-Hungary can use, but the chapter keeps the gap clear between assassination and general war.

19. The End of the Concert of Europe: Austria-Hungary Declares War on Serbia

Summary: The July crisis begins as Austria-Hungary decides how to answer Sarajevo, seeks German backing, receives the blank check, drafts the ultimatum to Serbia, and waits until after Poincare's visit to Russia before acting. MacMillan follows Berchtold, Conrad, Tisza, Franz Joseph, Bethmann-Hollweg, Wilhelm II, Sazonov, Nicholas II, Poincare, Grey, and the ambassadors as they interpret timing, honor, deterrence, and risk. Serbia's reply accepts much but not all of the ultimatum; Austria-Hungary breaks relations and declares war on July 28, while Russia considers mobilization and Britain searches for mediation. Source anchors: blank check; Berchtold; Tisza; ultimatum; Serbia's reply; July 28.

Analysis: Blank check, Berchtold, Tisza, ultimatum, Serbia's reply, and July 28 mark the collapse of the Concert of Europe as a working habit. The blank check gives Vienna confidence that Germany will stand behind it, while Tisza's hesitation shows that even inside Austria-Hungary the risks are understood. The ultimatum is designed to be hard to accept, yet Serbia's reply is conciliatory enough to make Austria-Hungary's declaration on July 28 look like a choice for punishment. This chapter matters because mediation still exists as a language, but crisis governance fails when key governments refuse to let mediation slow the machinery they have set moving.

20. Turning Out the Lights: Europe's Last Week of Peace

Summary: The final prewar chapter tracks the last week as Austria-Hungary shells Belgrade, Russia moves from partial to general mobilization, Germany issues ultimatums, France prepares, Belgium receives German demands, and Britain decides after the violation of Belgian neutrality. MacMillan follows Nicholas II and Wilhelm II's telegrams, Moltke, Bethmann-Hollweg, Sazonov, Poincare, Joffre, Grey, Asquith, the British Cabinet, Jean Jaures's assassination, German mobilization crowds, and the declarations of war that culminate with Britain on August 4. The title echoes Grey's remark about lamps going out across Europe, but MacMillan's emphasis remains on identifiable steps rather than one sudden darkness. Source anchors: Belgrade; general mobilization; Belgian neutrality; Grey; August 4; Jaures.

Analysis: Belgrade, general mobilization, Belgian neutrality, Grey, August 4, and Jaures show diplomacy losing to sequence. Belgrade's shelling makes the crisis military, while general mobilization convinces Germany that its own plans must begin. Belgian neutrality gives Grey and the British Cabinet a public and legal ground for intervention that previous Entente understandings had not supplied. Jaures's assassination removes a major French antiwar voice at the worst moment. August 4 matters because mobilization throughput lets each government describe itself as responding to someone else's step even as every response closes the remaining exits.

Epilogue: The War

Summary: Europeans enter August 1914 expecting a short war; even financial experts assume disrupted trade and credit will force peace. Instead, governments tax, direct production, mobilize women, and extract enough men and matériel to continue. Belgian resistance, troops diverted east, and the British Expeditionary Force help stop Germany's advance, after which trenches and defensive fire turn the Western Front into mass attrition while the more mobile Eastern Front also fails to produce decision. Imperial subjects and new belligerents make the conflict global, and German submarine warfare brings the United States in. By 1918 Russia, Austria-Hungary, the Ottoman Empire, and Germany have lost their old regimes; Bolshevism, new national states, American and Japanese power, and colonial nationalism replace Europe's prewar dominance. MacMillan closes by returning responsibility to choices, naming failures of imagination and courage rather than treating the catastrophe as automatic. Source anchors: short-war assumption; Belgian resistance; wartime resource extraction; Western Front attrition; four fallen empires; American entry; failure of imagination.

Analysis: The short-war assumption failed because leaders underestimated their own states' capacity for wartime resource extraction. Economic interdependence did not stop combat; taxation, administrative control, women's labor, and mass compliance sustained Western Front attrition after the opening plans failed. That endurance destroyed the political objects the belligerents claimed to defend: four fallen empires, Bolshevism, and American entry remade the balance of power. MacMillan's final “failure of imagination” therefore answers the book's causal question. Structures made prolonged war possible, but officials still chose escalation without imagining what their institutions could do once mobilized or showing the courage to resist claims that no alternative remained.
