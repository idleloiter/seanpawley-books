Maisky Diaries

Place in the vault

This note belongs with War, Revolution, and Civilizational Breakdown because Ivan Maisky's diary records how a revolutionary state tried to survive the collapse of European order while its own diplomatic corps lived under Stalin's suspicion. It is strongest when read as a record of alliance-making under mistrust: Maisky courts Churchill, Eden, Vansittart, Beaverbrook, Lloyd George, the Webbs, and the British press while knowing that Moscow may treat those contacts as evidence against him.

Detailed overview

Gabriel Gorodetsky presents Ivan Mikhailovich Maisky's London diary as an unusually intimate record of Soviet diplomacy because Soviet officials were normally discouraged from writing private diaries at all. The book begins with the afterlife of the document: Maisky was arrested in February 1953, his papers were seized by the Ministry for State Security, and only after Stalin's death and his 1955 pardon did he try, unsuccessfully, to regain full control of the diary. Gorodetsky's discovery of the 1941 volume in 1993, during work on Israeli-Soviet documents, makes the edition a recovered archive as much as a diplomatic memoir.

The diary's narrative logic follows Maisky's effort to make himself indispensable in London while remaining legible to Moscow. The biographical opening explains why he could work this way: he was Ivan Lyakhovetsky from Kirillov, a Menshevik exile shaped by Fabianism, London emigre circles, Georgy Chicherin, Maksim Litvinov, the Webbs, and his own rehabilitation after serving in Komuch during the Civil War. Those experiences made him unusually fluent in British political society, but they also left him permanently vulnerable to accusations about Menshevik origins, bourgeois sociability, and excessive independence.

The middle of the book uses the annual diary entries to show the slow failure of collective security. In 1934 and 1935, Maisky and Robert Vansittart try to make the League of Nations, the Eastern Pact, and Anglo-Soviet rapprochement into tools against Hitler, while John Simon, Neville Chamberlain, and British class hostility repeatedly narrow what can be done. The Metro-Vickers dispute, Soviet entry into the League, the Kirov assassination, Abyssinia, and Spain are not side episodes; they are the cases through which Maisky tests whether Britain can move from anti-Bolshevik reflex to strategic cooperation.

Munich and 1939 are the central reversal. Maisky watches Chamberlain, Halifax, Horace Wilson, and the Cliveden circle treat Czechoslovakia as a price for buying time or avoiding war, while Litvinov's collective-security language loses authority in Moscow. The failed triple negotiations, the April Kremlin meeting with Stalin and Molotov, Litvinov's dismissal, Strang's Moscow mission, and the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact turn Maisky from advocate of an Anglo-Soviet bloc into a distrusted observer of a policy he cannot openly oppose.

The wartime entries give the book its most dramatic evidence. In 1940, the Blue Book/White Book dispute, Finland, and the fall of France leave Anglo-Soviet relations near rupture, yet Churchill's government and Cripps's mission keep channels open. In June 1941, Maisky resists British warnings about Barbarossa until Novikov's telephone call, Molotov's speech, and Eden's meeting force a new reality. The diary then traces a fragile alliance built around Churchill's 22 June broadcast, the 12 July agreement, Golikov's military mission, the BBC's refusal to play the Internationale, Stalin's letters, supply promises, Iran, and the repeated demand for a second front.

The final wartime years show Maisky's usefulness becoming dangerous. In 1942 he presses Eden, Cripps, Beaverbrook, Harriman, and Churchill over supplies, the 1941 Soviet borders, the Baltic question, Roosevelt's role, Operation Torch, PQ 17, and the second front, but Stalin increasingly reads British delay as deliberate bleeding of the Red Army. In 1943, after Stalingrad, Casablanca, Sicily, Italy, the Comintern's dissolution, Katyn, and renewed postponement of the cross-Channel attack, Maisky is recalled. The closing chapters make the recall part of a larger transformation of Soviet diplomacy: Litvinov and Maisky are replaced by more obedient figures, Narkomindel is militarized with uniforms and rank, and Maisky's London celebrity becomes incriminating material in the Beria-era case that ends with his 1955 conviction for abuse of power rather than treason.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Introduction

Summary: Gorodetsky introduces Ivan Mikhailovich Maisky as the Soviet ambassador in London from 1932 to 1943 and as the keeper of a diary that should not have survived Stalin's political world. The introduction recounts Maisky's February 1953 arrest, the seizure of the papers by the Ministry for State Security, his 1955 pardon, and his failed attempt to regain access to the diaries. It also explains Gorodetsky's 1993 discovery of the 1941 volume while working on Israeli-Soviet documents, the Yale University Press full edition, the abridged version, and the comparison of the diary with telegrams, private papers, memoirs, and photographs from Alexei D. Voskressenski. The section names Churchill, Eden, Chamberlain, Halifax, Litvinov, Molotov, Lloyd George, Nancy Astor, the Webbs, Bernard Shaw, H.G. Wells, and Joe Kennedy as part of the British and international world that passed through Maisky's rooms. Source anchors: Maisky, Ministry for State Security, 1941 volume, Yale University Press, Churchill, Great Terror.

Analysis: The introduction establishes why Maisky's diary is not just another memoir of Churchill or appeasement. Maisky writes inside Stalin's diplomatic system while the Ministry for State Security later turns the paper trail against him. The 1941 volume performs Knowledge Preservation by retaining the shock of Barbarossa and early alliance contacts before Soviet memoir discipline flattens them. Gorodetsky and Yale reconstruct that record by checking private impressions against Churchill, Eden, telegrams, and silences created by the Great Terror.

The Making of a Soviet Diplomat

Summary: The biographical chapter traces Maisky from Ivan Lyakhovetsky's birth in Kirillov in 1884 through revolutionary activism, exile, Menshevik politics, and eventual entry into Soviet diplomacy. It gives his family roots near Nizhny-Novgorod, his Populist sympathies, his arrest for agitation, exile to Tobolsk, and the Fabian influence of the Webbs' History of Trade Unionism. It follows him through Switzerland, Munich, the German Social Democratic Party, London emigre networks around Chicherin and Litvinov, service in Kerensky's Ministry of Labour, the Komuch government in Samara, flight from Kolchak to Mongolia, rehabilitation through Lunacharsky, and assignments in Narkomindel, London, Tokyo, and Helsinki. The chapter ends with Litvinov's 1932 decision, backed by Stalin's experiment, to send him to London. Source anchors: Kirillov, Tobolsk, Webbs, Komuch, Narkomindel, Helsinki.

Analysis: Kirillov and Tobolsk give Maisky a Russian revolutionary biography, but the Webbs and London emigre circles supply Elite Formation outside the Bolshevik party by teaching him British political language, networks, and manners. Komuch makes that cosmopolitan competence suspect even after Narkomindel needs it. Helsinki becomes a calculated Litvinov-Stalin appointment: the state promotes a diplomat whose access to British elites creates value abroad and vulnerability at home.

Prologue

Summary: The prologue records Maisky's surprise at learning in Helsingfors that he would replace Grigory Sokolnikov as ambassador to Britain. He had read speculation in the Manchester Guardian, then received Litvinov's 3 September notice appointing him, and soon had to move with Agniya into a London life shaped by embassy routine, court protocol, and uncertainty about how long he would remain. The section looks back from 27 October 1937 on five years in the post, naming Sokolnikov, Litvinov, Agniya, Stalin's appointment logic, the arrival through British immigration, and the formal presentation at St James's Palace. Source anchors: Helsingfors, Sokolnikov, Litvinov, 3 September, Agniya, St James's Palace.

Analysis: Helsingfors and the Manchester Guardian make the appointment feel accidental from Maisky's own vantage point, while Litvinov and 3 September show that it is a deliberate personnel move by Soviet diplomacy. Sokolnikov is a warning because his later arrest and murder mark the risk attached to high office abroad. Agniya and St James's Palace pull the diary into domestic and ceremonial detail, showing how the Soviet ambassador's household becomes a working instrument inside Britain's monarchy and political society.

1934

Summary: The 1934 entries begin with Anglo-Soviet relations damaged by Metro-Vickers, the trade conflict, and British suspicion, then show Hitler's Germany making rapprochement more urgent. Maisky records the new trade agreement, Soviet entry into the League of Nations, Litvinov's collective-security line, the Eastern Pact, and long conversations with Robert Vansittart about Germany, Poland, France, and British caution. The year also includes Maisky's cultivation of editors such as Garvin, the Guildhall dinner where Lady Kynaston Studd denounces Soviet bloodshed, the Prince of Wales recognizing him at court, the Duke of Kent's wedding to Princess Marina, and the shock of Kirov's assassination. Source anchors: Metro-Vickers, Eastern Pact, Vansittart, League of Nations, Kirov, Prince of Wales.

Analysis: Metro-Vickers supplies the damaged starting point from which Maisky must rebuild access, and the League of Nations offers a public stage for Soviet respectability. The Eastern Pact and Vansittart show Informal Institutions carrying diplomacy through personal trust, dinners, court access, and private warnings before governments accept a binding strategy. The Prince of Wales and Guildhall scenes make social recognition operational, while Kirov forces Maisky to defend Stalinist executions without losing the British relationships needed for anti-Hitler cooperation.

1935

Summary: The 1935 entries mix embassy domesticity with intensive campaigning for collective security. Maisky opens with an embassy fire near his study fireplace, hosts Mikhail Sholokhov and his wife during their London stay, speaks at Cambridge about Soviet planning, and lectures the League of Nations Union at the London School of Economics before roughly six hundred listeners. He tracks MacDonald and Simon as men still inclined toward Hitler, measures Baldwin and Eden as more useful, presses the Eastern Pact and mutual assistance, accompanies the planning for Eden's Moscow visit, and reacts to Hitler's March announcement of compulsory military service and a 500,000-man army. Source anchors: embassy fire, Sholokhov, Cambridge, League of Nations Union, Simon, Eden.

Analysis: The embassy fire and Sholokhov episodes keep Maisky's diary from becoming a bare diplomatic calendar, but Cambridge and the League of Nations Union show his deliberate use of public opinion. Simon is the obstacle because he represents a British policy that still tries to manage Hitler, while Eden becomes the possible channel to Moscow and Stalin. The chapter matters because Maisky tests whether speeches, intellectual respectability, and Eden's visit can turn collective security from Litvinov's slogan into British policy.

1936

Summary: The 1936 entries begin with Wickham Steed's observations on George V and court speechmaking, then move through George V's death, Edward VIII's court, and a protocol fight over whether the Soviet trade representative Mosina could attend in the proper place. Maisky reads Mussolini's conquest of Abyssinia and the Negus's flight as a disaster for the League, watches Baldwin and Churchill through conversations with British insiders, and then turns to Spain after the July military revolt. The Non-Intervention Committee, Soviet assistance, Litvinov's troubles, Maisky's summons to Moscow, and Lord Lothian's claim that Spain is the first duel between the USSR and Germany-Italy shape the year. Source anchors: Wickham Steed, Edward VIII, Mosina, Abyssinia, Non-Intervention Committee, Lord Lothian.

Analysis: Wickham Steed and Edward VIII place Maisky inside Britain's constitutional theater just as European order is breaking outside it. Mosina's seating problem matters because Soviet status is fought through protocol as well as treaties. Abyssinia demonstrates that the League cannot restrain fascist conquest, and the Non-Intervention Committee turns Spain into a test where Lord Lothian can already imagine Britain drifting toward whichever side appears likely to win.

1937

Summary: The 1937 entries measure whether Eden's speeches and British handling of Spain mean a real change toward resistance. Maisky receives the Japanese ambassador Shigeru Yoshida, presents credentials to George VI while Ribbentrop gives a Nazi salute, notices Elizabeth and Margaret, and watches the Soviet battleship Marat impress British observers at Spithead. He hears Vansittart warn about Henderson's Germanophile appointment, listens to Titulescu at the Ritz warn of Mitteleuropa, discusses Chamberlain's succession to Baldwin, and records Chamberlain's visit and the appeal of a four-power pact. Source anchors: Eden speeches, Shigeru Yoshida, George VI, Marat, Titulescu, Chamberlain.

Analysis: Eden speeches are important because Maisky is looking for signs that Britain has stopped retreating before fascist pressure, while Shigeru Yoshida widens the danger beyond Europe to Japan. George VI and Marat show symbolic Soviet acceptance in Britain, but Titulescu's warnings and Chamberlain's rise expose how fragile that acceptance is. Chamberlain's interest in a four-power pact threatens to bypass the USSR, making Maisky's court access and naval display insufficient substitutes for binding strategy.

1938

Summary: The 1938 entries begin with Vansittart's removal from the permanent undersecretaryship and then follow the collapse of the anti-appeasement position. Maisky records Hitler's army purge, Ribbentrop's rise, Eden's resignation over Chamberlain's Italian policy, Halifax's arrival at the Foreign Office, Austria's annexation, the third Moscow trial, Joe Kennedy's reports that Britain sees the Red Army as crippled by purges, and Churchill's interest in a grand alliance. The Sudeten crisis brings Litvinov's League speech, Chamberlain's Berchtesgaden trip, Halifax's errand-boy exchange with Masaryk, the Munich agreement, Masaryk's despair, and Churchill's reports about Cliveden Set rumors over Soviet aviation. Source anchors: Vansittart, Eden resignation, Halifax, Munich, Masaryk, Cliveden Set.

Analysis: Vansittart's removal and Eden's resignation show anti-appeasement officials losing the internal contest, leaving Halifax to administer Munich. Masaryk gives the Czechoslovak cost a human voice, while the Cliveden Set turns Language and Ideology into strategy by circulating claims of Soviet military weakness and Bolshevik unreliability. Those elite narratives make exclusion of Moscow appear prudent before formal negotiations test whether Soviet cooperation is possible.

1939

Summary: The 1939 entries open with Maisky in Paris but quickly move into the last failed attempt at Anglo-Soviet alignment before war. Chamberlain's March reception at the Soviet embassy brings thirteen Cabinet members and a British prime minister across the Soviet threshold, while Hudson's Moscow trip probes whether relations can be repaired after Munich. Germany's seizure of Prague, the Poland guarantee, the proposed six-power conference on Rumania, Lloyd George's warning that no second front is possible without the USSR, and the April Soviet proposal for a Britain-France-USSR pact all frame the spring. Maisky is then called to Moscow for the April Kremlin meeting with Stalin, Molotov, Mikoyan, Kaganovich, Voroshilov, Litvinov, and Potemkin; Litvinov is dismissed, Strang goes to Moscow, and the year ends in the logic that produces the Molotov-Ribbentrop Pact. Source anchors: Chamberlain reception, Hudson, Poland guarantee, six-power conference, Litvinov, Strang.

Analysis: Chamberlain's reception is theater meant to repair Munich's humiliation, but Hudson and the Poland guarantee show Britain seeking Soviet help without an equal pact. The six-power conference exposes a failure of Information and Coordination because London, Paris, Warsaw, and Moscow cannot align commitments, military access, and threat assessments before Germany acts. Litvinov's dismissal removes the chief broker of collective security, while Strang's slow mission reinforces the mistrust driving Stalin and Molotov toward Berlin.

1940

Summary: The 1940 entries begin with the Blue Book or White Book dispute over the failed 1939 Moscow negotiations and the danger that publication would break relations. Maisky watches Finland, Daladier's pressure, Seeds's departure from Moscow, Strang's offer of proofs, and British claims that Molotov sabotaged negotiations while Maisky misled London. He then records the fall of Chamberlain, the Churchill Government, Cripps's Moscow appointment, Dunkirk, Belgium's collapse, Paris has fallen, Churchill's speeches, Keynes's gloom, Kennedy's pessimism, Mers-el-Kebir, and the December incendiary raid in which the City burned while the Bank and Stock Exchange survived. Source anchors: Blue Book, Finland, Churchill Government, Cripps, Dunkirk, Paris has fallen, City burned.

Analysis: The Blue Book threatens to convert failed 1939 diplomacy into a public indictment of Soviet motives, while Finland supplies a current military grievance. The Churchill government and Cripps practice Crisis Governance by preserving a channel to Moscow even when formal relations remain hostile. Dunkirk, the fall of Paris, and the burned City change Britain's strategic dependence, making continued contact with Maisky a hedge against isolation and a possible German-Soviet realignment.

1941

Summary: The 1941 entries begin with Blitz danger at Kensington Palace Gardens, Weizmann's visit about Palestine oranges and Soviet furs, Balkan anxieties over Yugoslavia and Bulgaria, and British rumors after Hess's flight. Maisky hears Eden, Cripps, and British intelligence warnings about German forces, including Cripps's claim of 147 divisions, yet on 21 June he is still asking whether Hitler will really attack. On 22 June Novikov calls with the news of Barbarossa; Maisky listens to Molotov, meets Eden with the white rag on his hand, welcomes Churchill's broadcast, then follows the July alliance agreement, General Golikov's mission, the BBC dispute over the Internationale, Stalin's message to Churchill, the Chequers meeting, Iran, Beaverbrook's supply promises, Moscow's danger under Kluge and Guderian, Zhukov's counterstroke, and Eden's December trip to Moscow after Pearl Harbor. Source anchors: Kensington Palace Gardens, Weizmann, Hess, 147 divisions, Barbarossa, Internationale, Zhukov.

Analysis: Kensington Palace Gardens ties the Soviet embassy to London's physical war, while Hess and 147 divisions expose fears that Britain might reach terms or Moscow might misread Hitler. Barbarossa converts the relationship into War, Crisis, and Collapse on June 22, forcing an alliance Maisky had predicted but failed to secure in 1939. The Internationale dispute shows ideology surviving the treaty, while Zhukov's counterstroke changes battlefield survival into diplomatic leverage before Eden reaches Moscow.

1942

Summary: The 1942 section is thinner as a diary because of malaria, work, and Maisky's unease over Kremlin policy, but the entries and commentary show him pressing the alliance at its most difficult points. He reports Harriman's idea of a Roosevelt-Stalin meeting at Iceland, the Bering Strait, Arkhangelsk, or Astrakhan; watches the Red Army's prestige rise after Moscow; reads Churchill's weakened position after Singapore; and weighs Eden, Cripps, Beaverbrook, Litvinov, Halifax, and Molotov as rival channels. The year centers on supplies, the second front, the 1941 borders, the Baltic question, Roosevelt's consultation, the treaty, Tobruk, PQ 17, Churchill's August Moscow visit, Operation Torch, El Alamein, and Stalin's suspicion that Churchill wants the USSR bled white. Source anchors: Harriman, Singapore, Cripps, 1941 borders, PQ 17, Operation Torch, El Alamein.

Analysis: Harriman gives Maisky a route toward Roosevelt, but the 1941 borders and Baltic question let postwar claims obstruct immediate cooperation. PQ 17, convoy losses, and second-front delay make Logistics and Throughput the alliance's test: ships, tanks, aircraft, Arctic routes, and promised divisions determine whether political solidarity has material content. Operation Torch and El Alamein strengthen Britain but redirect resources from the cross-Channel attack Stalin demands, leaving Maisky to defend Churchill without delivering throughput Moscow considers decisive.

1943

Summary: The 1943 entries open with a changed mood after Stalingrad, the Don Bend, Mozdok, Velikie Luki, and the lifting of the Leningrad blockade. Maisky predicts the European war will not end before 1944 unless a real front is opened in 1943, worries about Roosevelt and American expansion, and talks with Eden about Casablanca, Sicily, Italy, and Churchill's effort to bring Turkey in through Inonu. He records Stalingrad's liquidation, Roosevelt's proposal for aircraft in the Far East, Lloyd George's eightieth birthday, German peace feelers, the Comintern dissolution, Bevin's question about the CPSU, Butler and Morrison on postwar Britain, Sidney Webb after Beatrice's death, Bomber Harris's faith in air war, Husky, Italy, and finally his July flight route through Gibraltar, Cairo, Habbaniya, Kuibyshev, and Moscow after recall. Source anchors: Stalingrad, Leningrad blockade, Casablanca, Comintern, Bevin, Husky, Gibraltar.

Analysis: Stalingrad and the Leningrad blockade give Maisky confidence that the USSR will survive, but Casablanca shows the Western Allies choosing Mediterranean operations over the French front he wants. Comintern dissolution is important because it removes a propaganda weapon against Moscow and helps Maisky argue that Stalin seeks security rather than world revolution. Bevin's question about the CPSU reveals how British Labour misreads that move, and Husky plus Gibraltar mark the route by which Mediterranean strategy and Maisky's recall converge.

End of an Era: Maisky's Recall

Summary: The recall chapter argues that Maisky and Litvinov publicly presented their return to Moscow as a protest over the postponed second front, while the deeper issue was Stalin and Molotov's effort to end the old diplomats' independent power abroad. Maisky briefs the British press about his supposedly elevated deputy-foreign-minister status; The Times says Stalin wants him near Molotov, while Time predicts he will disappear into the Narkomindel maze. The chapter includes Ivy Litvinov's fears, Gromyko's later criticism of Maisky's Menshevik past and long telegrams, Maisky's political will to Agniya, Kerr's failed effort to keep him in London, Molotov's agrement request for Gusev, the new diplomatic uniforms with marshal shoulder straps and a dagger, and the anxiety caused by honors, farewell letters, Epstein's bust, and Kokoschka's portrait. Source anchors: Litvinov, The Times, Narkomindel, Agniya, Gusev, Kokoschka, diplomatic uniforms.

Analysis: Litvinov and Maisky represent a diplomacy based on personal Western networks that Stalin can use but not trust. Narkomindel answers through Path Dependence and Institutional Drift: Gusev, military-style ranks, and diplomatic uniforms redirect the foreign service from cosmopolitan brokerage toward obedience without abolishing the institution. Agniya's political will, the Kokoschka portrait, The Times, and Kerr make Maisky's London fame evidence that his personal authority has outgrown the role Moscow now wants.

The Price of Fame: A Late Repression

Summary: The final chapter follows Maisky after London, when Narkomindel marginalization, the anti-cosmopolitan campaign, and the Cold War turn his British friendships into danger. Yalta is his last international display, with Churchill, Eden, Roosevelt, and Stalin seeing him near the center, but he is later caught in the Doctors' Plot climate, arrested in 1953, connected to Beria's schemes for foreign contacts, rearrested after Beria's fall, and charged under Article 58 before the case narrows. The indictment uses his diary, Churchill and Eden intimacy, the 1939 White Book microfilm, embassy subordinates, Deborin's accusations, and Article 109 on abuse of power; in 1955 he is convicted, pardoned, released from Butyrka, later rehabilitated in 1960, and spends his final years with Agniya writing memoirs until his death in 1975. Source anchors: Yalta, Doctors' Plot, Beria, Article 58, White Book, Butyrka, 1960.

Analysis: Yalta gives Maisky one final image of usefulness before the Soviet system reclassifies Anglo-American access as incriminating. The Doctors' Plot and Beria turn Legibility into retrospective criminalization: contacts once rewarded as diplomacy become signs of disloyalty when Stalin's last purge needs suspects. Article 58 supplies the treason category, while the White Book and Article 109 provide a lesser administrative charge when evidence fails; Butyrka punishes the same relationships that made the diary historically valuable.
