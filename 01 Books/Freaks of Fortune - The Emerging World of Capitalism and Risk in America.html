Freaks of Fortune - The Emerging World of Capitalism and Risk in America

Buy on Amazon — Freaks of Fortune - The Emerging World of Capitalism and Risk in America

Position in the vault

A standalone work of economic, legal, and cultural history — the vault's anchor for the financialization of everyday life and for the historical invention of risk. It belongs to the Finance, Firms, and Industrialization MoC and to the Finance, Allocation, and Industrial Power reading path, where it supplies the nineteenth-century setting that books about later finance build on. Per 06 Plans/book-affinity.md (Finance & industrial capitalism cluster), its kindred siblings are The Rise and Fall of American Growth (the growth epoch this book's risk institutions helped finance), Lords of Finance - The Bankers Who Broke the World (the interwar central-banker sequel to Perkins's Morgan-capital world), and The Machinery of Freedom (the voluntary-order counterpart to Levy's fraternal self-help risk communities). It also sits beside the early-modern money-and-credit books in the same cluster (Prometheus Shackled, The Dutch Republic) as the American side of the financial-revolution story, and complements the state-power cluster: it is, in effect, a history of how the legibility and security functions Scott describes arrived through private insurance before migrating to the federal welfare state.

Detailed overview

Jonathan Levy's Freaks of Fortune (Harvard University Press, 2012) reconstructs nineteenth-century American capitalism by following the word and practice of "risk" from its maritime birthplace into wage labor, slavery, life insurance, Reconstruction banking, farm mortgages, fraternal orders, futures markets, and industrial trusts. The book opens with voyage because "risk" first named the commodity bought and sold in marine insurance contracts — a financial instrument for coping with the contingency of long-distance seaborne trade, not an inner feeling of danger. Its roots pass through French risque, Italian rischio, and a likely Arabic candidate, all in commercial maritime contexts; the Oxford English Dictionary emphasizes the "possibility of damage to merchandise when transported by sea." Levy shows how a term once confined to the edges of Atlantic trade exploded into the ordinary grammar of American life, so that by Whitman's 1871 "Passage to India" a poet could invest "risk" with lyric power, and by the end of the century "risk was in fact everywhere."

The book's central move is to insist that risk was not discovered by modern finance; it was made — by law, by corporations, by actuarial tables, by banks, exchanges, and political institutions. Lemuel Shaw's 1842 decision in Farwell v. Boston and Worcester Railroad moved marine-insurance reasoning inland into the railroad workplace, holding that the engine-man Nicholas Farwell had personally "assumed" the perils of his employment through free contract, his two-dollar-per-day wage containing a "premium for the risk" he bore. The Creole insurance litigation did something more brutal: it exposed how masters owned the "risks" on enslaved bodies, and how a successful slave revolt was described in law as the enslaved repossessing the risk on their own lives. Levy frames these two cases as the poles of a moral revolution: a free man owned his own downside and upside, while a slave's destiny was a master's insurable property.

Levy's treatment of freedom is deliberately double-edged. To be free in nineteenth-century liberal thought was to own oneself and therefore to bear one's own risk. But that freedom immediately required a new form of dependence on financial corporations — accident insurers, life insurers, savings banks, mortgage brokers, fraternal benefit orders, futures exchanges, and eventually corporate welfare programs. The free actor was instructed to manage the future by buying a policy, opening an account, taking a mortgage, joining an order, hedging a crop, or accepting a benefit plan. Rural hierarchies of master, husband, and father died out as security migrated into institutions characterized by abstract, statistical, contract-based risk communities. Here Levy recruits Polanyi's "double movement": alongside the expansion of markets ran a countermovement that commodified peril itself into financial risk, and risk management became "a candidate in the broader countermovement against the perils of capitalism." Yet corporate risk management time and again manufactured new insecurity — the "freaks of fortune," the sudden, unforeseeable, amoral turns of wealth that eluded every attempt at mastery.

The book repeatedly returns to contested or failed forms of security. Elizur Wright, the abolitionist turned America's first professional actuary, tried to make life insurance compatible with antislavery self-ownership: surrender values and Massachusetts regulation were his weapons against the London death-speculation "auction blocks" that he compared to slave auctions, and his campaign culminated in the 1876 Supreme Court decision New York Life Insurance Company v. Statham. The Freedman's Bank promised four million freedpeople a safe home for their wages, then channeled nearly $50 million of deposits through Henry Cooke and Jay Cooke & Co. into Northern Pacific railroad bonds until the panic of 1873 destroyed black savings in an opaque circuit of white trusteeship. Western farmers sought landed independence but were pulled into mortgage-backed securities, crop and weather forecasting, the deflationary gold standard, and finally futures markets, discovering that the western mortgage could follow them into their homes and work them "harder than any of them." Fraternal orders built brotherly risk communities against corporate life insurance, only to be forced into actuarial discipline, reserves, and contract by courts like the Iowa ruling in State v. Miller. Futures contracts promised wheat farmers hedging against price volatility, but courts, exchanges, bucket shops, and the doctrine of "contemplating delivery" struggled to separate productive speculation from gambling until Holmes's 1905 Board of Trade v. Christie — "the self-adjustment of society to the probable" — destroyed the bucket shops and stamped the pits legitimate.

The last chapters move the story toward the trust question. George W. Perkins — vice president of the New York Life, then partner at J. P. Morgan & Co., organizer of U.S. Steel and International Harvester — believed giant industrial combinations could stabilize destructive competition, rationalize markets, and turn the corporation into a social risk community through profit-sharing, pensions, and employee benefits. Perkins's conflicted career connects the insurance and finance history to Theodore Roosevelt, the Bureau of Corporations, the National Civic Federation, the Hepburn Bill, and the Progressive search for federal supervision; the 1907 panic, which Morgan and Perkins settled with the TC&I purchase and the locked-door $25 million levy on 120 trust and bank heads, showed the corporation acting as a private central authority that would soon be displaced by the Federal Reserve. The epilogue carries the story into the twentieth century: the New Deal — Glass-Steagall, the SEC, mortgage insurance, and above all the 1935 Social Security Act — inherited rather than rejected the nineteenth-century puzzle of how to secure free people inside a capitalism that produces the insecurities it asks them to manage. Levy's "freaks of fortune" are not accidents outside capitalism; they are the booms, busts, panics, lawsuits, revolts, failed banks, and speculative devices through which Americans learned to imagine the future as something personally owned, financially managed, and never finally mastered.

Major people, societies, and motivations

  • Nicholas Farwell: an engine-man on the Boston and Worcester Railroad whose right hand was crushed in 1836 when a fellow employee mislaid a switch. He valued the hand at $10,000 and lost when Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw ruled in 1842 that he had "assumed" the risks and perils of railroad work as a free man; his story grounds the book's argument that the law enclosed accident risk as private property.
  • Lemuel Shaw: Chief Justice of the Massachusetts Supreme Court and author of Farwell (1842), which dispensed with respondeat superior and invited marine-insurance reasoning inland. He re-appears in Loomis v. Eagle Life and Health Insurance Company (1856), holding that "every man has an interest in his own life to any amount." He embodies the jurist as the maker of risk's working rules.
  • Madison Washington: the Virginia slave who led the November 1841 revolt aboard the brig Creole and steered 135 enslaved people toward freedom in the British Bahamas. He is Levy's proof that "to be free was to own that personal risk yourself," and the hero of Frederick Douglass's novella The Heroic Slave.
  • Judah P. Benjamin: the New Orleans lawyer who defended the marine underwriters in Thomas McCargo v. The New Orleans Insurance Company by arguing that a slave's "ever wakeful and ever active longing after liberty" was an "inherent vice" — and therefore an uninsurable "act of man." He later served the Confederacy as attorney general and secretary of state.
  • Henry Adams Bullard: the Louisiana Supreme Court justice who nonetheless ruled that the insurrection, not the British seizure, was the "proximate cause" of the owners' loss — agreeing with Benjamin that the nineteen rebels had repossessed their own risks. He then co-authored the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 with Benjamin.
  • Elizur Wright: the abolitionist, actuary, and first insurance commissioner of Massachusetts. Repulsed by the London life-insurance auction blocks, he built an "actuarial science of freedom" — surrender values, the 1858 valuation registry, the 1861 Massachusetts surrender-value law, and the doctrine enshrined in Statham (1876) — to keep free "life risks" from behaving like slave property. He died a fervent atheist.
  • John W. Alvord: the ordained Congregationalist minister, Freedmen's Bureau inspector, and founder then president of the Freedman's Bank, who told freedpeople to "work and save and buy for ourselves" after land redistribution failed.
  • Rufus Saxton: the Union general who administered Sherman's reserve on the Sea Islands, advocated land redistribution, founded the South Carolina Freedmen's Savings Bank, and lectured the freedpeople to develop "an eye to the future."
  • Henry Cooke (and Jay Cooke): the investment-banking brothers who marketed the Union war debt; Henry ran the Freedman's Bank's finance committee from 1867, plundering its deposits into Northern Pacific bonds and speculative loans until Jay Cooke & Co.'s failure on September 18, 1873 triggered the panic. They embody "risk management" as the capture of other people's money.
  • Frederick Douglass: the ex-slave and abolitionist who became president of the Freedman's Bank in its final months in 1874, loaned it his own money, and saw the bank's "golden dreams" become an abstract, unmasterable financial power.
  • Edward Atkinson: the Boston textile industrialist and Freedman's Bank trustee who preferred wages, cotton monoculture, and savings accounts to black landed independence — and who by 1894, as a Republican gold bug and president of the Boston Insurance Company, read the farm-mortgage census as proof of western "independence."
  • Henry and Rosie Ise (and John Ise): German immigrant homesteaders on 160 acres of western Kansas whose 1887 mortgage of $363 to pay a polio doctor became the "relentless master of the family destinies"; their story carries the "betting the farm" chapter. Rosie's verdict on plains farming — "Nobody's responsible" — is the book's most quoted formulation of the impersonal economic chance-world.
  • John Upchurch: the former Erie Railroad master mechanic who founded the Ancient Order of United Workmen in Meadville, Pennsylvania, in 1868 — the flagship fraternal answer to corporate life insurance, built on $1 assessments and $2,000 certificates.
  • Samuel Gompers: the cigar maker, early Ancient Order of Foresters member, and AFL leader who carried the fraternal assessment system into national trade unionism and argued that an ideal union was a "life and health assurance company."
  • Andrew J. Sawyer: the Minneapolis grain elevator king and futures trader who testified before the House Committee on Agriculture in 1892 that he used the Chicago Board of Trade to "insure" his physical wheat against price swings — while his member of the board could just as easily turn speculator.
  • Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.: the Supreme Court justice whose Board of Trade v. Christie (1905) declared "set-off has all the effects of delivery," called hedging "a serious business contract for a legitimate and useful purpose," and stamped futures speculation by "competent men" as "the self-adjustment of society to the probable."
  • William James, Chauncey Wright, and Charles Sanders Peirce: the Harvard circle whose pragmatism and "radical uncertainty" supplied the philosophical air Holmes breathed; James's "The Will to Believe" and his credit-system metaphor of truth ("We trade on each other's truth") are Levy's lens on the metaphysics of trading thoughts as things.
  • George W. Perkins Sr.: vice president and one-man finance committee of the New York Life, then J. P. Morgan & Co. partner, organizer of U.S. Steel and International Harvester, architect of profit-sharing and the Employee Benefit Association, and campaign manager for Theodore Roosevelt's 1912 Progressive Party bid. He is the book's hero-villain of corporate risk management and "socialism of the highest form."
  • J. P. Morgan: the investment banker who "Morganized" the railroads, floated U.S. Steel's $500 million stock issue, and personally settled the panic of 1907 — the last private individual in the book able to master an economy.
  • Theodore Roosevelt: trust prosecutor (Northern Securities, 1904), friend and correspondent of Perkins, creator of the Bureau of Corporations, and 1912 Progressive candidate — the president through whom the trust question became a political question.
  • John D. Rockefeller Sr.: builder of the 1882 Standard Oil trust, who cast the corporation late in life as an "angel of mercy" saying "Get into the ark. We'll take all the risks!" — the frankest statement of the corporate assumption of risk.
  • William Graham Sumner: the Yale author of What Social Classes Owe to Each Other (1883), whose laissez-faire gospel — "the negroes, once slaves... now work and hold their own products, and are assured of nothing but what they earn" — is the individualist creed the fraternals rose against and Perkins rejected.
  • Societies and institutions: the merchant "risk communities" of Atlantic marine insurance (from Joseph Balch's brokerage journal to Lloyd's revolutionary successor in the 1779 common policy clause read verbatim into the Creole policy); the Freedman's Bank (April 1865–July 1874); the fraternal orders (the AOUW, Knights of Pythias, Ancient Order of Foresters, Knights of Honor, Colored Knights of Pythias); the Big Three Wall Street life insurers (Mutual Life, New York Life, Equitable) and their tontine funds; the Prudential and Metropolitan "industrial insurance" firms; the Chicago Board of Trade and its bucket-shop rivals; and the First National Bank, National City Bank, and trust companies that turned savings into railroad and steel capital.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Risk as a historical invention, not a timeless fact: risk began as the commodity in a marine insurance contract; "freaks of fortune" was the period's own term for the amoral turns of the economic chance-world.
  • The double freedom: liberal self-ownership (freedom from hierarchy) arrived bound to a new dependence on corporate financial institutions (freedom to call one's upside one's own).
  • Countermovements against capitalism's insecurities: landed independence, slave mastery, fraternalism, corporate welfare capitalism, and finally the state as risk manager of last resort — Polanyi's double movement applied to peril itself.
  • Risk management manufactures uncertainty: every hedge breeds new speculation; the towers of security are built out of the very chance they promise to tame.
  • The law as the working rule of risk: Farwell, the Creole litigation, Loomis, Statham, State v. Miller, Barnard, Irwin v. Williar, and Christie set the boundary markers of who owns downside and upside.
  • From providence to probability to pragmatism: providentialism gives way to the "law of mortality," and probability's "certainty" yields to Holmes's and James's "radical uncertainty."
  • Race and risk: slave property, self-ownership as antislavery doctrine, the Freedman's Bank, and sharecropping all test who may own a personal risk.
  • The politicization of risk: Populism, the trust question, Progressivism, and the New Deal each moved risk from the individual to society and the state.

Core concepts

  • Financial Infrastructure: the book is a genealogy of the modern American financial system — marine insurance, accident and life policies, savings banks, mortgage-backed securities, fraternal assessment funds, futures exchanges, and corporate benefit plans — showing how each made risk portable while creating new dependencies.
  • Commercial Society: Levy's America is a commercial society from the earliest colonies; the book shows commerce teaching men that "productive risk-taking was the rightful activity of a free man" while straining to separate it from gambling.
  • State Capacity: the state appears as charter-giver, regulator (Wright's Massachusetts), anti-bucket-shop legislator, and finally, in the New Deal, the insurer of last resort — absorbing functions private risk communities could not hold.
  • Capital Allocation: life-insurance reserves, savings-bank deposits, and freedpeople's wages flowed west as mortgage and railroad capital; the Freedman's Bank and the western mortgage market are allocation experiments with catastrophic failures.
  • Path Dependence and Institutional Drift: marine-insurance reasoning migrates into railroad employment law (Farwell), the "perils of the seas" checklist re-emerges as the "contemplating delivery" doctrine, and actuarialism migrates from life insurance into fraternal orders that had sworn against it.
  • Institutions: corporate charters, courts, exchanges, regulatory offices, and fraternal by-laws are the vessels in which risk is enclosed, priced, and reallocated across the century.
  • Coercive Labor: the Creole litigation exposes enslaved people as insured property until revolt and British emancipation shatter the masters' claims; the book places self-ownership against slave ownership throughout.
  • Informal Institutions: fraternal orders, mutual-aid societies, kinship savings, and Black communal land use form non-contractual, noncommercial hedges that nonetheless get reabsorbed into formal actuarial and legal structures.
  • Illicit Markets: bucket shops, marriage-insurance swindles, lotteries, and "fictitious" deals define the contested boundary between legitimate speculation and gambling.
  • Speculative Governance: futures speculation is defended as "insurance" and the "self-adjustment of society to the probable"; speculation becomes a mode of governing the economy's future.
  • Commodity Systems: wheat, cotton, and corn become double commodities — physical staples and incorporeal futures — as elevator-receipt and then purely financial exchange transforms the grain trade.
  • Ecological Constraint: the 100th meridian, drought, grasshoppers, and the "fickle-ness of the seasons" force the new sciences of weather and crop forecasting and weather insurance.
  • Frontier Systems: the Homestead Act, land speculation, and pioneer capital systematize westward expansion into the mortgage-insurance complex.
  • Political Economy: the trust question, currency politics, free silver, tariffs, and the Sherman Act make risk a national political economy problem.
  • Elite Formation: the Perkins-Morgan-Rockefeller stratum and the "Big Three" life insurers concentrate financial capital into a new governing elite.
  • Crisis Governance: panics of 1837, 1873, 1893, and 1907 are the recurring tests that expose the limits of private risk management and call forth new institutional fixes.
  • Collapse and Resilience: the 1873 and 1893 collapses, the 1929–1933 bust, and the 1970s deindustrialization (USX, Navistar) bookend the rise and fall of the regime Levy describes.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Prologue: Voyage

Summary: Levy opens with Walt Whitman's 1871 "Passage to India" ("we will risk the ship, ourselves and all") and the Moby-Dick epigraph on chance, free will, and necessity. He argues that "risk" first named the commodity bought and sold in a marine insurance contract — a financial instrument for coping with the uncertainty of shipping goods over maritime space — and that only in the nineteenth century did it become a universal vocabulary for life under capitalism. The etymology runs through French risque, Italian rischio, and a possible Arabic root in maritime "commercial contexts," with the Oxford English Dictionary defining risk as the possibility of "damage to merchandise when transported by sea." In the 1820s "risk" was still being anglicized from "risque." Americans called the sudden, inexplicable twists of the new economy "freaks of fortune." At the opening of the century most Americans were legal dependents whose security came from households, communities, land, and slaves rather than financial instruments; onshore dangers remained "acts of God." By the century's end, however, insurance policies, savings accounts, mortgage-backed securities, futures, and stocks had made the corporation risk management's "institutional home ground," and the moral link between liberal self-ownership and the personal assumption of risk was established. Levy warns that every effort at corporate risk management "manufactured new forms of uncertainty and insecurity," with the freaks as the events that always eluded mastery.
Analysis: Whitman and Melville supply the voyage idiom before Levy uncovers the technical genealogy. The prologue frames the book's thesis through Financial Infrastructure and Commercial Society: security migrates from household, land, and providence into corporate, probabilistic institutions. The "freaks of fortune" thesis — risk management manufacturing new risk — connects to the MoC Finance, Firms, and Industrialization and the reading path Finance, Allocation, and Industrial Power, and anticipates the twentieth-century outcome in State Capacity.
Source anchors: Walt Whitman; "Passage to India"; Moby-Dick; risque; rischio; Oxford English Dictionary; marine insurance; freaks of fortune; self-ownership; acts of God.

Chapter 1: The Assumption of Risk

Summary: Chapter 1 centers on Nicholas Farwell, an engine-man on the one-year-old Boston and Worcester Railroad whose right hand was crushed in 1836 when a fellow employee mislaid a switch. Chief Justice Lemuel Shaw's 1842 decision in Farwell v. Boston and Worcester R.R. Corp. ruled that Farwell had taken "upon himself the risks and perils incident to his situation," his two-dollar-per-day wage containing a "premium for the risk which he thus assumes." Shaw rested on marine-insurance precedent — including his own 1841 decision Copeland v. New England Marine Insurance Company — thereby treating Farwell as a ship's owner rather than a waged seaman (whose wages were not insurable), and dispensed with the master-servant rule of respondeat superior and its "fellow servant" liability. Levy reads Farwell as a second enclosure: just as English commons were fenced into absolute property, so a contingency was enclosed and commodified as a personal "accident risk," which Farwell could then offload onto the accident-insurance corporations that appeared on both ends of his old line by 1846. He documents the new market — a conductor's fifteen-cent policy paid $200 in 1850 — and traces the intellectual climate: Edwin Freedley's 1856 A Practical Treatise on Business ("A man has a right... to risk his own capital"), Fortuna as "a fickle goddess" in an 1861 New Orleans article "A Lucky Freak of Fortune," William Dean Howells's A Hazard of New Fortunes (1890) and its "economic chance-world," Tocqueville on America as a "vast lottery," John O'Sullivan's 1839 "The Great Nation of Futurity," and Karl Polanyi's "double movement." Panics of 1837, 1873, 1893, and 1907 frame the narrative; Rockefeller eventually insisted "God gave me my money." By the end, risk's moral logic had attached itself to freedom, and financiers were "in possession of other people's money."
Analysis: Farwell's crushed hand is Levy's demonstration of Path Dependence and Institutional Drift: maritime insurance reasoning migrates inland and is institutionalized by Institutions and the law. The enclosure of a contingency and the commodification of peril advance Commercial Society while the wage-worker's dependence on accident insurers exposes the marriage of freedom and financial dependence. The chapter's productive-speculation/labor debate and its growth backdrop (speculative "creative destruction" settling a continent and building Chicago) genuinely engage the growth narrative of The Rise and Fall of American Growth. Respondeat superior and the master-servant hierarchy also tie the chapter to Coercive Labor.
Source anchors: Nicholas Farwell; Boston and Worcester Railroad; Lemuel Shaw; Farwell v. Boston and Worcester R.R.; two-dollar wage; premium for the risk; respondeat superior; Copeland v. New England Marine Ins. Co.; enclosure; Edwin Freedley; William Dean Howells; economic chance-world; Tocqueville; Karl Polanyi; panic of 1837.

Chapter 2: The Perils of the Seas

Summary: Chapter 2 returns to marine insurance through the merchant world of "risk communities" — Joseph Balch's Boston brokerage journal, Robert Pringle's Charleston "Risque and Account" letters, William Vernon's Rhode Island slave-trade insurance, and the legal rules (insurable interest, deviation, no overvaluation, premium rating) that governed the double commodification of cargo into "cotton risks" and "slave risks." The centerpiece is the brig Creole, which sailed around November 6, 1841 from Norfolk for New Orleans with a cargo of tobacco and 135 enslaved people insured by their owners. On the night of November 7, nineteen men led by Madison Washington (with Elijah Morris, Ben Blacksmith, and Doctor Cuffin) seized the ship after a pistol shot grazed First Mate Zephaniah C. Gifford's head; a white passenger, John R. Hewell, was killed. They forced the slave overseer William Henry Merritt to navigate to Nassau in the British Bahamas, arriving November 8, where most were freed November 12 under the 1833 British emancipation — following the Comet, Encomium, Enterprise, and Hermosa precedents and the Colonial Office rule that slaves reaching British soil were free. In Thomas McCargo v. The New Orleans Insurance Company (decided 1845), the underwriters' lawyer Judah P. Benjamin argued that the slaves' desire for liberty was an "inherent vice" and their revolt an "act of man" voiding the policy; Justice Henry Adams Bullard ruled for the owners but agreed the insurrection — not the British seizure — was the "proximate cause" of the loss, meaning the nineteen had repossessed their own "risks." Levy closes with the Zong jettison case (Gregson v. Gilbert), British abolitionism, and Frederick Douglass's The Heroic Slave (1852) and Chesapeake Bay "ships" apostrophe: "You are loosed from your moorings, and are free; I am fast in my chains."
Analysis: The Creole connects maritime insurance to Coercive Labor without metaphor: insured "cargo" included enslaved human beings, and masters had first owned risks on the bodies of others before free men owned risks on themselves. Maritime Power and the British imperial periphery — the Bahamas as a jurisdictional gateway where Atlantic emancipation reversed the property logic of the East India Company-era joint-stock pooling of risk — make the chapter a Empire, Trade, and Maritime Worlds case study. The commodification of peril into insurable categories is core Financial Infrastructure and Commercial Society; the whole chapter anchors the claim that risk's history runs through slavery before self-ownership.
Source anchors: Creole; Madison Washington; Nassau; Thomas McCargo v. New Orleans Insurance Company; Judah P. Benjamin; Henry Adams Bullard; inherent vices; proximate cause; perils of the seas; insurable interest; Joseph Balch; Robert Pringle; Zong; Gregson v. Gilbert; Hermosa; British emancipation; Frederick Douglass; Heroic Slave.

Chapter 3: The Actuarial Science of Freedom

Summary: Chapter 3 follows Elizur Wright from abolitionist activism to the business of life insurance after his 1844 voyage to London, where an outdoor auction block near the Royal Exchange — Englishmen reselling life policies to "speculators" betting on early death — struck him as "slave auctions at home." Wright became America's leading actuary and carried back English actuarial tables for the New England Mutual; premised on self-ownership, life insurance commodified the "productive energy" of a free life. American life insurance leapt from fewer than 100 policies in 1825 (about $168,000 in force) to $800,000 policies and $2.3 billion by 1870, after the panic of 1837, as northern urban middle-class men insured their bodies — beneficiaries at first creditors, then wives and children. Levy places Wright beside the southern alternatives: the countermovement of landed independence (the "bank of earth"), and the proslavery "slave mastery" hedge defended by Charles Hodge and James Henley Thornwell in Cotton is King, by George Fitzhugh ("Slavery insurance... never fails") and Henry Hughes ("warranteeism"), and by William John Grayson's The Hireling and the Slave. Actuarial science introduced probability theory — the "law of mortality," unity, level premiums, reserves — and Wright used it to fight the forfeiture clause: surrender values, the 1858 Massachusetts Life Insurance Registry, the surrender-value law of April 10, 1861, and finally the 1876 Supreme Court victory in New York Life Insurance Company v. Statham, which made a policy's actuarial "value" the insured's property. Levy notes the irony that the Court's ruling applied mainly to former Confederates, and that the ex-abolitionist died an atheist actuary. Life-insurance reserves (about 80 percent of assets in 1860 went into farm mortgages) quietly funded northern farm lending.
Analysis: Elizur Wright is Levy's clearest example of reform from inside corporate risk management, and his religion-and-commerce arguments show Religion and Legitimacy and Legitimacy financing the new probabilistic worldview — providence recedes, the "law of mortality" ascends, and Institutions (the Massachusetts commissioner, Statham) enforce the actuarial science of freedom. The sharp self-ownership-versus-slave-ownership contrast extends Coercive Labor, while the routing of reserves and premiums into farm mortgages connects the chapter to Capital Allocation and to State Capacity as Wright wields the coercive arm of the state. The growth-and-distribution backdrop (a "life risk" market that would shortly become the world's largest) pairs with The Rise and Fall of American Growth.
Source anchors: Elizur Wright; Royal Exchange; London auction block; New England Mutual; panic of 1837; actuarial science; mortality tables; level premiums; surrender values; Massachusetts insurance commissioner; Loomis v. Eagle Life; Statham; Cotton is King; Fitzhugh; Hughes; Thornwell; slave mastery; landed independence; self-ownership; farm mortgages.

Chapter 4: The Failure of the Freedman's Bank

Summary: Chapter 4 opens with emancipation: four million newly freed slaves, Lincoln's Second Inaugural, and the providential wartime conviction that the "Jubilee" was foreordained (Clara Jones's testimony, James Lynch's sermons, even Charles Hodge's pivot). Yankee emancipators insisted the freedpeople must offload their personal risks onto the northern financial system. Lincoln signed the Freedman's Bank charter on March 13, 1865, a nonprofit savings bank run by white northern philanthropists — John W. Alvord, Edward Atkinson, Samuel Gridley Howe, Rufus Saxton — who directed freedpeople toward wages and savings instead of the land many demanded ("Gib us our own land and we take care ourselves," a South Carolina freedman told a reporter). Levy traces the wartime experiments: the Free Labor Bank of New Orleans under General Nathaniel P. Banks, the South Carolina Freedmen's Savings Bank, and Jay Cooke's mass marketing of Union war bonds. Sherman's Field Order 15 promised forty-acre plots on 400,000 coastal acres, but Andrew Johnson's Amnesty Proclamation and the October 17, 1865 restoration meeting — where the Edisto Islanders petitioned, "we want Homesteads... to lay our Fathers bones upon" — ended land redistribution. From 1867 Henry Cooke ran the bank's finance committee, moving deposits — $50 million from nearly 100,000 depositors by 1873 — into Pacific Railroad and Northern Pacific bonds and speculative loans while lecturing freedpeople against gambling in lotteries. Jay Cooke & Co. failed September 18, 1873; the Freedman's Bank suffered runs, Frederick Douglass took over as president in March 1874 and loaned it $10,000, and on July 2, 1874 the bank closed with about $3 million owed, an unaccountable deficit of $218,000, and $1.2 million of bad loans. Receivers repaid just over $1.7 million (a 41 percent loss at time of failure); the receivership ran until 1919. Freedpeople had used the bank to buy mules, tools, and land (1 in 21 rural black SC families owned land by 1870), and black mutual-aid societies and churches had lodged their treasuries there.
Analysis: The Freedman's Bank turns emancipation into a controlled experiment in Financial Infrastructure: freedpeople's savings become "a distinct new form of social dependence residing in the opaque sinews of financial intermediation." The chapter tests State Capacity (federal chartering, the Bureau, the failure of federal guarantees) and Capital Allocation (sharecroppers' deposits recycled into Northern Pacific construction and the westward railroad rush), while the freedpeople's landed-independence countermovement and the collapse into sharecropping extend Coercive Labor and the archive's agrarian-wage labor theme. Panic of 1873 as the "coming-out party for the economic chance-world" links forward to Crisis Governance and the savings-bank runs that followed.
Source anchors: Freedman's Bank; March 1865; John W. Alvord; Edward Atkinson; Rufus Saxton; Samuel Gridley Howe; Jay Cooke & Co.; Henry Cooke; Northern Pacific; Sherman's Field Order 15; Edisto Islanders; Andrew Johnson; sharecropping; panic of 1873; Frederick Douglass; $50 million; receivership.

Chapter 5: Betting the Farm

Summary: Chapter 5 follows the western farmer from landed independence into mortgage and forecasting systems. Henry and Rosie Ise mortgage their 160 Kansas acres in 1887 for $363 to hire a doctor for polio-stricken John; the mortgage enters the new "western mortgage market" and becomes, in his memoir, the "relentless master of the family destinies." Levy reconstructs the machinery: brokers like A. W. Ocobock and Austin Corbin's Conduits (the New England Mortgage Security Company, the Corbin Banking Company, the American Mortgage Company of Scotland), securitization into debentures, the "craze" of the 1880s, and its collapse in the panic of 1893. The Olmsteds' fine-grained mortgage (a $400 loan at 10 percent interest, balloon payment, usury suit) and Jane Addams's horrified withdrawal of her inheritance from western mortgages personalize the abstraction. Farming faced the closing of the forestal frontier near the 100th meridian, wheat monoculture, drought, grasshoppers, and world-market competition; farmers turned to market, crop, and weather forecasting (Congress created the National Weather Service in 1870 and it began daily forecasts in 1871; Benner's Prophecies, weather flags, Frederick L. Hoffman's Tornadoes and Tornado Insurance) and to life insurance — the Travelers sold "accident insurance" against lost labor time. The 1890 Census's Report of Real Estate Mortgages showed 28.9 percent of taxable farm acreage encumbered, with Kansas and Nebraska over 50 percent; the Atkinson-Goodloe debate in the Forum over whether debt meant slavery or business set off the Populist Revolt of the 1890s against the deflationary gold standard. Levy concludes that financialization, not proletarianization, brought agriculture into the "Age of Capital," and that the mortgage followed farmers into their homes and "worked" them — Will Carleton's "Tramp's Story" has the wife dying "of mortgage."
Analysis: Western farmers show landed independence losing its insulating power while surviving as an ideal, making Chapter 5 a study in Capital Allocation and Financial Infrastructure — land becomes a claim circulating through insurers, savings banks, debenture holders, and soon futures markets. The Ecological Constraint of the 100th meridian and the disciplinary mortgage-insurance complex (plus Homestead-era settlement) align with Frontier Systems and with the reading path Private Frontier Capital, Infrastructure, and Technical Risk. The forecasting sciences and the quantification of farming risk are early exercises in Information and Coordination, and the chapter's growth statistics (Kansas corn from 30 million to 750 million bushels, Dakota wheat to 60 million) are the agricultural side of The Rise and Fall of American Growth; the collapse-and-failure arc (1893, then the Dust Bowl) links to Collapse and Resilience.
Source anchors: Henry and Rosie Ise; John Ise; western mortgage market; mortgage-backed securities; Austin Corbin; New England Mortgage Security Company; debentures; Olmsteds; Jane Addams; Homestead Act of 1862; 100th meridian; weather forecasting; National Weather Service; Travelers Insurance; accident insurance; 1890 census; Report of Real Estate Mortgages; Populist Revolt; gold standard; Will Carleton; "Tramp's Story".

Chapter 6: Fraternity in the Age of Capital

Summary: Chapter 6 examines fraternal benefit orders as a collective countermovement after the panic of 1873. John Upchurch, a former Erie Railroad master mechanic, founded the Ancient Order of United Workmen at Meadville, Pennsylvania in 1868; reformed after 1873 it spread nationally on the "assessment system" — uniform $1 assessments and $2,000 certificates, no reserves, no "insurance" word, no actuarial pricing, certificates explicitly not contracts. Levy sets this against William Graham Sumner's What Social Classes Owe to Each Other (1883) — "life, at your own risk" — and shows the fraternals' twin rejections of actuarial science and contract: ritual brotherhood (Knights of Pythias, Ancient Order of Foresters, Knights of Honor), racial exclusion (the white Pythians expelled Dr. Thomas Skinner; the Colored Knights of Pythias formed separately, with women often leading black mutual aid), and the quasi-legal internal tribunals of the Grand Lodges. The commercial industry fought back — Jacob Greene's Connecticut Mutual called the certificate "speculative insurance"; actuaries invoked the "law of mortality" — while the industry itself rescued itself with tontine insurance (introduced by the Equitable's Henry B. Hyde in 1867), which bundled speculation on cohort death and lapse into the policy; by 1905 there were 9 million tontine policies and $6 billion in force, mostly written by the Big Three (Mutual Life, New York Life, Equitable). Meanwhile the Prudential (1875) and Metropolitan imported the English "industrial insurance" of weekly premium collection for funeral costs, amassing capital while lapsing most policyholders early. Courts and constitutions forced the fraternals onto actuarial rails: State v. Miller (Iowa, 1885) declared the AOUW an insurance company and its certificates binding contracts, and the 1885 National Fraternal Congress began compiling vital statistics; by 1899 the Knights of Honor lodged a reserve fund at the Farmers' Loan and Security Trust Company in New York. Labor absorbed the assessment idea (Gompers's Cigar Makers, the railroad brotherhoods), while the 1883 Senate hearings probing Jay Gould on "relations between labor and capital" exposed the "Sancho Panza" defense of the wage system.
Analysis: Fraternalism is the vault's best case of Informal Institutions becoming financially consequential: ritual brotherhood, assessments, and beneficiary certificates turn fraternity into family security outside contract — a voluntary-order case that genuinely engages the themes of The Machinery of Freedom. But mortality tables and reserves force the orders into the same probabilistic world as life insurers, a convergence of competing collective strategies that illustrates Path Dependence and Institutional Drift and the absorbing power of Financial Infrastructure and Institutions. The tontine's "speculative inducement" and the courts' contract rulings show Commercial Society and legality overriding fraternity, while the state examinations of 1885-1905 (and the 1890 New York law barring tontine accounting) foreshadow State Capacity.
Source anchors: Ancient Order of United Workmen; John Upchurch; assessment system; $2,000 certificates; Grand Lodge; State v. Miller; National Fraternal Congress; Knights of Pythias; Ancient Order of Foresters; Knights of Honor; Colored Knights of Pythias; William Graham Sumner; tontine insurance; Henry B. Hyde; Equitable; Big Three; Prudential; industrial insurance; John F. Dryden; Samuel Gompers; Cigar Makers International Union; Jay Gould; Farmers' Loan and Security Trust.

Chapter 7: Trading the Future

Summary: Chapter 7 turns to commodities futures, the decade after 1870's frontier of corporate risk management. Charles Pillsbury's 1892 testimony before the House Committee on Agriculture (the hearings "Fictitious Dealings in Agricultural Products") — "some young fellow who stands howling around the Chicago wheat pit" setting the price of the year's wheat — opens the fight over whether the pits' incorporeal trade was legitimate production or gambling. Levy explains the mechanics: contracts for "September wheat," the method of "setting off" price differentials, short-selling and going long, "scalpers" and "locals," elevator receipts, and the telegraph-led separation of the futures market's geography from the physical crop (a New York volume of 8.5 billion bushels of wheat contracts vs. 162 million bushels actually delivered, 1885–1889). Andrew J. Sawyer, Minneapolis's grain-elevator king, testified that he used the Chicago Board of Trade to "insure" 25,000 unsellable bushels a day held back from a weak market. The Illinois courts invented the doctrine of "contemplating delivery" in 1875 (Pickering v. Cease), which Wisconsin's Barnard v. Backhaus (1881) and the U.S. Supreme Court's Irwin v. Williar (1884) tightened; anti-futures and anti-option legislation (the Hatch and Washburn bills of 1892) died in Congress as Populists rallied round Bryan's "Cross of Gold" and Macune's subtreasury plan. The "bucket-shop war" ended when Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.'s Board of Trade v. Christie (1905) held "a set-off is, in legal effect, a delivery" — that hedging was "a serious business contract for a legitimate and useful purpose" and speculation by "competent men" "the self-adjustment of society to the probable" — while also granting the Board a property right in its quotations, extinguishing the bucket shops. Levy ties the decision to the pragmatism of William James, Charles Sanders Peirce, and Chauncey Wright ("bettabilitarians" in a radically uncertain universe), and to economists Henry Crosby Emery ("conjuncture risk") and Allan Willett (The Economic Theory of Risk and Insurance), who recast speculation as social insurance.
Analysis: Futures contracts make Commercial Society temporal — exchange organized around price movement before delivery — and turn wheat, corn, and cotton into double commodities Commodity Systems. The bucket shops, lotteries, and wagering arguments place gambling at the contested boundary of Illicit Markets (see Illicit Markets, Contraband, and State Capacity), while the exchanges' "insurance" argument advances Speculative Governance and Financial Infrastructure. The telegraph and price-information war make it a study in Information and Coordination, and the Populist response — subtreasuries, currency inflation, federal assumption of risk — belongs to Commercial Society and its Stagnation and Institutional Drift variant; Holmes's and James's "radical uncertainty" is the epistemology for a world in which State Capacity and markets jointly price the future.

Sawyer; Charles Pillsbury; House Committee on Agriculture; Fictitious Dealings in Agricultural Products; contemplating delivery; Pickering v. Cease; Barnard v. Backhaus; Irwin v. Williar; Board of Trade v. Christie; Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr.; bucket shops; Christie Grain & Stock Company; William James; Charles Sanders Peirce; Chauncey Wright; pragmatism; Henry Crosby Emery; Allan Willett; Hatch Bill; Macune; subtreasury plan; William Jennings Bryan; "Cross of Gold".

Chapter 8: The Trust Question

Summary: Chapter 8 follows George W. Perkins Sr., the New York Life vice president and J.P. Morgan & Co. partner who believed the "Modern Corporation" could "insure industrial society as a whole." Levy traces Perkins's rise — Denver insurance salesman, reorganizer of the New York Life's agency system, creator of the Nylic Association — and his career as a trust-builder: U.S. Steel (created February 1901 from a $492 million buyout of Carnegie and a $500 million stock issue; 213 plants, 41 mines, over 1,000 miles of railroad, 162,000 employees, \(1.4 billion capitalization) and International Harvester (1902, 85 percent of the farm-machinery market; Perkins final arbiter until 1912). The 1905 New York insurance investigation led by Charles Evans Hughes exposed Perkins's self-dealing — the New York Security and Trust Company cover for the New York Life's illegal U.S. Steel holdings, "\)435,207.71" of profit "wholly without risk" — and Louis Brandeis's Other People's Money made him the symbol of the corrupt "money trust." The panic of 1907 — set off by August Heinze's failed corner of United Copper and the run on the Knickerbocker Trust — was settled when Morgan, with Perkins, moved $25 million of Treasury funds, pried another $25 million from 120 locked-in trust heads at 4:45 a.m., and bought Tennessee Coal, Iron, and Railroad for $45 million to save Moore & Schley (with Roosevelt's antitrust clearance). Perkins then built corporate welfare capitalism: profit-sharing at U.S. Steel from 1902, and International Harvester's Employee Benefit Association (September 1908) with pensions, accident/sickness insurance, and 2 percent wage contributions against corporate contributions. His corporatist politics — the Bureau of Corporations, the Hepburn Bill, "national incorporation and regulation," federal control — culminated in his management of Roosevelt's 1912 Progressive Party campaign ("The very universe... teaches us regulation, supervision and control"), which failed; the Clayton Act's Federal Trade Commission he dismissed as "a poor law with a very good name." The chapter closes with his wartime social-insurance turn and, after the death of his daughter-in-law and grandchild in 1918, his breakdown and death in 1920.
Analysis: George W. Perkins brings the book's insurance and finance history into the industrial corporation: J. P. Morgan, the New York Life's river of reserves, and the trust's promise to end "cutthroat competition" make corporate consolidation a Political Economy event and a mechanism of Elite Formation. The chapter's core causal claim — the trust question politicized risk, making corporate security depend on federal oversight — engages State Capacity and Industrial Policy (the Bureau of Corporations, Sherman Act enforcement, federal incorporation), while the Perkins-era concentration of capital via Morgan, Baker, and the trust companies is the overture to Lords of Finance - The Bankers Who Broke the World's world. Knight's Risk, Uncertainty, and Profit (1921) and John Bates Clark's "risk-maker/risk-taker/risk-reducer" taxonomy formalize the corporate assumption of risk as Institutions, and the growth-and-consolidation backdrop (the Great Merger Movement of 1895–1904) pairs with The Rise and Fall of American Growth.
Source anchors: George W. Perkins; J.P. Morgan & Co.; U.S. Steel; International Harvester; Nylic Association; Charles Evans Hughes; Louis Brandeis; Other People's Money; Frenzied Finance; Thomas Lawson; panic of 1907; Knickerbocker Trust; August Heinze; TC&I; Moore & Schley; Northern Securities; Theodore Roosevelt; Bureau of Corporations; Hepburn Bill; Sherman Antitrust Act; Great Merger Movement; Frank Knight; John Bates Clark; profit-sharing; Employee Benefit Association; Progressive Party 1912; Woodrow Wilson; Clayton Act; Federal Trade Commission.

Epilogue: Freaks of Fortune

Summary: The epilogue carries the story through the twentieth century. In 1979 David Roderick — a financier, not a steel man — became CEO of U.S. Steel and began closing mills; by 1984 employment had fallen from ~166,800 to 88,753 while he leveraged a $6 billion Marathon Oil purchase. International Harvester (Navistar from 1987) shed $65 million in pension and $20 million in shutdown obligations through Chase Manhattan dummy corporations; Wall Street's "shareholder value" and the leveraged buyout threat (Icahn, Pickens) hollowed out the corporate welfare regime Perkins had built. Levy then explains the "freaks of fortune" themselves: sudden, unforeseeable, amoral turns of wealth — a former slave who died worth $85,000, an anonymous beggar who reached $800,000, the banker Daniel Dale Haskel who died in an almshouse, the Kentucky lottery farmer — recounted in "anti-Horatio Alger" narratives as the power of time and chance. The New Deal warred on the freaks: Glass-Steagall (1932), the SEC (1934), government mortgage insurance, futures regulation, and the 1935 Social Security Act, which Roosevelt called security for "the average man" under "that broader definition of Liberty." Yet Social Security inherited the instruments and the individual-entitlement logic of the corporate insurance principle (a payroll-tax "accounting fiction"), and after World War II the phrase "freaks of fortune" all but vanished from the language. But the 1970s brought the freaks back: C. Wright Mills's critique of "pensioned security," George Gilder's call for risk borne by individual citizens, and a wave of financial innovation in which "capitalism was put back in charge of taming capitalism." Levy ends: "Radical uncertainty rules. Only one prediction feels safe: Capitalism's future will be stormy."
Analysis: The epilogue closes by turning State Capacity toward social risk: Roosevelt, Glass-Steagall, the SEC, and Social Security answer the panic of 1907, the Freedman's Bank, and the failed private instruments with a federal risk community. The claim that the welfare state "inherited" rather than rejected the private insurance principle links Financial Infrastructure and Path Dependence and Institutional Drift, while the New Deal's response to the worst bust since 1837 is a case study in Crisis Governance and War, Crisis, and Collapse. The neoliberal "doppelgänger" return of individualized risk and the 1970s-1980s financialization (Marathon Oil, leveraged buyouts, Chase's pension dumping) speak directly to the pace, growth, and structural decline questions of The Rise and Fall of American Growth, and the rise of a Federal Reserve able to act as lender of last resort (1966) closes the loop on Lords of Finance - The Bankers Who Broke the World's central-banking sequel.

Wright Mills; financialization; "Neoliberal doppelgänger"; Federal Reserve lender of last resort.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • Risk's etymology: from French risque and Italian rischio (probably maritime roots, a possible Arabic candidate); the OED ties risk to the "possibility of damage to merchandise when transported by sea" — for decades Americans still used "risque" for the commodity in a marine policy.
  • Farwell's arithmetic: Nicholas Farwell's right-hand case valued at $10,000; his two-dollar-per-day wage contained Shaw's "premium for the risk"; a Worcester conductor's fifteen-cent railroad-accident policy paid $200 in 1850.
  • The Creole ledger: 135 enslaved people; nineteen male rebels; the Comet (146 freed), Encomium (13), Enterprise, and Hermosa precedents; the 1839 Colonial Office instruction that slaves reaching British soil were free; 1853 U.S. indemnity; the "inherent vices" and "proximate cause" arguments of McCargo v. New Orleans Insurance Company.
  • The insurance takeoff: from under 100 U.S. life policies in 1825 (~$168,000) to $2.3 billion and 800,000 policies by 1870 (about 1 in 3 northeastern adult males); Wright's surrender-value law of April 10, 1861; Statham (1876) made the "value of the policy" the insured's property — largely benefiting ex-Confederates.
  • Freedman's Bank arithmetic: nearly $50 million from ~100,000 depositors; by 1868 $355,000 of $928,063.49 in assets sat in transcontinental bonds; failure July 2, 1874; unaccountable deficit \(218,000; 61,114 accounts, ~\)3 million owed; $1.2 million in bad loans; 41 percent loss at failure; only $1.7 million repaid by 1919.
  • Betting the farm: the Ise mortgage was $363 for a polio doctor; the 1890 census counted 4.6 million farms averaging 137 acres, 28.9 percent of taxable acreage encumbered, Kansas and Nebraska >50 percent, average loan $1,032 at 7.36 percent over 4.54 years, and 89.82 percent of debt for land/improvements; Will Carleton's wife in "Tramp's Story" "died of mortgage."
  • The accident men: the Travelers (chartered 1864 in Hartford) claimed its millionth accident policy by 1885 and $232 million of insurance in force; "experiencing" brakeman J. Furney Patterson collected six separate claims in eighteen months.
  • Fraternity numbers: the AOUW ran on $1 assessments and $2,000 certificates; by 1880 it had 79,248 members across lodges; the 1885 State v. Miller made certificates contracts; by 1905 tontine stood at $6 billion in force across 9 million policies (Big Three share $5 billion).
  • Christie's shorthand: Holmes's 1905 one-liners — "Set-off has all the effects of delivery"; hedging is a "serious business contract for a legitimate and useful purpose"; speculation by "competent men" is "the self-adjustment of society to the probable." Bucket shops were extinguished via the Board's property right in its quotations (197 injunctions in 1905 alone).
  • U.S. Steel scale: $1.4 billion capitalization (about 40 percent "water"), 213 plants, forty-one mines, over 1,000 miles of railroad, 162,000 employees, 6.8 percent of 1901 GNP; Perkins's profit-sharing by 1927 had spread to 315 corporations and 800,000 employees at over $1 billion of stock.
  • The 1907 panic in one line: Morgan's locked-door all-night session extracted \(25 million from 120 bank and trust heads; the TC&I purchase (\)45 million for a steelmaker Moody later valued near $1 billion) closed the panic and begat the Federal Reserve in 1914.
  • Bookend images: the Creole insured "slave risks" in 1841; David Roderick's USX — the same corporate vessel Perkins helped launch — divested itself of steel for Marathon Oil in the 1980s. "Freaks of fortune" all but disappeared from the language after 1945 and returned with the financialization of the 1970s.
