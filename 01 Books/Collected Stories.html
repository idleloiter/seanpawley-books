Collected Stories

Buy on Amazon — Collected Stories

Detailed overview

William Faulkner's Collected Stories arranges Yoknapatawpha, Jefferson, wartime Europe, New York, Italy, the Caribbean, and dreamlike interiors into six named groupings rather than a single chronology. The opening country stories begin with tenant farms, mules, small stores, church shingles, radios at Old Man Killegrew's, and the Frenchman's Bend habits of bargaining, boasting, and watching. That rural world is not treated as pastoral escape. It is where Abner Snopes teaches Sarty that blood loyalty can mean perjury and arson, where V. K. Ratliff turns a joke on Lucius Provine into a story about old grievance and racial revenge, and where two boys hearing about Pearl Harbor through a neighbor's radio translate world war into Memphis buses, firewood, and a shikepoke egg.

The Jefferson village stories tighten the focus around public knowledge. Miss Emily Grierson becomes a town monument whose house preserves Homer Barron as much as it preserves old tax arrangements; Minnie Cooper's rumor about Will Mayes turns a barber shop into a lynch mob; Flem Snopes converts missing brass, city audits, Tom-Tom, and Turl into a private monument inside a condemned water tank. These stories show the town as an institution made from gossip, race law, old families, store counters, barber chairs, courthouse habits, and the ability of onlookers to decide when they will not look further.

The wilderness stories move backward into Chickasaw country and federal encounters. Issetibbeha, Moketubbe, Three Basket, Doom, the red-heeled slippers, the slave quarters, and the missing slave in "Red Leaves" make the old plantation order seem both grotesque and bureaucratic. "A Justice," "A Courtship," and "Lo!" turn Chickasaw history into comic procedure: Sam Fathers tells children about Doom and his courtship arithmetic, David Hogganbeck competes with Ikkemotubbe, and a President and Secretary are trapped by Francis Weddel's patient manipulation of Washington ceremony over a ford. Faulkner uses comedy here to expose property, status, and legal authority as costumes that only work when enough people agree to keep wearing them.

The war stories in "The Wasteland" leave Mississippi but keep the same interest in caste, masculine display, and damaged memory. "Ad Astra" places Sartoris, Comyn, Bland, Monaghan, an Indian subadar, and a captured German officer inside the drunken pause after the Armistice, where words about nation, brotherhood, music, and defeat are bruised by French outrage and Allied exhaustion. "Turnabout" and "All the Dead Pilots" use airmen, patrol boats, Captain Bogard, Claude Hope, Captain Spoomer, dogs, Camels, night-flying squadrons, and letters home to show war as a machine that turns private vanity and private courage into paperwork, wreckage, and anecdotes.

The middle-ground stories move between plantation remnants, aviation, spas, New York, Hollywood, Sartoris family decline, and mountain cabins. "Wash" revisits Thomas Sutpen through the eyes of Wash Jones, whose worship of Sutpen survives war, poverty, and Milly's pregnancy until Sutpen's comparison of the girl to a mare breaks the illusion. "Dr. Martino" builds an entire relation between Jules Martino and Louise King from a bench, a metal rabbit, a dangerous horse, and the idea that fear proves life. "The Brooch" turns Howard Boyd's house into a trap between Amy, Mrs. Boyd, a family brooch, a stopped clock, and a book of Green Mansions. This part repeatedly asks what happens when an old loyalty cannot absorb the living body in front of it.

The final "Beyond" grouping is the strangest and most various. Judge Allison, Mothershed, and Ingersoll walk through a possible afterlife full of arguments about proof, sons, and belief; Wilfred Midgleston remakes a botched architectural errand into a goat-faced myth and a newspaper resurrection; "The Leg" makes amputation, double identity, Everbe Corinthia, and Jotham Rust into a ghostly prosecution; "Mistral" turns an Italian village funeral into a story of Giulio, a priest's ward, a sudden draft, and a suspicious death. By the time "Carcassonne" ends with a skeleton under tarred roofing paper, Rincon, Mrs Widdrington, rats, and a buckskin pony galloping into heaven, the collection has moved from local social fact into the mind's last effort to invent a bold performance out of hunger, idleness, and bodily decay.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Informal Institutions: Jefferson, Frenchman's Bend, hunting camps, barber shops, churches, and family houses govern conduct through gossip, deference, reputation, and remembered obligations.
  • Legibility: draft papers, town narration, courthouse habits, taxes, receipts, letters, audits, and public rumor test what communities and officials can see or choose not to see.
  • Coercive Labor: tenant labor, family obedience, racial hierarchy, plantation remnants, and war service repeatedly turn duty into extraction.
  • Language and Ideology: stories such as "Dry September" show how rumor, racial categories, honor language, and public narration make violence actionable.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: the Sartoris, Sutpen, Snopes, Grierson, and Chickasaw materials track how authority survives, mutates, or fails across families and local orders.
  • Technological Change: radios, airplanes, cars, Hollywood publicity, stations, and war machines expand local worlds while preserving older conflicts inside new machinery.
  • Knowledge Preservation: Faulkner's collection preserves local grievance, family myth, public memory, and official residue through story rather than archive alone.

Section-by-section notes

Barn Burning

Summary: Sarty Snopes sits in a Justice of the Peace's store that smells of cheese, tin cans, fear, and "the old fierce pull of blood" while Mr. Harris accuses Abner Snopes of burning his barn after a dispute over a hog, wire, and a dollar pound fee. The Justice asks Colonel Sartoris Snopes to testify, Harris refuses to make the boy lie, and Abner is ordered to leave the country before dark. The family wagon carries Sarty's mother, aunt, sisters, older brother, broken furniture, and a stopped dowry clock to Major de Spain's place, where Abner deliberately tracks horse manure across a pale imported rug and later scrubs it with lye and field stone. After the court assesses twenty bushels of corn, Abner prepares another burning, but Sarty breaks from blood loyalty, runs to warn de Spain, hears shots, and walks alone into the dark. Source anchors: cheese store, Mr. Harris, Colonel Sartoris Snopes, dollar pound fee, Major de Spain, pale rug, twenty bushels, blood loyalty.

Analysis: The cheese store makes the boy's moral crisis bodily before anyone names it: hunger, shame, and fear surround Colonel Sartoris Snopes before he understands law. Mr. Harris and the dollar pound fee show that Abner's violence begins in ordinary tenancy disputes but exceeds them through fire, while Major de Spain and the pale rug give Abner a target grand enough to dramatize his hatred of owners. The twenty bushels judgment forces Sarty to see coercive labor at family scale: blood loyalty is not protection but the mechanism by which Abner recruits children into retaliation. The story closes by removing Sarty from the wagon, so the collection begins with an act of refusal rather than reconciliation.

Shingles for the Lord

Summary: A boy narrator follows his father after Pap borrows a froe and maul from Killegrew's so he can help reshingle the church, a community job that should be pious but becomes a contest of laziness, work pride, and bargaining. Pap tries to evade responsibility, Res Grier's neighbors gather around the church roof, and the men measure one another through shingles, time, mules, borrowed tools, and church obligation. Cash offers, wagers, and rural reputation matter as much as religion, while the boy watches adult talk turn a simple repair into a test of who can make whom do unpaid labor. The story's humor comes from the gap between service "for the Lord" and the very human maneuvering around the job. Source anchors: froe and maul, Killegrew's, shingles, church roof, borrowed tools, Res Grier, mules, for the Lord.

Analysis: The froe and maul anchor the story in actual rural work instead of devotional language; the job is wood, shingles, and a roof that leaks. Killegrew's and borrowed tools show informal institutions where obligation travels through objects, favors, and remembered debts. Res Grier and the mules place religious duty inside the same bargaining economy as hauling, plowing, and loafing. By making "for the Lord" coexist with evasion, Faulkner turns church repair into a comic measurement of character, where belief is less visible than who picks up the tool and who stands aside talking.

The Tall Men

Summary: A federal investigator and a marshal arrive at a dark farm after passing the cotton gin, the doctor's coupe, a lamplit house, and a baying hound, intending to arrest two McCallum brothers as draft evaders. Instead of finding cowardice, the investigator meets a household organized around Dr. Schofield, old Anse McCallum, a sick mother, family military memory, and a code of self-reliance that treats Washington papers as less binding than local honor. The McCallums do not hide from service; they insist on managing illness, crops, and obligations in their own order, and the investigator is forced to distinguish bureaucratic evasion from a family that has already given sons to earlier wars. The title points to height of conduct rather than physical size. Source anchors: cotton gin, doctor's coupe, McCallum brothers, draft evaders, Dr. Schofield, Anse McCallum, Washington papers, local honor.

Analysis: The cotton gin and doctor's coupe announce that the investigator has entered a farm crisis, not a simple law-enforcement scene. The McCallum brothers and draft evaders labels matter because the federal category is true on paper but false as a description of motive. Dr. Schofield and Anse McCallum reveal the household's real chain of command: sickness, crop work, memory, and local honor precede Washington papers. The story tests legibility under national mobilization, asking whether distant officials can see men who are willing to fight but refuse to let paperwork define their honor.

A Bear Hunt

Summary: V. K. Ratliff tells how Lucius Provine's drunken hiccups disrupt Major de Spain's hunting camp after bear meat, whisky, deer stands, Uncle Ike McCaslin, Old Man Ash, and John Basket's mound all enter the tale. Ratliff jokingly suggests that the Indians at the mound can cure the hiccups, and Provine walks there with a lantern and shotgun, not knowing that Ash has gone ahead to warn the Indians that a revenue agent is coming. The Indians stage a fake execution with wood and fire, Provine flees back cured, attacks Ratliff, and Ash later explains that he used the joke to avenge a collar Provine burned twenty years earlier. The bear hunt disappears behind a story about memory, humiliation, and delayed payment. Source anchors: V. K. Ratliff, Lucius Provine, Major de Spain, Uncle Ike McCaslin, Old Man Ash, John Basket, revenue agent, blue collar.

Analysis: V. K. Ratliff supplies the comic surface, but Old Man Ash supplies the story's hidden accounting system. Lucius Provine's hiccups, Major de Spain's camp, and Uncle Ike McCaslin create a white male leisure world that assumes Black servants are background. John Basket and the false revenue agent turn that assumption against Provine by making him the object of a staged terror. The blue collar gives Ash's preserved memory its exact measure: he has not waited twenty years for abstract justice, but for compensation for a specific insult that white men forgot and he did not.

Two Soldiers

Summary: A nearly nine-year-old boy and his brother Pete listen outside Old Man Killegrew's window as the radio reports Pearl Harbor, Japanese bombs, General MacArthur, the Philippines, and the Pacific Ocean, which the child first imagines as the government reservoir near Oxford. Pete decides he must go to war even though the family still has vetch, firewood, a lagging father, and a grieving mother to manage at home. After Pete takes the six o'clock bus toward Memphis to join the Army, the boy sneaks out with a slingshot and a shikepoke egg, walks twenty-two miles to Jefferson, meets Mr. Foote, Mrs. Habersham, and a bus agent, and reaches Memphis only to be rejected by the recruiting soldier. The story turns world war into a child's stubborn attempt to follow his brother with wood, water, and a pocketknife. Source anchors: Old Man Killegrew, Pearl Harbor, Pacific Ocean, vetch, six o'clock bus, shikepoke egg, Mrs. Habersham, Memphis.

Analysis: Old Man Killegrew's radio lets Pearl Harbor enter a farm family through sound before geography, which is why the Pacific Ocean has to be translated away from Oxford. The vetch and firewood keep Pete's decision from becoming pure patriotism; leaving means abandoning work that will still be there. The six o'clock bus and Memphis make enlistment a physical route the child can imagine walking, while the shikepoke egg shows how little his economy matches the Army's. Mrs. Habersham and the recruiting soldier mark a logistics and throughput boundary: adults can move him through buses and offices, but they cannot let him enter Pete's war.

Shall Not Perish

Summary: After the message about Pete arrives in a pale envelope, the same child narrator watches his mother, father, fields, mailbox, and family language reorganize around loss. Pete's death does not produce grand public speech; it enters through the farm fence, the mother's unsunbonneted face, and the boy's memory of his brother leaving for the Army after Pearl Harbor. The family receives national condolence and local attention, but the child processes death through concrete tasks, older boys, the Army, and the belief that Pete's sacrifice must belong to something beyond the farm. The story continues "Two Soldiers" by showing what the earlier bus ride costs. Source anchors: pale envelope, Pete, mailbox, farm fence, Pearl Harbor, Army, unsunbonneted face, family loss.

Analysis: The pale envelope matters because it is official and intimate at once; it brings the Army into the field without ceremony. Pete and Pearl Harbor return from "Two Soldiers" changed from motive into consequence, and the mailbox becomes the point where world war touches the family fence. The unsunbonneted face tells the boy that his mother understands before anyone opens or explains the message. By keeping family loss inside farm gestures rather than speeches, Faulkner makes the national claim "shall not perish" depend on whether a child can connect Pete's absence to any durable meaning.

A Rose for Emily

Summary: The town narrates Miss Emily Grierson's life after her death, beginning with her funeral, her decaying house with cupolas and scrolled balconies, Colonel Sartoris's old tax remission, and the later aldermen who try to collect from her. The story moves backward and forward through her father's control, the smell around the house, lime sprinkled at night, Homer Barron the Yankee foreman, the Baptist minister, arsenic bought from the druggist, and the townspeople's speculation about marriage. After Emily dies, the sealed upstairs room reveals Homer Barron's body and a gray hair on the pillow beside it. Jefferson's curiosity becomes belated evidence. Source anchors: Miss Emily Grierson, Colonel Sartoris, tax remission, Homer Barron, arsenic, lime, sealed room, gray hair.

Analysis: Miss Emily Grierson is not simply eccentric; she is a civic problem produced by Colonel Sartoris and the tax remission, which teach her that old status can cancel ordinary rules. Homer Barron and arsenic give the town's gossip a physical center, but lime and the sealed room show how Jefferson prefers containment to confrontation. The gray hair makes the final discovery both intimate and institutional, because the town has been managing Emily through informal institutions of deference while failing to understand the corpse inside the monument. The story turns communal narration into an autopsy of its own evasions.

Hair

Summary: The barber Maxey tells how Susan Reed, an orphan living with the Burchetts, first comes into the shop with frightened eyes and distinctive hair that becomes the visible thread of her life. Mr. McLendon, Hawkshaw the barber, Mrs. Burchett, and the townspeople watch Susan grow from neglected girl into a woman whose hair attracts attention, pity, and control. Hawkshaw's quiet attachment to Susan is filtered through barber-shop talk, errands, and the social difference between an older man and a vulnerable girl. The story studies care that never knows how to declare itself cleanly. Source anchors: Susan Reed, Burchett, Maxey, Hawkshaw, barber shop, straight hair, orphan girl, frightened eyes.

Analysis: Susan Reed's straight hair is the story's evidence because it is what the barber shop can notice without admitting what Susan needs. Burchett and orphan girl place her inside dependency, while Maxey and Hawkshaw show two forms of looking: gossiping observation and awkward guardianship. The barber shop matters as a male institution that trims, talks, and judges but cannot easily house tenderness. By making Hawkshaw's care indirect, Faulkner keeps the story uneasy; protection, desire, class difference, and pity remain braided in the same visible strand.

Centaur in Brass

Summary: Flem Snopes, now superintendent of the Jefferson power plant, secretly creates a monument to himself through missing brass, city audits, and a manipulation of the Black firemen Turl and Tom-Tom. Harker narrates how Snopes uses a magnet, old valves, discarded fittings, Tom-Tom's fear of losing the day shift, Turl's reputation for night prowling, and Tom-Tom's young wife to move brass into hiding. The plot turns when Tom-Tom and Turl discover Snopes's game, cooperate, hide the brass in the water tank, and make Snopes pay auditors first three hundred four dollars and fifty-two cents, then another two hundred twenty. The "monument" is a poisoned, condemned water tank. Source anchors: Flem Snopes, power plant, brass, Turl, Tom-Tom, Harker, city audit, water tank.

Analysis: Flem Snopes uses the power plant because it is a public utility with private blind spots: brass can disappear while steam still runs. Turl and Tom-Tom are meant to be tools, but their shared discovery turns the city audit against Flem. Analysis: Saucier Weddel's desire to go home is simple, but Jubal, Vatch, and Hule make homecoming pass through race, defeat, and mountain poverty. The Tennessee cabin is not hospitality; it is a pressure chamber where the Civil War continues as family grievance. The laurel copse and Thoroughbred turn Hule's warning into action, proving that the boy understands both the ambush and Weddel better than the adults do. The final rifles show that victory and defeat have not ended violence; they have redistributed it among people who cannot afford mercy.

Beyond

Summary: Judge Allison leaves his deathbed after Lucius Peabody's stethoscope, Chlory's wailing, Jake's silence, his overcoat, jasmine, ebony stick, briefcase, and Voltaire book all accompany him into a strange afterlife crowd. He meets a young bridegroom, Mothershed the atheist, a figure identified as Robert Ingersoll, and a woman with a scarred child playing with Roman soldiers. Allison has lost a son who died after being dragged by a pony, and he seeks not proof exactly but some word he can trust about whether the boy can be found. He refuses the consolations offered, clings to consistency, and turns the afterlife into another courtroom for doubt. Source anchors: Judge Allison, Lucius Peabody, Chlory, Mothershed, Robert Ingersoll, scarred child, Voltaire, dead son.

Analysis: Judge Allison's jasmine, Voltaire, and briefcase show that he enters death carrying the habits of a cultivated federal judge. Mothershed and Robert Ingersoll matter because Allison trusted unbelief more than church language, yet even unbelief cannot give him the dead son. The scarred child with Roman soldiers is the story's hardest test: it offers a recognizable religious image without forcing Allison to accept it. His refusal is not shallow skepticism; it is loyalty to the only intellectual honesty he believes remains his.

Black Music

Summary: The narrator finds Wilfred Midgleston in Rincon after Midgleston has vanished from his New York life, Martha, architecture, Park Avenue aspiration, and the Van Dyming commission. Midgleston tells of carrying theatre plans by train into the Virginia mountains, seeing a horned face by a white fence, drinking "tonic," buying a tin whistle, shedding clothes, releasing a bull, and terrifying Mrs. Carleton Van Dyming in the vineyard. Newspaper clippings misname him as Middleton, report a maniac attack, and then let him send Martha proof that he has become someone worth printing. His life after disappearance rests on the belief that he was chosen for one act beyond ordinary human measure. Source anchors: Wilfred Midgleston, Rincon, Martha, Van Dyming, horned face, tin whistle, Mrs. Carleton Van Dyming, newspaper clippings.

Analysis: Wilfred Midgleston's name is the fragile property he protects after abandoning everything else, which is why the newspaper clippings matter so much. Rincon gives him a marginal place from which to turn the Van Dyming humiliation into myth. The horned face and tin whistle let him imagine himself as an instrument of forces beyond architecture or employment, while Mrs. Carleton Van Dyming supplies the social victim whose panic becomes his evidence. Martha remains central because his fantasy of being printed is partly a gift to the woman who told him he was as good as the people in the papers.

The Leg

Summary: Davy remembers Oxford, George, Everbe Corinthia at the lock, Simon, Jotham Rust, a yawl, a skiff, Milton, and a comic fall into the Thames before the memory crosses into the war where George is dead and Davy has lost a leg. In hospital, Davy hallucinates George searching for the amputated leg to make sure it is dead, while the missing limb becomes a double that may commit acts he cannot own. Later Jotham Rust, Corinthia's brother, attacks Davy because Corinthia died after an encounter with a man whose photograph looks like Davy but seems to be the missing leg's independent life. The padre gives Davy the photograph after Jotham's execution. Source anchors: Davy, George, Everbe Corinthia, Simon, Jotham Rust, amputated leg, photograph, padre.

Analysis: Davy's amputated leg becomes a terrifying answer to the old Oxford comedy with George and Everbe Corinthia: the body part that should be dead keeps moving through guilt. George is the only figure Davy trusts to kill that double, but George is already dead, so the search occurs inside delirium and memory. Jotham Rust and the photograph convert private hallucination into external evidence, which is why the story is so unsettling. The padre's role matters because religious explanation cannot contain what the body and image have produced.

Mistral

Summary: Two young walkers, Don and the narrator, descend an Italian mountain after finishing Milanese brandy and meet a deaf man, his wife, a mule with a goat bell, a shrine, and talk of a funeral for the rich fiance of the priest's ward. The woman's story introduces Giulio Farinzale, a sudden draft, Caporetto, the ward's hidden meetings, delayed banns, the priest's possible desire, and a sudden death just as Giulio is due home. In the village, wind, church bells, the soldier's bicycle, the aunt's house, and the funeral make Don suspect poisoning or priestly manipulation, but the travelers choose shelter and ignorance over action. The mistral blows knowledge into them and then past them. Source anchors: Don, Milanese brandy, goat bell, Giulio Farinzale, priest's ward, funeral, bicycle, mistral.

Analysis: Don and Milanese brandy establish the travelers as witty outsiders, but Giulio Farinzale and the priest's ward make their jokes inadequate. The goat bell and funeral turn the mountain into a place where every sound seems like evidence. The bicycle is crucial because it proves the returned soldier has taken a hidden route, making the story's suspicions practical rather than fanciful. The mistral names the pressure that pushes truth toward the travelers, yet their decision to seek warmth shows how easily moral perception yields to bodily comfort.

Divorce in Naples

Summary: Monckton, the bosun, Carl, George, the narrator, and three silent women sit in Naples while the Greek seaman George curses and the young blond Carl becomes attached to one of the women. George is arrested after trying to leave without settling a bill and stepping on the king's effigy on a coin, spends time with political prisoners, and returns to find Carl gone for three days. Carl comes back before dawn, washes for a long time, throws away clothing, works obsessively on the bridge deck, and avoids the starboard side until he and George slowly resume their partnership at sea. Near Galveston, Carl asks George to buy pink silk teddy-bears for the woman. Source anchors: Monckton, bosun, Carl, George, Naples, king's effigy, political prisoners, pink silk teddy-bears.

Analysis: George and Carl's relation is treated like a rough seamen's marriage, which is why Naples becomes the divorce court. The king's effigy and political prisoners turn George's panic into farce, but Carl's washing and avoidance of the starboard side show real shame and attachment. Monckton and the bosun interpret the pair differently, giving the story its hard comic chorus. The pink silk teddy-bears prove that Carl has not simply been corrupted; he has crossed into adult sexual obligation and wants George to help him honor it in the only form he can imagine.

Carcassonne

Summary: A starving or idle man lies under tarred roofing paper in Luis's garret in Rincon while his skeleton speaks to him, rats move in the dark, Standard Oil and Mrs Widdrington own the space, and a buckskin pony with blue electric eyes gallops through his imagination toward heaven. He thinks of Norman steeds, Saracen emirs, Godfrey of Bouillon, Tancred, chamfrons, Byron, women, rats, and the desire to perform something bold, tragical, and austere. The body lies still under paper, but the mind rides through crusade, sea caves, poetry, and decay. The story ends with the pony still galloping into the high heaven of the world. Source anchors: tarred roofing paper, Rincon, skeleton, buckskin pony, Mrs Widdrington, rats, chamfron, Carcassonne.

Analysis: Tarred roofing paper and Rincon give the fantasy its poverty; the speaker's exalted ride begins from a rented or tolerated corner above a cantina. The skeleton is a brutal companion because it knows stillness while the buckskin pony insists on motion. Mrs Widdrington and rats keep ownership and hunger inside the dream, preventing the medieval images from becoming pure escape. Carcassonne is less a destination than the name of the performance he wants life to become before the body, the skeleton, and the dark insist otherwise.
