Go Down, Moses

Buy on Amazon — Go Down, Moses

Position in the vault

Go Down, Moses is the McCaslin-Edmonds-Beauchamp book of the Faulkner corpus in this vault. It carries Ike McCaslin's moral education, Lucas Beauchamp's later story, the plantation-commissary ledgers, and the hunting-camp world of Sam Fathers, Boon Hogganbeck, Old Ben, and Lion. There is no 04 Series/ hub for the Faulkner corpus, so the book's structural place is the affinity map: it links outward to Intruder in the Dust (Lucas Beauchamp's legal reckoning in Jefferson), Absalom, Absalom! (the dynasty built on blood and repudiated inheritance), Light in August (racial identity and public violence), The Reivers (the comic register of the same McCaslin-Edmonds world, with Boon Hogganbeck), Three Famous Short Novels (the novella version of "The Bear"), and Big Woods (the hunting stories re-gathered later). The Essential Faulkner is the anthology that gathers this material alongside the rest of the corpus.

Thematically the book feeds several hubs and paths: Identity, Culture, and Social Formation (blood, name, race, and legitimacy), State, Order, and Legibility (the ledgers, the census form, the courthouse, the newspaper), Coercive Labor, Commodity Systems, and Extraction (slavery, sharecropping, timber cutting), Identity, Memory, and Cultural Legitimacy (the family chronicle as inheritance), State Formation, Legibility, and Informal Order (plantation governance and the informal law of the hearth and the camp), Testimony, War Memory, and Broken Official Narratives (the deputy's monologue, the census questionnaire, the newspaper notice), and Archives, Skill, and Civilizational Continuity (Sam Fathers' oral transmission and the hunters' tacit woodland knowledge). The note sits beside the other Faulkner notes in Book Index and Linkage Atlas.

Detailed overview

Go Down, Moses is organized as a set of linked McCaslin and Beauchamp stories, not as a linear family chronicle. Faulkner keeps returning to the same land, first taken by Carothers McCaslin from an Indian patent, and to descendants divided by law, race, inheritance, sex, and memory. Isaac McCaslin, Lucas Beauchamp, Molly Beauchamp, Roth Edmonds, Sam Fathers, Tomey's Turl, Tennie, Rider, and Samuel Worsham Beauchamp are not arranged as equal branches of a family tree. They appear through stories that expose what plantation genealogy tries to hide: the people without recognized title often know the land, the bloodline, and the moral debt more intimately than the legal heirs do.

The first two McCaslin stories use comedy and domestic conflict to introduce slavery's afterlife inside kinship. "Was" stages Tomey's Turl's repeated flight to Tennie at Hubert Beauchamp's place as a fox hunt, a marriage trap, and a poker bargain in which enslaved people and white spouses are wagered across the same table. "The Fire and the Hearth" moves that inheritance into Lucas Beauchamp's cabin, where Lucas claims patriarchal authority, hidden gold, and McCaslin descent while Molly insists that the divining machine has put a curse on their household. Faulkner lets farce, dialect, and property law coexist, so that jokes about Uncle Buck, Uncle Buddy, and Miss Sophonsiba open onto the same buried violence that later appears in Lucas's pride — most starkly in the remembered dawn when Lucas stood over the sleeping Zack Edmonds with a razor and then let the pistol cartridges fail to fire.

"Pantaloon in Black" is different in texture because it does not begin from McCaslin genealogy but from grief that the white official world cannot read. Rider's response to Mannie's death is physical, sacramental, and socially illegible: he digs her grave, returns to their cabin, sees or imagines her figure, eats cold food, works at the mill, drinks raw whisky, kills Birdsong, tears the jail cell apart, and is lynched. The deputy's kitchen monologue to his wife turns Rider into an example of supposed Black incomprehensibility, but the reader has already been inside Rider's mourning. Faulkner makes the racist explanation arrive after the evidence that refutes it.

The hunting stories make Isaac McCaslin's moral education depend on Sam Fathers and the wilderness rather than on the plantation house. "The Old People" marks Ike with buck's blood and connects Sam to Ikkemotubbe, Doom, a quadroon mother, and Carothers McCaslin's purchase of Sam and his mother. "The Bear" expands that education through Old Ben, Lion, Boon Hogganbeck, Major de Spain, General Compson, Walter Ewell, the Big Bottom, the commissary ledgers, and Ike's refusal of the McCaslin inheritance. The wilderness is not innocent, but it gives Ike a language of humility and use that opposes ownership, sale, incest, and plantation accounting.

The central burden of "The Bear" is the ledgers' discovery that Eunice drowned herself, that Tomey was Carothers McCaslin's enslaved daughter as well as his sexual victim, and that Tomey's Turl was therefore both son and grandson of the founder. Ike's famous renunciation of the land is not abstract idealism. It is an attempt to refuse a title made out of Indigenous dispossession, slavery, incest, and unpaid labor. Yet Faulkner does not let that refusal become clean redemption: Ike's wife rejects the sexual and economic emptiness of his choice, Lucas remains on the land in a different relation to power, and the timber company later cuts the Big Bottom anyway.

"Delta Autumn" returns to Ike as Uncle Ike, nearly eighty, still traveling to a diminished hunting camp while the Delta has been ditched, planted, leveed, mechanized, and cut away. Roth Edmonds's affair with a light-skinned descendant of Tennie's Jim brings the McCaslin-Beauchamp line back to Ike in the form of a baby. Ike can identify the old family wrong and still cannot answer the young woman's question about love. His advice that she marry within her race sounds like a survival rule from an old man trapped in the very categories the book has spent hundreds of pages exposing as false and destructive.

The title story closes the book by turning the McCaslin plantation's buried history into public ritual. Samuel Worsham Beauchamp, known as Butch in Jefferson and executed in Illinois for killing a Chicago policeman, is found through a census questionnaire and brought home because his grandmother Mollie demands it. Gavin Stevens, Miss Worsham, Hamp Worsham, the newspaper editor, the undertaker, the hearse, the flowers, and the newspaper notice all become part of Mollie's effort to make her grandson's death visible and properly mourned. The refrain "sold my Benjamin" turns the biblical Exodus lament back onto Roth Edmonds, Pharaoh, and the county that would prefer to treat Samuel as a criminal file rather than kin.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Isaac "Uncle Ike" McCaslin: youngest and last male-line McCaslin, born 1867 when his father Theophilus was near seventy; the true heir by male descent who renounces the inheritance. His motivation is to refuse a title grounded in Indigenous dispossession, slavery, and incest, and to live unencumbered — owner of nothing but an iron cot, a mattress, a shotgun, and General Compson's compass and horn. His arc runs from the blood-marked boy in the woods to the old man in the Delta who has "knew" the wrong and cannot repair it.
  • Lucas Quintus Carothers McCaslin Beauchamp: son of Tomey's Turl and Tennie, born 1874, "the oldest living McCaslin descendant still living on the hereditary land"; proud, impervious, financially independent (three thousand dollars from old Carothers' legacy in the Jefferson bank). His motivation is to be "the man in this house" and to command McCaslin/Edmonds respect without servility. His arc here runs from the gold-coin treasure hunt and divorce crisis through his decision to surrender the divining machine. He reappears in Intruder in the Dust.
  • Molly Beauchamp (Aunt Molly): Lucas's wife, the "only mother" Roth Edmonds knew, who delivered him in the flood and nursed black and white children together. Her motivation is to protect the hearth and her children from what she calls "the curse of God" attached to the divining machine; she asks for a divorce to save Lucas and her last child Nat, and finally gets Lucas to give the machine up.
  • Roth (Carothers) Edmonds: the Edmonds who owns the plantation in the 1940s sections, descended from the McCaslins through a distaff line. Motivated by duty, proprietorship, and a choleric sense of being worn down by Lucas. In "Delta Autumn" he repeats the family wrong: an affair with a light-skinned descendant of Tennie's Jim, a child, an envelope of money, "Tell her No."
  • Zack Edmonds: Roth's father; the white man Lucas grew up with almost as a brother and once intended to kill over Molly. The razor confrontation in "The Fire and the Hearth" is the hinge of Lucas's story.
  • Sam Fathers: son of Ikkemotubbe (Doom) and a quadroon slave woman, "Had-Two-Fathers," sold with his mother to Carothers McCaslin. A blacksmith who "farmed no allotted acres," he teaches Ike the woods, the Chickasaw "old tongue," and the ethics of the kill. He dies of "exhaustion — he just quit" after the killing of Old Ben.
  • Boon Hogganbeck: quarter-Chickasaw, child-minded, huge and loyal; the "plebeian" huntsman who nurtures Lion and kills Old Ben with a knife; in the last story's aftermath he guards Sam's body with a gun. He carries the same comic-violent energy into The Reivers.
  • Rider: the sawmill gang-boss of "Pantaloon in Black" who cannot survive Mannie's death; he cuts Birdsong's throat at the crap game and is lynched. His grief is counterpointed by the deputy's racist account.
  • Tomey's Turl: the enslaved son-and-grandson of Carothers McCaslin who repeatedly runs from Uncle Buck and Uncle Buddy to see Tennie; the poker hand that decides his marriage is dealt by his own hands.
  • Uncle Buck (Theophilus) and Uncle Buddy (Amodeus) McCaslin: the twins who freed the family's slaves in title, moved them into the big house, won Tennie from Hubert Beauchamp in 1859, and kept the ledgers whose marginalia expose the founder's acts.
  • Hubert Beauchamp and Miss Sophonsiba: the neighbors of "Was" — Hubert the noisy godfather whose IOU-hoard bequeaths Ike "one silver cup," Sophonsiba the marriage-pursuing mistress of "Warwick."
  • Tennie and Tennie's Jim (James Beauchamp): Tennie, the woman Tomey's Turl chases and marries and the mother of the Beauchamp children; Tennie's Jim, her surviving son, who flees across the Ohio River before his twenty-first birthday and whose granddaughter returns to Ike in "Delta Autumn."
  • Old Ben and Lion: the two-male nonhuman protagonists of "The Bear" — Old Ben, the trap-ruined "head bear" anachronism, and Lion, the blued, gun-barrel-colored dog that holds him so Boon can knife him.
  • Gavin Stevens: Jefferson's county attorney who arranges the return of Samuel Worsham Beauchamp's body; he reappears across Intruder in the Dust and Knight's Gambit as the county's lawyer-interpreter.
  • Mollie Worsham Beauchamp and Samuel Worsham Beauchamp: grandmother and grandson of the title story — he the twenty-six-year-old executed in Joliet for killing a policeman, she the tiny woman who chants "Roth Edmonds sold my Benjamin" and insists the funeral be put in the paper.

Major linkages

  • Intruder in the Dust: Lucas Beauchamp's prison-walk sequel; Molly and the Edmonds place, Gavin Stevens, and the county's readiness to convict a Black man return there. This book supplies Lucas's pre-history.
  • Absalom, Absalom!: the same obsession with a house built on enslaved labor, an unacknowledged black branch, and repudiated inheritance; the Surpen design is the McCaslin design seen from outside. The commissary debate in "The Bear" Part 4 is the book's Absalom argument.
  • Light in August: racial identity, classification, and public violence; Rider's lynching and the young woman's revealed "nigger" status in "Delta Autumn" belong to the same county economy of naming.
  • The Reivers: the same McCaslin-Edmonds-Boon material in comic, late register; Boon Hogganbeck and the idioms of the plantation comedy reappear.
  • Three Famous Short Novels: the novella version of "The Bear," alongside "Spotted Horses" (whose comic commerce pairs with the divining-machine salesman) and "Old Man."
  • Big Woods: re-gathers "The Old People" and the Bear material with later hunting stories; the same wilderness elegy.
  • The Essential Faulkner: the anthology hub that gathers the corpus; positions this book among Faulkner's other novels and collections.
  • Coercive Labor: slavery, the manumitted "free" still bound by ledgers, sharecropping, the sawmill, and the cash economy of bodies at the poker table.
  • Moral Economy: the ledgers as moral accounting; Ike's renunciation; Molly's "curse"; the county's funeral economy in the title story.
  • Legibility: the ledger records, the census questionnaire, the newspaper notice, the courthouse — and what they render readable or unreadable about people.
  • Informal Institutions: the poker table, the hearth, the hunting camp's unwritten rules, and the county's informal fund-raising.
  • Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation: Sam Fathers' oral transmission and the ledger-archive; Ike inherits both.
  • Ecological Constraint: the retreating wilderness, the ditched and leveed Delta, and a two-hundred-mile drive to reach game.
  • Language and Ideology: "Warwick," "sold my Benjamin," Ike's Book-reading, the names on the ledger pages.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: who inherits the land and by what right; the distaff Edmonds line vs. the male McCaslin line vs. the Beauchamp black line.
  • Identity, Culture, and Social Formation and State, Order, and Legibility as the two MoCs the book most feeds.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Land and inheritance: the Indian patent, the "curse" on the land, Ike's renunciation, Lucas's continued occupancy, timber sale.
  • Blood, race, and the name: McCaslin / Edmonds / Beauchamp, "the one that had no name but Terrel," Lucas self-naming, the returned blood in "Delta Autumn."
  • Slavery's afterlife: manumission in title, the ledgers that keep binding, the poker-table pricing of people, the "Egypt"/"Pharaoh" lament of the title story.
  • The wilderness as teacher: Sam Fathers' ethics, "shoot quick and slow," the bear's "rules," the destruction of the Big Bottom.
  • Legibility and erasure: who is recorded, and how; the courthouse, census form, newspaper, and deputy's monologue as rival institutions of record.
  • Grief and the official explanation: Rider's mourning vs. the deputy's racism; Mollie's funeral vs. the county's case file.
  • Loyalty, humility, and pride: the hunter's code, Ike's bearing, Lucas's imperviousness, Boon's faithfulness.

Core concepts

  • Coercive Labor: the book is the vault's richest single record of American slavery as a property regime — the poker table valuing Tomey's Turl and Tennie at three hundred dollars, the ledgers listing "Eunice Bought by Father in New Orleans 1807 $650," and the manumission that still leaves Thucydus charging his keep against a wage he cannot verify.
  • Moral Economy: Ike's renunciation is a moral-economy gesture — refusing a title whose "amortization" can never be completed; Lucas's hoarding and Molly's "curse" are the household-level version of the same reckoning.
  • Legibility: the ledgers and the census questionnaire make people readable as property and as a case; the deputy's monologue makes Rider unreadable except as "not human"; Mollie forces the newspaper to make grief legible.
  • Informal Institutions: the hunting camp's unwritten rules, the poker settlement of "Was," the county's word-of-mouth fund-raising for the funeral, and Molly's hearth-centered authority all outrun formal law.
  • Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation: Sam Fathers' "old tongue" and woodland teaching, the family chronicle Ike inherits by listening, and the ledgers that outlive their owners ("Lucas would outlive the ledgers").
  • Ecological Constraint: Old Ben as "anachronism out of an old dead time"; the Delta's ditching, leveeing, and mechanized cotton; wilderness retreating from thirty to two hundred miles from Jefferson.
  • Language and Ideology: rival vocabularies — Sophonsiba's "Warwick," the ledger's misspellings, Doom's renaming, Lucas's self-composed name, and Mollie's biblical chant — carry the book's ideological contest.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: the question of who can hold the land — the distaff Edmonds line, the repudiating male heir, the Black Beauchamp line — is the book's central succession problem.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Front matter

Summary: The extract opens with a list of William Faulkner's Works (1924–1979), the Vintage International title page and copyright (Copyright 1940, 1941, 1942 by William Faulkner; renewed by Estelle Faulkner and Jill Faulkner Summers; originally published by Random House in 1942; stories first appeared in The Atlantic Monthly, Harper's, The Saturday Evening Post, and Story), the dedication "TO MAMMY … CAROLINE BARR / Mississippi [1840–1940] / Who was born in slavery and who gave to my family a fidelity without stint or calculation of recompense and to my childhood an immeasurable devotion and love," and a table of contents listing exactly seven pieces: Was; The Fire and the Hearth; Pantaloon in Black; The Old People; The Bear; Delta Autumn; Go Down, Moses.

Analysis: The dedication to Caroline Barr binds the whole book to a woman born in slavery, "to my family" — a white man's household — and makes the volume a return of that debt, which the stories then dramatize across the McCaslin and Beauchamp lines. The double provenance (1942 Random House original; the components as magazine stories) explains why the book reads both as a novel and as a sequence of self-standing stories, a structure the reader should track through coercive labor and the politics of giving and obligation in culture, identity, and legitimacy.

Source anchors: Caroline Barr; Mammy 1840–1940; Vintage 1990; Random House 1942; Contents; Atlantic Monthly; Harper's; Saturday Evening Post; Story.

Was

Summary: "Was" is told through old Isaac McCaslin's inherited memory of McCaslin Edmonds's childhood visit with Uncle Buck to Hubert Beauchamp's so-called Warwick plantation after Tomey's Turl runs away again to see Tennie. The story turns the pursuit into a comic fox hunt with Black John, old Jake, the dogs, a fyce, a fox-horn, Miss Sophonsiba's perfume and roan tooth, and Uncle Buck's fatal mistake of entering the wrong bedroom. Hubert Beauchamp uses that bedroom scandal and a five-hundred-dollar wager to force a poker settlement, while Uncle Buddy arrives in a wagon and lets Tomey's Turl deal the stud hand that saves Buck from marriage without dowry. The result is that Uncle Buddy wins the arrangement that allows Tennie and Tomey's Turl to be joined, with enslaved people valued at three hundred dollars inside the same game that treats Sophonsiba's marriage as a stake. Tomey's Turl outruns, outwits, and finally knocks Uncle Buck down ("Look out of here, old Buck"), and Cassidy's report to Uncle Buddy ends with the fox racing through the house and Uncle Buddy kicking the crate off old Moses' head.

Analysis: Uncle Buck and Uncle Buddy make slavery look temporarily comic because they are outmaneuvered by Tomey's Turl, Tennie, Hubert Beauchamp, and Miss Sophonsiba, but the poker table keeps the comedy inside coercive labor as a property regime. Tomey's Turl deals the cards that determine his own future with Tennie, which makes his agency real and still constrained by the three-hundred-dollar price Hubert assigns him; Uncle Buddy's wager bends the same informal economy to let the pair be joined. Warwick and the bedroom farce introduce the book's recurring method: family anecdote preserves jokes while also preserving the fact that marriage, slavery, land, and inheritance were bargained together — the informal institutions of kinship and wager doing what no court would do. The comic fray connects forward to Faulkner's late comedy about the same McCaslin-Edmonds plantation machinery in The Reivers.

Source anchors: Uncle Buck; Uncle Buddy; Tomey's Turl; Tennie; Hubert Beauchamp; Miss Sophonsiba; Warwick; poker; five hundred dollars; three hundred dollars; Black John; old Jake; fyce.

The Fire and the Hearth — Chapter One

Summary: Lucas Beauchamp begins by trying to hide his illegal still because George Wilkins, who wants to marry Nat, has started making bad whisky and may bring deputies onto the Edmonds place. At the Indian mound in the creek bottom, Lucas buries the copper-lined kettle and worm, triggers a cave-in, finds a single gold coin, and becomes convinced that Buck and Buddy McCaslin left buried money there. Nat sees him at the mound, George is spared prison because Lucas now needs quiet for treasure hunting, and Roth Edmonds becomes entangled in Lucas's authority over Molly, Nat, George, and the old land. The chapter also contains the book's central flashback: the flood spring forty-three years earlier, when wide-swollen rivers forced Lucas to fetch the doctor by water, Molly delivered the white child Roth in the big house, and the white wife died; Molly then stayed six months, until Lucas confronted Zack Edmonds and, one dawn, stood over the sleeping white man with an open razor before throwing it out the window and demanding the pistol. Lucas let the two live cartridges fail to fire and walked away, "impervious to anybody." The chapter closes with the courthouse scenes: Lucas reports George's still, both stills are found, and in the federal courthouse Lucas produces the marriage license of George and Nat dated the previous October, which breaks the case; the judge clears his office, and Lucas gives George fifty dollars for a well, a stove, and a porch — money George spends on a better still instead.

Analysis: Lucas Beauchamp's pride rests on being both a Beauchamp in public law and a McCaslin by blood, so the Indian mound and gold coin tempt him with a buried inheritance that seems older than Roth Edmonds's control. George Wilkins and Nat threaten Lucas's household order, while the razor scene with Zack Edmonds shows the same pride at the edge of lethal violence: Lucas claims that he is "a nigger but a man too" and that "the same thing made my pappy that made your grandmaw," and he settles the feud not with the razor but by making Zack acknowledge him as a man. The flashback belongs to the same lineage-reckoning as Absalom, Absalom!, and Lucas's later legal exposure is the direct pre-history of Intruder in the Dust. The courthouse and the marriage license, meanwhile, show formal legibility arriving late while informal institutions — the hearth, the kinship claim, the poker-style settlement — decide the household's real order; the coin itself turns on Lucas the temptation that moral economy calls "rendered to God's earth."

Source anchors: Lucas Beauchamp; Indian mound; gold coin; cave-in; Nat; George Wilkins; Zack Edmonds; Molly; razor; pistol cartridges; courthouse; marriage license; Judge Gowan; fifty dollars.

The Fire and the Hearth — Chapter Two

Summary: A Memphis "drummer" salesman appears with a divining machine (an "oblong metal box" with knobs and dials ordered from St. Louis) and sells Lucas a share in buried treasure. Lucas, refused the three hundred dollars by Edmonds, swaps his three-year-old mare-mule Alice Ben Bolt for the machine with a written bill of sale. Edmonds, discovering the mule missing, tracks Lucas and George to the Indian mound, and then the salesman claims the mule; Lucas, after a third night of searching, tells the salesman the money is buried in two places and that he has "a letter" giving the location. In the old orchard on the hill the machine finds a can of silver dollars, the salesman tries to keep it all, and Lucas holds him to a bargain: half of anything further found, the mule-paper back, and the machine's bill of sale transferred to Lucas. Lucas then returns the mule to Edmonds's stable and reports that the salesman "rents" the machine from him at twenty-five dollars a night before leaving the country. Edmonds, enraged by the story of "hunting buried money," orders Lucas out of the commissary for good.

Analysis: The machine is a grotesque instrument of legibility — a metal box that promises to make buried value readable — and Lucas defeats its white salesman by playing the salesman's own contract games better than the salesman, coming away with title to "a three-hundred-and-twenty-five-dollar talking box." The episode is the book's purest comic-commerce interlude, the same register Faulkner works in "Spotted Horses" within Three Famous Short Novels: a salesman, a bill of sale, a swindle that turns on who controls the paper. At stake beneath the comedy is Lucas's studied independence — his refusal to be a "nigger" in the commissary ledger even as he keeps drawing supplies — which links the local economic order to broader political economy and the informal norms that govern it.

Source anchors: divining machine; Memphis salesman; Alice Ben Bolt; bill of sale; three hundred dollars; twenty-five dollars a night; buried money; silver dollars; orchard; Edmonds's stable.

The Fire and the Hearth — Chapter Three

Summary: Molly walks to the commissary for the first time in years to demand a divorce, telling Edmonds she cannot live with Lucas because "the machine" has made him "crazy" and she is "afraid he's going to find it" — the gold being "rendered" to God's earth. Edmonds, recalling his own childhood with Molly and her son Henry as his foster-brother (and the shame-and-grief episode in which he refused at seven to share a bed), presses Lucas to give up the machine. Lucas at first offers it to George; Molly cries out that giving it to George would carry the curse onto Nat. The next day Molly disappears into the creek bottom with the machine and is found half-drowned, the handle still in her hand. In cold weather Lucas and Molly ride to the Chancellor's office in Jefferson, but when the divorce is about to be granted Lucas interrupts: "We dont want no voce. I done changed my mind," and the bill is struck off the docket. Lucas buys Molly a nickel's worth of candy, and that night he sets the polished divining machine on Edmonds's table: "Get rid of it." He tells Edmonds he is "near to the end of my three score and ten," and the money "aint for me."

Analysis: Molly's religious dread gives the chapter its moral weight: she names the machine a curse and the buried money property of God, and she is willing to break a forty-five-year marriage to save the household. Lucas's surrender is a conversion within moral economy — he finally treats gold found in the earth as a debt he is too late to claim — while the divorce-court farce shows him maneuvering around the state's legibility machinery the way he handled the salesman. The chapter is also the fullest portrait of the Edmonds-Molly household bond and of Roth's inheritance of guilt ("Even a nigger McCaslin is a better man … than all of us"), which carries directly into Lucas's later arrest in Intruder in the Dust. Molly's insistence that her hearth, not the plantation office, is the true site of authority models an informal institution that out-orders the formal one.

Source anchors: Molly; divorce; voce; curse; gold; Creek bottom; Chancellor; "three score and ten"; get rid of it; Edmonds's pickle; Nat.

Pantaloon in Black

Summary: Rider buries Mannie in a graveyard marked by pottery, broken bottles, and brick fragments that white observers cannot read, then returns alone to the cabin he rented from Carothers Edmonds and rebuilt with Mannie during their six months of marriage. He sees Mannie in the kitchen door, tries to feed himself and the dog from the cold food left by his aunt, walks all night, appears at the sawmill before dawn, eats the fireman's food, rides a log down the skidway, and leaves after lifting a log no one expects him to move. He buys a gallon of raw whisky from a white bootlegger, visits his aunt, refuses religious consolation, enters the mill tool-room crap game, catches Birdsong using crooked dice, and cuts his throat with the razor hidden on a string beneath his shirt. The deputy later tells his wife that Rider was inhumanly strange, recounting the arrest, the jail cell, the torn-out cot, the steel door ripped from the wall, the Beatles of the Birdsong men, the lynching at the negro schoolhouse, and the coroner's verdict of persons unknown — while his wife, fixed on a disputed rook-prize, hardly listens.

Analysis: Rider's grief for Mannie is shown through the grave, the cabin, the sawmill, and the dog before the deputy's racist account tries to explain it away. Birdsong and the crooked dice give Rider a concrete target for rage, but the razor stroke follows a day in which food, work, whisky, and prayer have all failed to make Mannie's absence survivable; the lynching and the "persons unknown" verdict make legibility lethal by converting a bereaved husband into a public danger whose death requires no named killer. The deputy's kitchen monologue is the book's stark exhibit of official narrative against lived witness — the same structure of broken official account that runs through testimony against official record — and Rider's reduction to "a damn herd of wild buffaloes" is the county logic that Light in August dramatizes in Joe Christmas. The story's sawmill world also belongs to the regular-labor economy of coercive labor that the McCaslin stories show in the countryside.

Source anchors: Rider; Mannie; dog; sawmill; skidway; whisky; bootlegger; aunt; Birdsong; crooked dice; razor; tool-room; negro schoolhouse; bell-rope; coroner; deputy; rook-prize.

The Old People

Summary: "The Old People" recounts Ike's initiation into hunting under Sam Fathers, beginning with the November dawn when Sam tells the twelve-year-old to "shoot quick, and slow," then marks his face with the buck's hot blood. The hunting party includes Tennie's Jim, Boon Hogganbeck, Walter Ewell, Major de Spain, General Compson, and McCaslin Edmonds, but Sam's authority comes from another history: Ikkemotubbe, called Doom, the Chevalier Soeur-Blonde de Vitry, a quadroon slave woman, Moketubbe, and Carothers McCaslin's purchase of Sam and his mother. Sam teaches Ike the woods, leaves the plantation after Jobaker's death to live at the Big Bottom camp, and on a later hunt stands beside the boy when a huge buck passes within twenty feet. Sam raises his hand, palm outward, and calls the buck "Oleh, Chief … Grandfather" in the old tongue, while Walter Ewell's horn announces the killing of a smaller spike buck whose track overlays the larger animal's path. That night before the fire McCaslin and Ike discuss the dead who inhabit the earth.

Analysis: Sam Fathers gives Ike a form of kinship that bypasses McCaslin property documents, and the hot blood on Ike's face makes hunting an ethical apprenticeship rather than sport alone. Ikkemotubbe, Doom's poisoned puppies, and Jobaker place Sam inside Chickasaw loss, slavery, and solitude, so the Big Bottom becomes memory, narrative, and knowledge preservation that the plantation cannot own — an oral archive of the "old tongue" and the People that Sam transmits to Ike. The Indian patent and Carothers' purchase tie this story directly to the frontier-dispossession theme of empire, frontier, and periphery and to the doubled identity of a man who is both royal and enslaved, which is the book's deepest culture, identity, and legitimacy problem. Walter Ewell's spike buck and Sam's "Grandfather" show Ike two kinds of seeing: one kills what is available, while the other recognizes a presence that should not be reduced to a trophy. The story's material is re-gathered in Big Woods.

Source anchors: Sam Fathers; Ike; hot blood; buck; Ikkemotubbe; Doom; Moketubbe; Chevalier Soeur-Blonde de Vitry; Jobaker; Joe Baker; Big Bottom; Grandfather; spike buck; Walter Ewell; General Compson; McCaslin Edmonds; Major de Spain; Boon Hogganbeck; Tennie's Jim.

The Bear — Part 1

Summary: "The Bear" opens with Ike at sixteen and the long legend of Old Ben, the trap-ruined "head bear" of a hundred-square-mile range, an anachronism "out of an old dead time." Ike's novitiate began at ten, when his cousin McCaslin first brought him to the Big Bottom camp; Sam Fathers placed him on the poorest stand, and on the third morning the dogs ran a deer, then the abject, high-pitched cry that meant Old Ben. Sam showed Ike the rotted log with claw-marks and the enormous warped two-toed print, teaching him that "we aint got the dog yet." To see the bear, Ike learns he must first relinquish the gun, then the compass and watch; he leaves them on a bush in the markless woods, gets lost, follows the filling prints to a glade, and at last sees the bear. Sam's coda draws the moral lesson about fear: "Be scared. You cant help that. But dont be afraid."

Analysis: This part sets up the whole "Bear" dialectic: the wilderness is older than any recorded deed — "bigger and older than any recorded document," older than Sutpen, older than Ikkemotubbe — and Ike's education is a graduated series of relinquishments, a form of knowledge preservation that insists value is held by humility, not title. The trap-ruined foot and the anachronism theme make Old Ben an emblem of ecological constraint against which plow and axe are "a puny gnawing." The same wilderness apprenticeship is what Big Woods and ''The Bear'' novella in Three Famous Short Novels re-gather, and the opening genealogy of the land (de Spain from Sutpen from Ikkemotubbe) re-asserts the succession question under which the whole book operates.

Source anchors: Old Ben; two-toed foot; trap; Big Bottom; Sam Fathers; the stand; compass and watch; relinquishment; the fyce; be scared/not afraid; Major de Spain; Sutpen; Ikkemotubbe.

The Bear — Part 2

Summary: Over the next years Ike becomes a better woodsman than most men and knows the old bear's footprint "better than he did his own"; twice he sees the bear, once ambushing it with the tiny fyce at a cypress, and neither he nor Sam shoots. When a horse-colt is found dead with its throat torn out beside the frantic mare, Major de Spain judges that "it was Old Ben. He has broken the rules," and Sam, who knows better, says nothing. In the corn-crib Sam traps the "blue dog" — a blued, gun-barrel-colored mongrel of mastiff and Airedale and more, weighing almost ninety pounds — and starves and feeds it until it accepts him, wanting "not tame but … not scared"; they name it Lion. Boon takes over the feeding and sleeps with Lion in his airless room, and the great dog leads the pack. On the last day of the November hunt, with swampers and townsmen crowded in to watch, Lion holds Old Ben, General Compson draws blood with both barrels, and Boon, riding the one-eyed mule, misses five times from twenty-five feet: "I missed him. I was in twenty-five feet of him and I missed him five times."

Analysis: The colt-killing is the book's turning point because Old Ben "broke the rules," transferring the informal institutional code of the hunt into a proprietary outrage. Sam's refusal to speak, and his foreknowledge, mark him as the one who recognizes Lion as "the dog" the camp has been waiting for. The Lion story is also a study of mastery within the camp's own order: Boon, "a slave to all the appetites," tends a creature that "cared about no man and no thing," while Sam's training methods — starvation and the pail of water — work on the animal's instinct, not its fear. The ecological balance that the bear's "rules" maintained is the same ecological constraint the lumber economy will destroy; the material is the heart of The Bear in the vault's novella collection.

Source anchors: Lion; colt; "He has broken the rules"; Sam's foreknowledge; corn-crib; starving; blue dog; blued gun-barrel color; Boon; one-eyed mule; swampers; General Compson's shots; "I missed him five times"; Katie.

The Bear — Part 3

Summary: In the coldest December they remember, Boon and Ike are sent to Memphis for whisky (with a note to Mr Semmes the distiller), and exercise of the comic Boon — the dollar borrow, the saloons, the zoo plan to match Lion against "bears fed on ice cream and lady fingers," the returned-at-sundown empty bottle. Back at camp, on the morning of the last day, dozens of onlookers gather to watch. General Compson insists Ike ride Katie; the run crosses the river, and at the bank the bear turns; Lion drives in, and Boon flings himself astride the bear and works in the knife — "working and probing the buried blade." Old Ben falls "all of a piece," with fifty-two old bullets in its hide and one almost invisible slit under the left shoulder. Sam Fathers is found lying in the trampled mud with no mark, paralyzed; Lion, disemboweled, is sewed up without chloroform and dies at sundown, buried with General Compson speaking "as he would have spoken over a man." Sam asks to be let out at his hut and dies — the doctor: "He's all right … He just quit." Boon refuses to give up Sam's body and stands guard at the fresh grave holding a gun, until McCaslin takes the gun from him: "Did you kill him, Boon?" "No!" And Ike, in tears, breaks between them: "Leave him alone!"

Analysis: Old Ben, Lion, and Sam Fathers die in a single linked ending: the bear can only be killed when the dog and the old teacher are also lost, so Ike's hunting education reaches completion exactly as the world that taught him begins to disappear. The deaths are consecrated by the camp's own liturgy — the grave-side oration, the buried bodies, the rites of unnamed men speaking over a dog as over a man — an informal institution of grief that the deputy's monologue in "Pantaloon in Black" will invert. Boon's gun-at-the-grave stand and Ike's "Leave him alone!" show the camp's loyalty code failing under the strain of loss. The sequence is the emotional heart shared with Big Woods and the novella in Three Famous Short Novels, and the December run is Faulkner's fullest picture of the wilderness as moral economy — a contest with rules, not a harvest.

Source anchors: December; Memphis; Mr Semmes; zoo; Katie; river crossing; Lion holds; Boon's knife; fifty-two bullets; slit under the shoulder; disembowelment; Sam "quit"; General Compson's oration; grave; axel-grease tins; "Leave him alone."

The Bear — Part 4

Summary: At twenty-one, in the commissary with McCaslin, Ike reads the ledgers and renounces the McCaslin inheritance. The section is the book's great interior debate: McCaslin argues the land was validly taken ("Bought it, got it, no matter; kept it, held it, no matter"), while Ike answers that "on the instant when Ikkemotubbe discovered, realised, that he could sell it for money, on that instant it ceased ever to have been his," and that man holds the earth "in the communal anonymity of brotherhood," not in fee. The ledgers themselves surface the record: Roscus, Fibby, Thucydus (paid off, set up blacksmith), and Eunice, "Bought by Father in New Orleans 1807 $650," "Drownd in Crick Cristmas Day 1832," with Uncle Buddy's laconic "Drownd herself" and Uncle Buck's "Who in hell ever heard of a niger drownding him self" — followed by Tomasina ("Tomey"), who "dide in Child bed June 1833," and Turl, "born Jun 1833 yr stars fell Fathers will." Ike realizes that Tomey was Carothers' own enslaved daughter and Turl both son and grandson, and that Eunice drowned herself rather than live with the knowledge. The section also follows the fates of Turl and Tennie's children: Amodeus McCaslin Beauchamp (died in infancy), two unnamed children, James Thucydus Beauchamp (Tennie's Jim, fled across the Ohio River), the daughter Fonsiba (married and moved to Arkansas, where Ike followed her to a falling cabin and an Arkansas "farm" with a pension-drawing husband), and Lucas (born 1874), who on his twenty-first birthday demanded his third of the old Carothers legacy. Ike, meanwhile, surrendered his inheritance to McCaslin for a fifty-dollar-a-month pension, kept the "legacy" from Uncle Hubert — whose shrinking IOU-hoard ends in "One silver cup. Hubert Beauchamp" — borrowed the banknotes "as a loan," and married; his wife, asking him to move to the farm, meets his refusal: "I cant. Not ever." The debate closes with Ike's claim that Sam Fathers set him free, and McCaslin's "So you will be free.—No, not now nor ever, we from them nor they from us."

Analysis: The commissary ledgers are the book's supreme instance of legibility: a property archive in which people are recorded as purchases, births, deaths, and cash bequests, and whose missing entries — Eunice's motive, Tomey's paternity — are exactly what the archive works to hide. Ike's renunciation is a moral economy judgment: documented incest, slavery, and sale make legal inheritance an unpaid debt no will can amortize, and his argument with McCaslin is the South's succession problem argued as legitimacy and succession — distaff Edmonds against repudiating male heir against the Black Beauchamp line. Lucas's refusal to be the obedient donee ("selfprogenitive and nominate, by himself ancestored") seeds his later role in Intruder in the Dust, while the whole ''curse upon the land'' argument is the McCaslin version of what Absalom, Absalom! builds around Sutpen's Hundred, and the racial-identity machinery of the county is what Light in August takes to its violent end. The ledger entries also carry the book's language and ideology — misspelled, marginal, self-canceling human records that outlast the planters who kept them, preserved in memory, narrative, and knowledge preservation.

Source anchors: commissary; ledgers; manumission; Eunice; Thucydus; Tomasina/Tomey; Turl; Fathers will; thousand-dollar legacy; Roscus; Fibby; Percival Brownlee; Spintrius; James/Tennie's Jim; Fonsiba; Arkansas; Lucas; twenty-first birthday; IOU cup; "One silver cup"; Hubert Beauchamp; fifty-dollar pension; "I cant. Not ever"; "Sam Fathers set me free"; "we from them nor they from us."

The Bear — Part 5

Summary: Years later, after Major de Spain sells the timber rights to a Memphis lumber company, Ike returns to the old camp one last time before the cutting. Hoke's has become a log-line junction of planing-mills, piled rails, and mule-tents; Boon is now town-marshal of Hoke's. Riding in with Ash's wagon, Ike finds the four concrete surveyor's markers of the reserved plot, the vanished graves of Lion and Sam (he steps over Sam's), and the axle-grease tin containing Old Ben's dried mutilated paw resting above Lion's bones; he leaves tobacco, a bandanna, and peppermint candy. Then, near the Gum Tree clearing, he comes upon Boon seated against the trunk, hammering the breech of his dismembered gun barrel against the butt, surrounded by a tree swarming with frantic squirrels — "Get out of here! Dont touch them! Dont touch a one of them! They're mine!" The section gathers, through memory, the camp years: Ash's comic insistence on shooting a deer, the use of a gun allotted to Boon for the day, and the year-by-year sequence of summer, fall, snow, and spring that shaped Ike.

Analysis: The final camp visit closes the wilderness against its industrial replacement: the lumber company's marks and cuts have already converted Old Ben's country into commodity systems, and the graves with no headstones are found, as Sam taught, "by bearings on trees." Ike's offering of tobacco and candy is a restrained act of knowledge preservation — a ritual that keeps the dead "free in earth and … of earth, myriad yet undiffused." Boon's furious hammering under the gum tree, with the squirrels he no longer has the discipline to shoot singly, is the grotesque emblem of a hunter's code returned to mere possession, the end of the ecological constraint that had once supplied the camp's order. Sam's bones and Lion's, the paw in the tin, and the "corporate" club plan all tie the section to the wider Bear cycle in Big Woods and to the novella in Three Famous Short Novels, while Boon's opposite fate as Hoke's marshal looks forward to his role in The Reivers.

Source anchors: lumber company; Hoke's; planing-mill; surveyor's markers; graves; axle-grease tin; Old Ben's paw; Lion's bones; tobacco; bandanna; peppermint candy; Gum Tree; squirrels; Boon's broken gun; "They're mine"; Ash; town-marshal.

Delta Autumn

Summary: "Delta Autumn" returns to Ike as Uncle Ike, near eighty, riding in cars instead of wagons to a shrinking Delta wilderness with Roth Edmonds, Will Legate, Henry Wyatt, Isham, and younger hunters. The story contrasts the old Big Bottom hunts with roads, levees, neon, locomotives, cotton, drainage, mechanized agriculture, and the fact that wilderness now lies two hundred miles from Jefferson instead of thirty. Roth is tense because a young woman from Aluschaskuna has followed him with their baby after a week in camp the previous fall and six weeks in New Mexico, and he leaves Ike an envelope of money with instructions to tell her no. When she arrives by skiff, Ike learns that she is descended from Tennie's Jim, recognizes the Beauchamp-McCaslin blood returning, gives her General Compson's horn for the child, and tells her to go North and marry a Black man — at which point she asks him "have you lived so long and forgotten so much that you dont remember anything you ever knew or felt or even heard about love?" She leaves; Legate comes for a knife because Roth has killed a deer; and Ike, alone, says "It was a doe."

Analysis: Uncle Ike's authority in the Delta is weakened by age and by the fact that Roth Edmonds repeats the family wrong with a woman descended from Tennie's Jim. The young schoolteacher from Aluschaskuna restages the old line's history — a white Edmonds, a light-skinned descendant, secret money, a child, unacknowledged — so that the repudiated Beauchamp blood of Absalom, Absalom! returns to confront Ike in the flesh; and Ike, who renounced the inheritance, cannot renounce the category, stammering "You're a nigger!" and then "marry a black man," the very classification machine the book has been destroying. That refusal of love is the racial identity trap central to Light in August. The roads, levees, drainage, neon, and killed doe make ecological constraint personal: Roth's sexual abandonment occurs inside the same diminished landscape Ike can no longer protect, and the Delta's cotton-and-mortgage economy is path dependence and institutional drift made visible in a generation. Ike's closing meditation — "This land which man has deswamped and denuded and derivered in two generations" — and his admission that "the ruined woods I used to know dont cry for retribution" because "the people who have destroyed it will accomplish its revenge" tie the human and ecological costs into one judgment.

Source anchors: Uncle Ike; Roth Edmonds; Will Legate; Henry Wyatt; Isham; Delta; levee; two hundred miles; Aluschaskuna; New Mexico; envelope; money; skiff; Tennie's Jim; General Compson's horn; "You're a nigger"; "love"; "It was a doe"; the ruined woods.

Go Down, Moses

Summary: The title story begins in an Illinois death cell, where Samuel Worsham Beauchamp, twenty-six, dressed in expensive fawn-colored clothes and known under an alias in Chicago, completes a census questionnaire before execution for killing a policeman: "No. It was another guy killed the cop"; occupation "Getting rich too fast — none." In Jefferson, his grandmother Mollie Beauchamp comes to Gavin Stevens, chanting that Roth Edmonds sold her Benjamin in Egypt and that Pharaoh has him. Stevens identifies Butch Beauchamp from old Jefferson jail records, learns from a Joliet dispatch that Samuel will die that night, and works with Miss Worsham, Hamp Worsham, the newspaper editor, an undertaker, Carothers Edmonds, and donors around the square to bring the body home. Mollie insists on a casket, flowers, a hearse, a car ride through town, and a newspaper notice, while Stevens and the editor follow the funeral procession toward the Edmonds place and realize that she wants all of it recorded. The editor reports her words at the station: "Is you gonter put hit in de paper? I wants hit all in de paper. All of hit."

Analysis: Samuel Worsham Beauchamp appears first as a condemned man in Joliet, but Mollie Beauchamp's language turns him back into Benjamin, kin stolen into Egypt by Roth Edmonds and Pharaoh — an Old-Testament account that outflanks the county's law-and-order narrative of a "bad son of a bad father." Gavin Stevens and Miss Worsham translate that grief into telephones, money, a casket, flowers, and a hearse, making county institutions serve a mourning they barely understand; the fund-raising around the square and the procession past the Confederate monument are informal institutions doing the formal ones' work. The census questionnaire that exposed the alias and the newspaper notice Mollie demands show legibility cutting both ways: the record that convicted also finally claims the body. Against the official file, Mollie's chant is a counter-narrative of the kind testimony against the official record gathers, and the story's legal machinery directly anticipates Intruder in the Dust's Gavin-Stevens-cases. The "sold my Benjamin" refrain makes the whole book's coercive labor history resurface as mourning: the county must be shown the body through knowledge preservation so the death is not quietly filed away.
