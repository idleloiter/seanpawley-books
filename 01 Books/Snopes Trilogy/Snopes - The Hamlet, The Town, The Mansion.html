Detailed overview

The Modern Library omnibus presents the Snopes material as Faulkner wanted it read: not as three detached novels but as one long Yoknapatawpha chronicle moving from Frenchman’s Bend to Jefferson and ending in the late 1940s. George Garrett’s introduction names Lafayette County, Mississippi, as the real ground behind Yoknapatawpha County, but it stresses that Faulkner’s county has its own myths, legends, histories, and populations. The trilogy is therefore both local and world-sized: Old Frenchman’s place, Varner’s store, Jefferson’s Square, the power plant, the bank, the De Spain house, and Parchman become stations in a history of how acquisitive modernity enters a poor rural society and learns to wear respectability.

The first large movement, The Hamlet, begins in Frenchman’s Bend with a ruined pre-Civil War plantation and Will Varner’s power over land, mortgages, the store, the cotton gin, the grist mill, and local law. Into that already predatory order comes Ab Snopes, followed by Flem Snopes, V. K. Ratliff’s alert commentary, Jody Varner’s attempted barn-burning leverage, and the story of Major de Spain’s rug and burned barn. Faulkner does not make the Snopes arrival a simple invasion of innocence. Varner already bleeds mules and stuffs ballot boxes, Ratliff can be fooled by buried silver dollars, Henry Armstid can be consumed by greed, and the Old Frenchman legend turns the whole community into spectators of its own appetite.

The second movement, The Town, shifts the Snopes rise into Jefferson and changes the telling. Charles Mallison, Gavin Stevens, and Ratliff make the town’s knowledge partial, retrospective, comic, and self-correcting. Flem’s tent behind the restaurant, the water tank, Grover Cleveland Winbush’s café, Manfred de Spain’s automobile politics, the power-plant superintendency, the missing brass fittings, Montgomery Ward Snopes’s French postcard club, Eula Varner Snopes’s monument, and Linda’s departure to New York all show Flem learning that money alone is not enough. He must protect reputation, office, church standing, and bank power, which makes him more dangerous because Jefferson now shares an interest in hiding the very things it claims to condemn.

The third movement, The Mansion, returns to the long debts created by the earlier books. Faulkner’s author’s note openly accepts discrepancies because the “essential truth” of the people matters more than strict factual consistency. Mink Snopes’s murder of Jack Houston, his expectation that Flem will rescue him, his life sentence, his time in Parchman, and his eventual return make kinship and vengeance the trilogy’s delayed reckoning. Linda Snopes Kohl, deafened and politically marked by the Spanish Civil War and Communism, adds another line of pressure: she inherits Eula’s beauty and loss but acts through labor, political conviction, and strategic release rather than through mythic sexual presence.

The omnibus is also a study of storytelling itself. Garrett emphasizes that The Hamlet uses a collective omniscient voice and Ratliff-like local memory, The Town is narrated by Charles Mallison, Gavin Stevens, and Ratliff, and The Mansion brings back third-person narration alongside those monologists. That design matters because the Snopeses are rarely understood directly. Flem and Eula remain especially opaque; people read them through gestures, deals, silences, clothes, offices, rumors, and consequences. The trilogy’s comedy depends on delayed recognition, while its tragedy depends on the fact that characters often understand too late what their own desire has made possible.

Faulkner’s specific moral pressure falls on equality of inward life. Garrett’s introduction points to Ratliff’s blue shirt, Stevens’s corncob pipe, Flem’s bow tie, Goodyhay’s prayer for “the poor sons of bitches,” and Mink’s final vision of himself among the dead as “equal to any.” That is the deepest answer to Flem’s cold acquisitiveness. The trilogy does not deny foolishness, greed, lust, cruelty, racial hierarchy, or class humiliation; it insists that even Ike Snopes, Henry Armstid, Tom Tom, Tomey’s Turl, Montgomery Ward, Byron’s shipped children, Linda, Eula, Mink, and Flem himself must be rendered as people whose actions have weight inside a shared moral world.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Commercial Society: the trilogy follows how stores, mortgages, cotton gins, buried-money rumors, auctions, bank offices, and power-plant jobs turn social appetite into local power.
  • Elite Formation: Flem Snopes rises from Frenchman's Bend outsider to Jefferson banker and church-respectable figure by capturing offices, reputations, secrets, and institutional blind spots rather than by inheriting status.
  • Informal Institutions: Ratliff's commentary, Varner's store, courthouse expectations, town gossip, kin obligation, and Jefferson respectability govern behavior as much as formal law.
  • Moral Economy: Mink's grievance, Mrs. Armstid's five dollars, Eula's public monument, Linda's release of Mink, and Goodyhay's prayer all press against Flem's purely acquisitive logic.
  • Path Dependence and Institutional Drift: the trilogy keeps showing earlier frauds, kin debts, sexual bargains, and institutional evasions returning decades later as revenge, reputation management, or inheritance.
  • Language and Ideology: Garrett's introduction and the trilogy's changing narrators make local speech, retrospective correction, and comic storytelling part of how Jefferson explains and excuses the Snopes rise.

Series context

This omnibus sits inside Snopes Trilogy and gathers The Hamlet, The Town, and The Mansion into one continuous Yoknapatawpha sequence.

Section-by-section notes

Introduction

Summary: George Garrett’s introduction places the trilogy at the center of Faulkner’s Yoknapatawpha work, naming Lafayette County, Mississippi, as the source transformed into Yoknapatawpha County’s independent myths, legends, places, and populations. Garrett identifies The Hamlet (1940), The Town (1957), and The Mansion (1959) as one continuous narrative about the Snopes family from the Civil War’s aftermath to 1948, while noting Faulkner’s work with Saxe Commins and Albert Erskine to reconcile the books. He quotes Faulkner’s letter to Erskine that “the essential truth” matters more than facts, describes the automobile replacing mule and wagon, Memphis airport replacing the railroad as the outside link, and names V. K. Ratliff, Gavin Stevens, Charles Mallison, Eula Varner Snopes, Linda, Flem, Jason Compson, Wall Street Panic Snopes, Goodyhay, Mink Snopes, and Joseph Blotner’s biography. Source anchors: George Garrett; Yoknapatawpha County; Saxe Commins; Albert Erskine; essential truth; V. K. Ratliff; Eula Varner; Mink Snopes.

Analysis: George Garrett and Yoknapatawpha County define the omnibus as a single historical field rather than a boxed set of rural anecdotes. Saxe Commins, Albert Erskine, and essential truth explain why the reader should not treat discrepancies as failures of bookkeeping; Faulkner’s revisions serve a living chronicle in which character knowledge changes over decades. V. K. Ratliff, Eula Varner, and Mink Snopes identify the introduction’s practical map: storytelling, erotic myth, and delayed revenge will carry the Snopes rise from Frenchman’s Bend to Jefferson and finally into the graveyard and open road. The introduction is also a Language and Ideology guide because it explains why the trilogy's shifting narrators, local voices, and corrected recollections are part of the Snopes story rather than commentary outside it.

THE HAMLET

Summary: The Hamlet opens in Frenchman’s Bend, twenty miles southeast of Jefferson, with Old Frenchman’s place reduced from plantation grandeur to a gutted house, fallen stables, slave quarters, cane-and-cypress growth, and a legend of buried money from Grant’s march to Vicksburg. Will Varner owns much of the land, the store, the cotton gin, the grist mill, the blacksmith shop, and mortgages, while Jody Varner tries to exploit Ab Snopes’s reputation as a barn burner. Ratliff supplies the tale of Major de Spain’s rug, horse manure, Ab’s lawsuit, and the burned barn, while Flem emerges as the silent son whose interest in Varner’s store matters more than farming. The novel expands through Book One “Flem,” Book Two “Eula,” Book Three “The Long Summer,” and Book Four “The Peasants,” bringing in Eula Varner, Ike Snopes’s love for a cow, the spotted horses, Henry Armstid’s broken leg, the Old Frenchman treasure scam, Eustace Grimm, Bookwright, Armstid’s sleepless digging, Flem’s marriage, and the move toward Jefferson. Source anchors: Frenchman's Bend; Old Frenchman; Will Varner; Ab Snopes; Major de Spain; Flem; Eula Varner; Henry Armstid.

Analysis: Frenchman’s Bend and Old Frenchman make The Hamlet a story of post-plantation ruins before the Snopeses arrive, and the section is a case for Commercial Society because acquisitive modernity enters a poor rural society through appetite rather than through industry. Will Varner, Ab Snopes, and Major de Spain show a countryside already organized by usury, threat, and retaliatory violence, so Flem simply learns to use those habits more coldly, which is the commercial-society mechanism in which exchange, credit, and hunger become the medium of power. Eula Varner and Henry Armstid expose the novel’s two main appetites, sexual fascination and money hunger, and Flem profits because he can provoke those appetites in others without appearing to be moved by them himself, so his rise runs on converting other people’s desires into his own advantage.

THE TOWN

Summary: The Town begins after Flem reaches Jefferson in a two-mule wagon with Eula, a baby, and furnishings, then moves from Ratliff’s back-alley restaurant and Grover Cleveland Winbush’s partnership to a tent behind the café and the first “Summer of the Snopeses.” Charles Mallison narrates what he has inherited from Gowan, Gavin Stevens, and Ratliff, making the water tank over Jefferson look first like Flem’s monument and then like his footprint. The section follows Flem’s acquisition of the power-plant superintendency, Manfred de Spain’s automobile politics, Mr Buffaloe’s handmade car, the Sartoris automobile law, Eula and De Spain’s affair, Tom Tom Bird, Tomey’s Turl, Harker, the stolen brass fittings, Montgomery Ward Snopes’s French postcard club, Hub Hampton, Judge Long, Wilbur Provine, Eula’s suicide, Linda’s New York departure, Byron Snopes’s Jicarilla Apache children, the dead Pekinese Lao T’se, and the tags sending the children back to El Paso. Source anchors: Charles Mallison; water tank; Grover Cleveland; Manfred de Spain; Tom Tom; Montgomery Ward; Eula's monument; Jicarilla Apache.

Analysis: Charles Mallison and water tank make The Town a study of civic perception, and the section is a case for Elite Formation because Flem’s ascent into respectability is built on jobs, blackmail, and reputation management rather than on inherited rank. Jefferson first misreads Flem’s presence because it notices the object after the power relation is already established, so the new elite is produced by positioning before anyone recognizes it as elite. Grover Cleveland, Tom Tom, and Montgomery Ward show Flem operating through hidden evidence and reputation rather than open force, which is elite formation through the capture of informal leverage. Manfred de Spain, Eula’s monument, and Jicarilla Apache children reveal what Jefferson cannot assimilate: Eula’s public beauty and private despair, De Spain’s modernizing glamour, and the raw Snopes energy that must be tagged, paid for, and shipped away when respectability requires it.

THE MANSION

Summary: The Mansion opens with Faulkner’s note that the book is the final chapter of a chronicle begun in 1925 and that contradictions arise because living literature means motion, change, and deeper knowledge of the human heart. The narrative begins with Mink Snopes hearing “Guilty” and “Life” after killing Jack Houston, though his mind is fixed on Flem’s failure to rescue him. Mink’s grievance starts with Houston’s stallion, the Bluetick hound, the barren milk cow, the scrub bull fee, Houston’s feed lot, Will Varner’s judgment, the eighteen dollars and seventy-five cents, post-hole digging, and the belief that fundamental justice will one day give him his turn. Later material follows Gavin Stevens, Linda Snopes Kohl, her Communist party card, Flem’s possible use of the F.B.I., Battle of Britain names such as Malan, Finucane, Spitfire, Hurricane, Buerling, Deere, and Clostermann, Pascagoula shipyard work, Gavin’s marriage to Melisandre, Mink’s release from Parchman, the killing of Flem, Ratliff’s “Give-me-lief” explanation, two hundred and fifty dollars, and Mink’s final westward walk into the earth. Source anchors: Mink Snopes; Jack Houston; Flem; eighteen dollars; Linda Snopes Kohl; Parchman; Give-me-lief; two hundred and fifty dollars.

Analysis: Mink Snopes and Jack Houston return the trilogy to bodily grievance after Flem’s ascent into bank and church respectability. Flem and eighteen dollars make the causal chain precise: Mink’s murder and imprisonment are tied to a kinship obligation Flem refuses and to a petty economic humiliation that grows into a life sentence. Linda Snopes Kohl, Parchman, Give-me-lief, and two hundred and fifty dollars convert revenge into a question of Moral Economy and inheritance; Linda releases Mink not merely to kill Flem but to answer Eula’s wasted love, while Mink’s final walk west gives the “poor son of a bitch” an equality the living world denied him. The delayed return from Parchman also turns the trilogy into Path Dependence and Institutional Drift, because Flem's earlier refusals and evasions remain active long after he appears to have stabilized his position.

William Faulkner

Summary: The closing biographical note identifies William Cuthbert Faulkner as born in 1897 in New Albany, Mississippi, the first of four sons of Murry and Maud Butler Falkner, and explains that the family moved in 1904 to Oxford, Mississippi. It links Faulkner to his great-grandfather, “The Old Colonel,” a Civil War veteran, railroad builder, state legislator, author of The White Rose of Memphis, and possible dueling victim. The note follows Faulkner from leaving high school after football season to work at his grandfather’s bank, failing to enlist in the U.S. Army in 1918, joining the RAF training program in Canada, returning in uniform, publishing The Marble Faun in 1924, meeting Sherwood Anderson in New Orleans in 1925, publishing Soldier’s Pay in 1926, writing Sartoris, The Sound and the Fury, Sanctuary, As I Lay Dying on a power-plant night shift, marrying Estelle Oldham, losing Alabama, raising Jill, writing Hollywood scripts for To Have and Have Not and The Big Sleep, receiving Malcolm Cowley’s Portable Faulkner revival, winning the 1949 Nobel Prize, and dying in Oxford on July 6, 1962. Source anchors: New Albany; Oxford; Old Colonel; RAF; Sherwood Anderson; As I Lay Dying; Malcolm Cowley; Nobel Prize.

Analysis: New Albany, Oxford, and Old Colonel tie Faulkner’s biography to the regional memory that becomes Yoknapatawpha without reducing the fiction to autobiography. RAF, Sherwood Anderson, and As I Lay Dying show the odd mixture of self-invention, apprenticeship, and disciplined labor behind the novels, including the power-plant work that echoes uncannily inside The Town. Malcolm Cowley and Nobel Prize explain why this omnibus can be framed as a major twentieth-century achievement rather than a regional curiosity, while the death in Oxford returns the writer to the Mississippi ground his fictional county transformed.
