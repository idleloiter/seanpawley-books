Detailed overview

Primo Levi builds The Periodic Table as a sequence of element-named stories in which chemistry is not a metaphor pasted onto autobiography but the actual trade, discipline, and vocabulary through which a life becomes legible. Argon opens with inert gases and Piedmontese Jewish ancestry; Carbon closes with an atom passing from limestone into air, vine, wine, muscle, cedar, insect, milk, and finally the nerve cell guiding Levi's hand. Between those poles, elements become handles for family memory, student apprenticeship, Fascist exclusion, industrial labor, Auschwitz survival, postwar improvisation, and the ethics of remembering.

The early chapters move from inherited identity into chosen vocation. In Argon, Levi treats his Jewish-Piedmontese relatives as noble, inert, rare presences who live at the edge of Italian society and preserve a comic Hebrew-Piedmontese jargon. Hydrogen and Zinc show adolescent chemistry as trespass and initiation: Enrico's brother's laboratory, electrolysis, Professor P., Rita, zinc sulfate, and the racial magazine Defense of the Race make laboratory impurity and Jewish impurity converge. Iron then gives the book one of its strongest human portraits in Sandro Delmastro, whose mountain craft, rock climbing, and later Resistance death turn chemistry's contact with matter into a preparation for courage.

War tightens the connection between chemical certainty and political exposure. Potassium describes 1941 Turin, Jewish isolation, the Talmud Torah gym, Croce, physics, the Institute of Experimental Physics, Onsager, the heterodyne, and the accident in which potassium is mistaken for sodium. Nickel sends Levi to a mine after graduation with a degree stamped by the racial laws; Phosphorus sends him to a Milanese factory run by a paranoid Swiss-Milanese commendatore, where diabetes research, anthocyanins, phosphorus extracts, rabbits, Giulia Vineis, and Dr. Kerrn expose both the absurdity and the human warmth of wartime technical work.

The book does not remain in first-person memoir. Lead and Mercury are invented historical tales about prospecting and colonial isolation, but they still obey Levi's chemical imagination: Rodmund finds galena, smelts lead, sells the secret of pipes and coffin linings, and heads toward the sea; Corporal Abrahams discovers mercury on a remote island and turns alchemical greed, sexual jealousy, and Burton's wife-barter into a grotesque mineral economy. These chapters widen the book's range while keeping its practical attention to ore, weight, tools, furnaces, retorts, and trade.

The center of the book is marked by captivity. Gold narrates Levi's failed partisan formation, betrayal, arrest with Aldo and Guido, the Aosta barracks, Fossa, Cagni, the Dora gold washer, and the desire for a free manual skill at the edge of deportation. Cerium returns explicitly to Auschwitz: prisoner number 174517 steals iron-cerium rods from the laboratory, works with Alberto under the blankets to scrape lighter flints, and turns sparks into bread. Vanadium later reopens Auschwitz through a postwar varnish dispute with W., where a misspelled "naptenate" leads Levi back to Dr. Lothar Muller, IG-Farben, Buna-Monowitz, Dr. Pannwitz, Goldbaum, and the unresolved question of how a perpetrator asks for absolution.

The postwar industrial chapters restore comic energy without trivializing the trade. Chromium is a detective story about livered varnish, a lost "or" in "2 or 3" drops, chromate, basic lead oxide, and ammonium chloride. Sulfur inhabits Lanza's night shift as pressure rises in a kettle full of sulfodiene. Titanium is a child's view of white paint, Felice, and a chalk circle. Arsenic, Nitrogen, Tin, Uranium, and Silver range from a cobbler's poisoned sugar to lipstick dyes, alloxan from chicken or python excrement, stannous chloride in Emilio's apartment, Bonino's imaginary uranium, and Cerrato's X-ray paper spoiled by polyphenols from a tannery. Levi's recurring claim is practical and moral: matter resists fantasy, but it also rewards patience, observation, craft, and honest naming.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Argon

Summary: Levi begins with inert gases, especially argon, xenon, fluorine, carbon dioxide, and the Nobel-winning 1962 feat of making xenon combine, then turns that chemical family into a portrait of his Jewish-Piedmontese ancestors. He traces surnames such as Bedarida, Momigliano, Segre, Foa, Cavaglion, Migliau, and Jarach from Spain, Provence, the Ebro, Lunel, and the Rhone into Piedmont, then recalls Bene Vagienna schoolyard mockery, the 1848 emancipation, barbe and magne, Barba Moréno, Gnôr Grassiadiô, the parrot saying "Nosce te ipsum," the crow answering "Wise up," and the Hebrew-Piedmontese jargon of havertà, goyim, manòd, and Nôssgnôr. Source anchors: argon, xenon, Bedarida, Bene Vagienna, Barba Moréno, Nosce te ipsum, havertà.

Analysis: Argon and xenon let Levi describe a community that survives by low reactivity rather than by conquest, and that chemical behavior gives structure to Bedarida, Bene Vagienna, and Barba Moréno without making them decorative examples. Havertà and Nosce te ipsum make Language and Ideology intimate: jargon hides, mocks, protects, and carries history at the same time. The chapter makes Jewish-Piedmontese identity a substance with specific impurities, comic charges, and defensive surfaces.

Hydrogen

Summary: In January, Enrico takes Levi after dinner to a laboratory that supposedly belongs to Enrico's brother, though the "keys" are really a euphemism for illicit access. The boys enter a dark, cold space, handle apparatus, produce hydrogen and oxygen by electrolysis, and experience the pleasure and danger of practical chemistry as a form of trespass. The chapter includes Enrico, the brother's lab, the stolen keys, water decomposition, hydrogen, oxygen, an explosion, the smell of gas, and the adolescent discovery that matter answers only exact operations. Source anchors: Enrico, brother's lab, stolen keys, electrolysis, hydrogen, oxygen, explosion.

Analysis: Enrico and the brother's lab make chemistry a clandestine initiation rather than a classroom subject, which is why stolen keys are as important as hydrogen. Electrolysis and oxygen show Levi learning that invisible substances can be separated, named, and made to act. The explosion corrects the boys' romantic excitement with material consequence, giving the book its first small lesson in the double nature of experiment: liberation and risk.

Zinc

Summary: Levi moves to Professor P.'s General and Inorganic Chemistry course, where the students face zinc and learn that pure zinc does not react unless impurity lets the acid attack. The laboratory includes Professor P., Corso Massimo d'Azeglio, used matches, the Institute's minarets, zinc sulfate, sulfuric acid, Rita, Thomas Mann's The Magic Mountain, Hans Castorp, Settembrini, Naphta, and the racial magazine Defense of the Race. Levi identifies himself as the impurity that makes the zinc react, first as a student attracted to Rita and then as a Jew newly forced to understand "purity" as Fascist poison. Source anchors: Professor P., zinc sulfate, Rita, Magic Mountain, Defense of the Race, impurity, sulfuric acid.

Analysis: Professor P. and zinc sulfate set up a chemical fact that Levi turns back onto social life: inertness is broken by impurity. Rita and Magic Mountain make the episode intimate rather than purely polemical, because the narrator's desire and awkwardness become part of the reaction. Defense of the Race turns Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy poisonous: Fascism tries to make "purity" the language of rightful belonging, so Levi's Jewishness becomes the grain the regime defines and excludes.

Iron

Summary: The year is 1939, and outside the Chemical Institute Chamberlain has returned from Munich, Hitler has entered Prague, Franco has taken Barcelona and Madrid, Italy has occupied Albania, and the racial laws have made Levi a loner. Inside the Qualitative Analysis laboratory, Professor D. hands out powders, Autenrieth guides the students, ammonium chloride fogs the windows, and Sandro Delmastro announces "Habemus ferrum." Sandro, the son of a mason from Serra d'Ivrea, teaches Levi the mountains, Sbarüa, the Tooth of M., frozen shoes, hunger, wrong routes, iron in rock, and a discipline that later meets the Resistance and Salò violence in Cuneo. Source anchors: Chamberlain, Prague, Sandro Delmastro, Habemus ferrum, Sbarüa, Tooth of M., Cuneo.

Analysis: Chamberlain and Prague place the laboratory inside European collapse, while Sandro Delmastro and Habemus ferrum make iron a human substance as well as an analytical finding. Sbarüa and Tooth of M. teach Levi bodily contact with rock, cold, error, and endurance, all of which matter more than abstract anti-Fascism at this stage. Cuneo gives the portrait its final weight: Sandro's mountain hardness becomes political courage, but not survival.

Potassium

Summary: In January 1941 Levi believes Germany likely to win and knows that a German Europe would be deadly for Jews, yet he and his friends in Turin practice self-imposed blindness. The chapter names Feuchtwanger's Oppermanns, a British White Book from Palestine, Madagascar, British Honduras, Talmud Torah, Einaudi, Ginzburg, Monti, Vittorio Foa, Carlo Levi, Croce, the Bible, geometry, physics, and the mountains around Turin as partial resources for inventing anti-Fascism. In the Institute of Experimental Physics, a shy astrophysics assistant gives Levi work on Onsager, polar molecules, the Westphal balance, a heterodyne filled with wartime Morse signals, and a purification mistake in which potassium explodes where sodium was expected. Source anchors: January 1941, Oppermanns, Talmud Torah, Onsager, heterodyne, potassium, sodium.

Analysis: January 1941 and Oppermanns establish the pressure under which Levi searches for certainty, while Talmud Torah and Croce show that moral education must be rebuilt from fragments. Onsager and the heterodyne give physics a seductive clarity, but potassium and sodium return him to chemical fallibility. The mistake matters because it joins political danger to laboratory danger: names are only useful when they match the substance in front of you.

Nickel

Summary: Levi's summa cum laude diploma is marked “of the Jewish race,” leaving him jobless until a soldier-messenger recruits him in secret to recover nickel from an asbestos mine's waste. Incognito at the isolated plant, Levi maps nickel through repeated assays while Alida pads results and the mine's stories briefly shelter him from his father's illness and the tightening war. Ordinary separation methods fail because 0.2 percent nickel is bound inside serpentine. Levi then heats the mineral to 800 degrees, reduces it with hydrogen, and magnetically collects a fraction containing 6 percent nickel. The apparent breakthrough collapses: the interpretation is partly wrong, no industrial selector can process the fine powder, richer Albanian ore makes the scheme irrelevant, and any product would have fed Axis weapons. Source anchors: racial laws; asbestos mine; Alida; serpentine; 800 degrees; 6 percent nickel; magnetic selector.

Analysis: The racial laws make Levi professionally disposable, while the asbestos mine values the same analytical training that Fascism has stigmatized. Serpentine and the 800-degree experiment give him a real intellectual victory: a hypothesis exposes material that routine assays could not isolate. Yet the magnetic selector, Albanian deposits, and likely military use separate discovery from usefulness and usefulness from moral value. The chapter therefore refuses a simple buried-treasure allegory. Chemistry restores Levi's agency and tests his claims against matter, but neither rigor nor ingenuity can by itself choose the political system that will exploit the result.

Lead

Summary: The first invented tale is narrated by Rodmund, a northern prospector from Thiuda whose family knows how to recognize, smelt, and sell lead. He travels south through passes, streams, shepherd settlements, larch valleys, Tringo, Sales, and eventually toward the sea, testing white speckled stones in a clay crucible with charcoal and a secret instrument. Rodmund finds galena, makes the shiny "little king," bargains with Borvio for gold, explains lead pipes, coffin linings, funeral statues, Saturn, and the metal of death, then carries his trade into mirror-making with a glazier and toward Icnusa, sailors, and new markets. Source anchors: Rodmund, Thiuda, Tringo, Sales, galena, little king, Borvio.

Analysis: Rodmund and Thiuda detach the book from Levi's own century while preserving the chemist's habits of eye, hand, furnace, and bargain. Tringo, Sales, galena, and little king make prospecting a narrative of inference: stream stones point to hidden veins, and the crucible tests suspicion. Borvio shows chemistry becoming commerce, because lead's meanings as pipe, coffin lining, Saturnine weight, and mirror backing all become part of its sale.

Mercury

Summary: Corporal Daniel K. Abrahams narrates life with his wife Maggie on a remote island, where Burton, Willem, Gaetano, Andrea, and Hendrik disturb a long routine of pigs, garrison duty, and isolation. An earthquake opens a cave where metallic mercury drips from the rock, and Hendrik, the fugitive Dutch alchemist, invokes spiritus mundi, the philosophic Egg, sulfur, the female principle, and the beast with two backs while pulling Maggie toward him. Abrahams forces Hendrik to distill the mercury in retorts, prepares forty sealed pint jars, trades through Burton for four wives and two children, and ends by marrying Rebecca Johnson while redistributing Maggie and Hendrik. Source anchors: Abrahams, Maggie, Burton, Hendrik, mercury cave, beast with two backs, Rebecca Johnson.

Analysis: Abrahams and Maggie make mercury a substance of jealousy before it becomes a commodity. Hendrik and beast with two backs bring alchemy's sexual and metaphysical language into collision with Abrahams's practical need for women, labor, and order. Burton, mercury cave, and Rebecca Johnson turn mineral discovery into social rearrangement, showing how a new material can distort a whole settlement's economy and marriages.

Phosphorus

Summary: In June 1942 Levi leaves the mine after Dr. Martini summons him to the Hotel Suisse in Turin and offers work in a Milanese factory researching an oral cure for diabetes. The commendatore's laboratory contains Giulia Vineis, Signora Loredana, Paglietta, Varisco, Ambrogio, staggered tram schedules, locked glassware, Basel oversight, Dr. Kerrn's German treatise, anthocyanins from cornflowers, organic phosphorus from sage, celandine, parsley, rabbits, glycemia tests, and a bombing that releases the animals into copulation. Giulia's warmth, the movie Port of Shadows, Michele Morgan, Jean Gabin, and her attempt to lend Levi her bicycle sit beside his humiliation at work he does not believe in. Source anchors: June 1942, Hotel Suisse, Giulia Vineis, Dr. Kerrn, anthocyanins, rabbits, glycemia.

Analysis: June 1942 and Hotel Suisse move Levi from mine chemistry into a stranger industrial theater ruled by secrecy and racial compromise. Giulia Vineis is the chapter's counterforce to Dr. Kerrn and the commendatore: her gossip, cooking, fear, and generosity keep the laboratory human. Anthocyanins, rabbits, and glycemia show bad science as a moral injury to the worker, because Levi must perform careful operations in service of a hypothesis he distrusts.

Gold

Summary: Gold places Levi in Milan among Euge, Silvio, Ettore, Lina, Vanda, Ada, poetry, bombings, O'Neill, Thornton Wilder, Stalingrad, North Africa, March strikes in Turin, July 25, September 8, and the Nazi occupation. He joins an unprepared partisan band in Piedmont with Aldo and Guido, is betrayed on December 13, 1943, hides a tiny revolver in stove ashes, eats his false identity card, drops his notebook in the snow, and is imprisoned in Aosta under Fossa and the spy Cagni. In the boiler room he meets a contrabandist and gold washer who describes the Dora, a secret bend, a pan, moon phases, two or three grams a night, and a freedom Levi can envy from his cell. Source anchors: December 13 1943, Aldo, Guido, Fossa, Cagni, Dora, gold washer.

Analysis: December 13 1943, Aldo, and Guido convert the political awakening of Milan into capture and helplessness. Fossa and Cagni give Fascism two faces, stupid legality and predatory betrayal, neither of which the students' mountain training can defeat. Dora and gold washer matter because they preserve an image of free work at the exact moment Levi is being carried toward deportation; gold becomes skill, water, and choice rather than wealth.

Cerium

Summary: In November 1944 Levi is prisoner 174517 in the Auschwitz chemical laboratory, close to death yet able to steal and reason because hunger has become the dominant fact of the body. He tries fatty acids, sanitary cotton, glycerin, and other failed loot before finding unlabeled gray rods of iron-cerium used for oxyacetylene torch ignition. Alberto recognizes their value for clandestine cigarette lighters, and during an air-raid alarm Levi steals the rest; together they scrape the rods under blankets at night, using a metal plate with a round hole to make flints worth bread, while the Russians approach, Buna shakes under bombing, and Alberto later disappears on the evacuation march to Buchenwald and Mauthausen. Source anchors: November 1944, 174517, iron-cerium, Alberto, air-raid alarm, flints, Buchenwald.

Analysis: November 1944 and 174517 place chemistry under Coercive Labor, where hunger, laboratory assignment, theft risk, and bodily exhaustion define what work and skill can mean. Iron-cerium and flints show the chemist's craft reduced to survival manufacture, while Alberto supplies imagination, courage, and market knowledge. Buchenwald matters because the success of the cerium episode saves days of life but cannot save Alberto from the later machinery of evacuation and murder.

Chromium

Summary: During a varnish-makers' meal about fish and red wine, Versino, Bruni, Cometto, and Levi move into a story of boiled linseed oil, anti-rust paint, and a persistent defect called livering. The investigation leads through purchase specifications, checking specifications, chromate file cards, the January 4, 1944 revision, the February 22 livered batch, a mistaken "23" where "2 or 3" drops had once stood, basic chromate, free lead oxide, ball mills, ammonium chloride, ammonia puffs, and two bicycle tires awarded after success. Years later the useless ammonium chloride remains in the formula because formulas are treated like prayers or dead languages. Source anchors: Versino, livering, chromate file card, January 4 1944, 23 drops, ammonium chloride, bicycle tires.

Analysis: Versino and the meal show how industrial knowledge circulates through anecdotes as much as through reports. Livering, chromate file card, January 4 1944, and 23 drops turn Knowledge Preservation into a factory problem: one lost word causes years of defective varnish because the formula survives after the reason for ammonium chloride has vanished. Bicycle tires give Levi comic reward, but the lingering recipe exposes an institutional weakness: solutions outlive the judgments that made them useful.

Sulfur

Summary: Lanza arrives before dawn, hooks his bike, punches the time card, starts the mixer, lights the naphtha burner, and watches a thermometer he alone knows reads five degrees low. The sulfodiene cook proceeds through 95 degrees, 180 degrees, B 41 that everyone knows is sulfur, a protective mask, vacuum, a centrifugal pump, a vacuum gauge, pressure rising in the kettle, yellowish foam, suction fan, compressor, and finally a black shiny stream of resin. Alone on the night shift, Lanza thinks of his wife, son, field, tavern, extra cow, wartime rooftop duty, and the ten lire each minute of work puts in his pocket. Source anchors: Lanza, naphtha burner, 95 degrees, B 41, vacuum gauge, suction fan, black resin.

Analysis: Lanza and the naphtha burner make factory labor bodily and solitary rather than managerial. 95 degrees and B 41 show the value of local knowledge: the worksheet and code name matter less than the worker's memory of the thermometer and sulfur. Vacuum gauge, suction fan, and black resin turn a near-accident into a study of competence under fear, where Lanza survives by thinking through the machine instead of fleeing it.

Titanium

Summary: Dedicated to Felice Fantino, this miniature is seen through Maria, a child watching a tall painter with a newspaper boat on his head, a pipe, a small can, and white paint that turns the kitchen closet, cupboard, chairs, and table dazzlingly clean. Maria asks why the paint is so white and hears "titanium" as something like a threat to cut her, then learns the man's name is Felice and tries not to touch the irresistible surface. Felice draws a chalk circle around Maria, tells her to stay there, leaves her in the magical boundary, and finally wipes the circle away so the enchantment is broken and she can leave happy. Source anchors: Felice Fantino, Maria, newspaper boat, titanium, white closet, chalk circle, enchantment.

Analysis: Felice Fantino and Maria let Levi make chemistry visible as childhood wonder rather than adult trade. Titanium and white closet give the element its sensory force: whiteness is so powerful that it seems magical and dangerous. Chalk circle and enchantment show how authority, language, and material transformation merge in a child's mind, making this brief chapter a comic counterweight to the harsher factories and camps.

Arsenic

Summary: In Levi and Emilio's small postwar laboratory, an old Piedmontese cobbler brings a parcel of sugar to be "chemistried" without explaining his suspicions. Levi detects turbidity, burns a gram in a platinum crucible, smells a metallic garlicky odor, uses hydrogen sulfide from the Kipp apparatus, and identifies yellow arsenious sulfide, the arsenic of Mithridates and Madame Bovary. The cobbler from Via Gioberti and Via Pastrengo explains that a younger rival spread lies about cardboard soles, drunkenness, insurance murder, and tetanus, then sent poisoned sugar; he will return it through a little old lady or in person, not prosecute, because there is room in the trade for everyone. Source anchors: Emilio, cobbler, sugar, garlicky odor, Kipp apparatus, arsenic, Via Gioberti.

Analysis: Emilio's improvised laboratory turns the cobbler's suspicion into evidence: the garlicky odor and Kipp apparatus distinguish arsenic from rumor, just as careful workmanship distinguishes the old shop from its mechanized rival's slander. The cobbler then refuses both helplessness and disproportionate revenge. He will confront the poisoner but not destroy him through prosecution because the trade has room for both men. Chemical detection thus supports a moral judgment without dictating it; Levi joins two crafts whose authority rests on patience, an exact eye, and restraint after the truth is known.

Nitrogen

Summary: A cosmetics manufacturer with Clark Gable mustache, pomade, hair tufts, and a dirty shed of made-up girls asks Levi to solve a lipstick problem. Levi observes rouge, "anelline," "adelaide," enameled pots, scarlet cylinders, kiss-proof tests, filter paper, an 80 degree stove, benzene, centrifugation, soluble dye, insoluble pigment, and then receives a request for alloxan as a permanent mucous-membrane dye. The search for alloxan sends him to the University of Turin library, Zentralblatt, Beilstein, uric acid, chicken excrement, python excrement, pollina, Corso Francia, his new wife, and a failed oxidation that leaves only foul vapors, blocked filters, and no crystals. Source anchors: lipstick, Clark Gable, soluble dye, alloxan, Zentralblatt, chicken excrement, python excrement.

Analysis: Lipstick and Clark Gable set up a comic marketplace where chemistry must serve vanity, fraud, and practical diagnosis. Soluble dye and alloxan move the chapter from ordinary consultation into Levi's deeper reflection on nitrogen, waste, and transformation. Zentralblatt, chicken excrement, and python excrement show the chemist's willingness to cross disgust boundaries, but the failed synthesis also teaches that matter does not reward every elegant idea.

Tin

Summary: Levi and Emilio try to live by turning tin from the Straits into stannous chloride for mirror manufacturers. Their laboratory occupies Emilio's parents' apartment, with hydrochloric acid demijohns in the hallway, concentration on the kitchen stove, a wooden and glass hood, the terrace full of DKW motorcycle parts and ammonia tanks, Signor Samuele's slaughterhouse blood business, Signora Ester's patience, crystallizing chloride in tureens and chamber pots, and fumes that discolor wallpaper and corrode nails. After failed vanillin, pyruvic acid, poor accounts, and Levi's need for employment, they dismantle the hood; the chain breaks, the hood falls four floors, and Emilio remarks that he thought it would make more noise. Source anchors: Emilio, tin, stannous chloride, hydrochloric acid, Signor Samuele, ventilation hood, DKW motorcycle.

Analysis: Emilio and tin make freelance chemistry both comic and economically fragile. Stannous chloride and hydrochloric acid show how a friendly metal becomes an aggressive salt that invades clothing, rooms, and family life. Signor Samuele, ventilation hood, and DKW motorcycle locate the failed enterprise in a household thick with experiments; the falling hood ends the adventure by breaking the physical sign of independence.

Uranium

Summary: Levi describes Customers' Service as a diplomatic trade, then visits Bonino after Tabasso sends him to discuss varnishes. Bonino recognizes Levi as the author of a book, recounts a confused partisan adventure involving Corso Orbassano, Val Susa, Badogliani, Aloisio Smit, Via Asti, a German Stork plane, two polite Germans asking the way to Switzerland, and a hot heavy "uranium" stone supposedly abandoned because the atomic bomb was no longer possible. Bonino sends a metal sample; Levi tests weight, Bunsen flame, brown smoke, silver nitrate, and identifies cadmium, then envies Bonino's freedom to invent a heroic past. Source anchors: Customers' Service, Bonino, Val Susa, Stork plane, uranium, cadmium, silver nitrate.

Analysis: Customers' Service requires Levi to flatter customers and manage belief, so Bonino's Val Susa adventure initially falls under the salesman's obligation to listen. The Stork plane and permanently hot uranium enlarge a confused partisan memory into the heroic past Bonino wants; the sample then transfers the story from courtesy to experiment. Weight, flame, brown oxide, and silver nitrate identify cadmium, restoring a boundary between a person's self-invention and a material claim. Levi can envy Bonino's freedom to remake his history while still insisting that the metal answer to reproducible tests.

Silver

Summary: A circular invites Levi to a twenty-fifth anniversary dinner for his chemistry class, where he identifies Cerrato as the likely organizer and asks for a chemical story. Cerrato, who spent years in photographic chemistry and X-ray paper, tells of a German department governed by pressurized filtered air, washed overalls, caps, slippers, a ban on face powder, silver bromide, hospitals in Vienna, Liege, and the Soviet Union, bean-shaped white spots, Wednesday batches, Turkish workers, a laundry, a polluted stream, a tannery, polyphenols, and a homeopathic dilution that reproduces the delayed defect. Source anchors: Cerrato, X-ray paper, silver bromide, Vienna, Wednesday batches, tannery, polyphenols.

Analysis: Cerrato and X-ray paper give Levi the kind of "solitary chemistry" he wants for the book: a material fault that has to be solved by patience and inference. Silver bromide, Wednesday batches, tannery, and polyphenols make Legibility material because the defect exists before the factory can see it, and only delayed hospital use in Vienna reveals what inspection missed. The chapter shows contamination crossing boundaries no regulation fully controls.

Vanadium

Summary: A defective resin from W., the German heir to part of IG-Farben, will not dry with lampblack until a letter from Doktor L. Müller recommends 0.1 percent vanadium naphthenate, misspelled "naptenate." The spelling recalls Auschwitz, Buna, beta-Naptylamin, Dr. Müller, shaving, shoes from the Effektenmagazin, Schkopau, Dr. Pannwitz, Goldbaum, the German edition of If This Is a Man, March 2, 1967, IG-Farben, Buna-Monowitz, crematorium flames, Volkssturm, and Müller's private attempt to ask for a meeting and for "overcoming that terrible past." Before Levi can meet him, Mrs. Müller reports Doktor Lothar Müller's unexpected death. Source anchors: W., vanadium naphthenate, naptenate, Dr. Müller, Buna, Goldbaum, March 2 1967.

Analysis: W. and vanadium naphthenate make a routine varnish dispute the route back to Auschwitz, and naptenate is the tiny linguistic trace that makes memory chemically precise. Dr. Müller, Buna, and Goldbaum turn Knowledge Preservation into a moral confrontation: the misspelled chemical term preserves enough of Monowitz to reopen questions of guilt, apology, and witness. March 2 1967 marks the point where business correspondence becomes moral correspondence, but Müller's death leaves judgment suspended in letters rather than settled face to face.

Carbon

Summary: Levi closes by saying the book is not a chemical treatise or simple autobiography but a micro-history of a trade, then follows one atom of carbon. The atom begins in limestone with calcium and oxygen, is quarried by a pickax in 1840, roasted in a lime kiln, released as carbon dioxide, lifted by wind, breathed by a falcon, dissolved in sea and torrent, fixed in a vine leaf in 1848 by photosynthesis, enters glucose, wine, a drinker's liver, lactic acid in a thigh muscle, returns to air, crosses the Apennines, Adriatic, Greece, Aegean, Cyprus, and Lebanon, becomes cedar cellulose, wood worm eye, humus, milk, bloodstream, and finally a nerve cell in Levi's brain making the dot on the page. Source anchors: micro-history, limestone, 1840, photosynthesis, glucose, cedar, nerve cell.

Analysis: Micro-history states Levi's scale: the trade is made visible through minute transformations rather than heroic generalities. Limestone, 1840, photosynthesis, and glucose connect geology, human work, plant chemistry, and animal energy in one continuous material passage. Cedar and nerve cell bring the book's method to its endpoint, because the carbon atom that the chemist narrates becomes part of the physical act of narration itself.
