Detailed overview

Philip Bobbitt's The Shield of Achilles joins constitutional law, military strategy, and diplomatic history into a single account of how the modern State changes form. Sir Michael Howard's foreword names the unusual ambition: Bobbitt is not only recounting wars or treaties, but asking how legitimacy, violence, law, finance, weapons, administration, and public obedience remake one another. The book's title points to Auden's "The Shield of Achilles," whose image of a brutal, exposed world returns in the epilogue as Bobbitt asks what sort of order can protect societies after the nation-state's promises have become harder to keep.

Bobbitt's first large claim is that the twentieth century should be read as one Long War from 1914 to the Peace of Paris in 1990. The First World War, Bolshevik Revolution, Spanish Civil War, Second World War, Korean War, Vietnam War, Cold War, collapse of Eastern European communism, German reunification, Charter of Paris, and Soviet dissolution are treated as linked contests over which kind of nation-state could claim legitimacy: fascist, communist, or parliamentary. The book does not say those events were identical; it says their unresolved constitutional question kept returning through new fronts, alliances, technologies, and domestic crises until parliamentary government under law defeated its rivals.

The middle of Book I reaches backward from the Long War to a sequence of earlier constitutional orders: princely state, kingly state, territorial state, state-nation, nation-state, and market-state. Bobbitt uses figures and institutions such as Charles VIII, Charles V, the Habsburgs, Gustavus Adolphus, Louis XIV, Frederick the Great, Napoleon, Lincoln, Bismarck, the levée en masse, the trace italienne, standing armies, public credit, railways, telegraphy, conscription, social insurance, and industrial mobilization to show that changes in warfare and changes in constitutional authority are mutually shaping. A state that cannot pay, recruit, command, legitimize, or protect under a new strategic environment either mutates or loses ground to states that can.

Book I ends by naming the market-state as the successor now pressing against the nation-state. Nuclear weapons, international telecommunications, and rapid computation helped the liberal democratic coalition win the Long War, but they also weakened the nation-state's ability to guarantee territorial defense, economic welfare, cultural cohesion, and public order. Bobbitt distinguishes entrepreneurial, mercantile, and managerial market-states, associating them with Washington, Tokyo, Berlin, the European Union, East Asian developmental systems, Thatcher, Reagan, Clinton, Blair, Schroeder, and George W. Bush. The new promise is not guaranteed welfare for the nation, but maximized opportunity for individuals and groups; Bobbitt worries that rival forms of that promise can again produce violence.

Book II repeats the pattern at the level of international society. The Peace of Augsburg, Peace of Westphalia, Treaty of Utrecht, Congress of Vienna, Versailles Treaty, and Peace of Paris are treated as constitutional settlements for the society of states, not merely diplomatic closings. Bobbitt reads international law through the constitutional orders of the states that make it: princely states use cuius regio eius religio; kingly states ratify sovereign equality after religious war; territorial states stabilize a balance of power; state-nations build the Concert of Europe; nation-states build League and United Nations structures around self-determination, collective security, Article 2(4), Article 51, and the Montevideo criteria.

The final chapters ask what a society of market-states must do when the threats are not simply armies crossing borders. Bobbitt's examples include Srebrenica, Kosovo, Rwanda, East Timor, Iraq, Serbia, Somalia, Haiti, Panama, Afghanistan, weapons of mass destruction, computer viruses, financial panics, environmental collapse, mass migration, human-rights catastrophes, NGOs, CARE, Amnesty International, multinational corporations, G-7, IMF, WTO, NATO, and the International Criminal Court. The 2001 postscript then places Osama bin Laden, Al Qaeda, the World Trade Center, Washington, Afghanistan, Russia, NATO, shared intelligence, missile defense, cyber defense, and coalition warfare inside the same problem: market-state technologies make possible a "virtual state" that must be met by vulnerability-based, cooperative security rather than Cold War deterrence alone.

Core concepts and linkages

  • State Capacity: Bobbitt evaluates every constitutional order by whether it can tax, recruit, command, legitimate violence, provide security, and adapt to strategic pressure.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: the book treats epochal wars and peace settlements as contests over which state-form can inherit lawful authority after an older constitutional promise fails.
  • Crisis Governance: Srebrenica, Kosovo, WMD, terrorism, cyber attacks, financial panic, refugee flows, and September 11 force decisions under emergency conditions where old sovereignty rules do not decide the answer.
  • Information and Coordination: international telecommunications, rapid computation, shared intelligence, cyber defense, market signals, NGOs, and coalition warfare change what states and nonstate actors can know and coordinate.
  • Financial Infrastructure: public credit, the Bank of England, Bretton Woods, global capital markets, IMF, WTO, financial panics, and market-state opportunity politics connect constitutional order to money systems.
  • Path Dependence and Institutional Drift: Bobbitt's sequence of princely, kingly, territorial, state-nation, nation-state, and market-state forms tracks how older institutions persist while strategic pressures force mutation.
  • Platform Governance: the market-state shifts legitimacy toward maximizing opportunity through markets, networks, regulators, corporations, NGOs, trade regimes, and shared security systems rather than national welfare alone.
  • Speculative Governance: Bobbitt's possible-world scenarios are not prediction for its own sake; they test how different market-state designs might govern future war, peace, law, and legitimacy.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Prologue

Summary: Bobbitt opens by saying that for five centuries only states could gather the revenues, conscript armies, equip divisions, and destroy other states, but international telecommunications, rapid computation, and weapons of mass destruction have ended that monopoly. He defines the book as an inquiry into the modern state, epochal wars, constitutional law, international law, strategy, and the emerging market-state. He names the Long War as the conflict from 1914 to 1990 and lists five pressures on the nation-state: human rights norms, nuclear weapons and other WMD, transnational threats such as environment and disease, global capital markets, and electronic communications that penetrate borders. Source anchors: international telecommunications; rapid computation; weapons of mass destruction; market-state; Long War; five developments.

Analysis: The Prologue fixes the book's scale by tying international telecommunications, rapid computation, and weapons of mass destruction to the constitutional failure of the nation-state, not merely to new military tools. The Long War gives Bobbitt a single historical unit for 1914-1990, while the five developments explain why victory by parliamentary nation-states creates the conditions for their replacement. The market-state appears here as a consequence of statecraft, law, and strategy working together. The prologue is also a Platform Governance argument because the market-state's legitimacy will depend on networks, opportunity, and shared systems that cross territorial borders.

Chapter One: Thucydides and the Epochal War

Summary: Bobbitt uses Thucydides' exile after Amphipolis in 424 B.C., the Peace of Nicias in 421 B.C., the Athenian raid in 414 B.C., and the wider Athens-Sparta conflict to define an epochal war as a sequence of conflicts whose main constitutional issue remains unsettled. He compares the Peloponnesian War with the Hundred Years' War, the Thirty Years' War, the Punic Wars, the English weekly The Moderate Intelligencer, Ferdinand of Habsburg, Bishop Gepeckh of Freising, the Peace of Westphalia, the Peace of the Pyrenees, and the Peace of Copenhagen. Source anchors: Amphipolis; Peace of Nicias; Athenian raid; Thirty Years' War; Bishop Gepeckh; Peace of Westphalia.

Analysis: Amphipolis and the Peace of Nicias show why a treaty does not prove that the underlying war is over; Bobbitt uses Thucydides to teach his method for reading 1914-1990. The Thirty Years' War, Bishop Gepeckh, and the Peace of Westphalia supply later evidence that historians identify wars by unresolved constitutional questions rather than by continuous fighting. The chapter gives the Long War a precedent before Bobbitt applies the term to fascism, communism, and parliamentarianism.

Chapter Two: The Struggle Begun: Fascism, Communism, Parliamentarianism, 1914-1919

Summary: Bobbitt argues that the First World War, Bolshevik Revolution, Spanish Civil War, Korean War, Vietnam War, and Cold War belong to one Long War because Germany and Russia made fascism and communism into great-power alternatives to parliamentary democracy. The chapter traces Prussia, Bismarck, Austria-Hungary, France, the Constitution of 1871, the Social Democratic election threat of 1912, Fritz Fischer's archival work, Bethmann Hollweg's September Program, Sarajevo in 1914, Russia's Provisional Government, Kerensky, Lenin, Brest-Litovsk, Petrograd soviet, and "all power to the soviets." Source anchors: Bismarck; Constitution of 1871; Fritz Fischer; September Program; Provisional Government; Brest-Litovsk.

Analysis: Bismarck and the Constitution of 1871 make fascism a German path to national legitimacy rather than a later aberration, and the chapter is a case for Legitimacy and Succession because Bobbitt frames the Long War as a contest over which constitutional form could claim the right to rule. Fritz Fischer and the September Program let Bobbitt connect 1914 policy to domestic fear of parliamentarianism and socialism, so the war’s legitimacy question is which state-form can survive its own population’s demands. The Provisional Government and Brest-Litovsk show the Russian side of the same Long War question: a parliamentary form collapsed because Lenin offered peace, land, soviets, and class rule where Kerensky offered legal continuity and continued war, which is succession decided by which legitimacy story the army and the streets would accept.

Chapter Three: The Struggle Continued: 1919-1945

Summary: Bobbitt reads the interwar period and the Second World War as the continuation of the unresolved constitutional contest after Versailles. Germany's Weimar Republic, Hitler, Nazism, the Soviet Union, Stalin, fascist Italy, Japan, the Spanish Civil War, appeasement, the Nazi-Soviet Pact, Pearl Harbor, the Allied coalition, strategic bombing, Normandy, Kursk, Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and the unconditional surrender of Germany and Japan all become tests of whether fascism, communism, or parliamentary government could mobilize and legitimate the nation-state. Source anchors: Weimar Republic; Hitler; Stalin; Spanish Civil War; Normandy; Hiroshima.

Analysis: Weimar Republic and Hitler matter because Bobbitt treats fascism as the revived German answer to a question Versailles failed to settle. Stalin and the Spanish Civil War show communism entering the same European contest before the Nazi-Soviet Pact and Allied coalition rearrange the battlefield. Normandy and Hiroshima then demonstrate that parliamentary nation-states could coordinate industrial production, alliance command, and total war well enough to discredit fascism without yet ending communism.

Chapter Four: The Struggle Ended: 1945-1990

Summary: Bobbitt follows the Cold War as the final campaign of the Long War, moving through containment, the Atlantic Alliance, Korea, Vietnam, nuclear deterrence, extended deterrence, reassurance, Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, South Korea, Japan, Thailand, Taiwan, Czechoslovakia, Afghanistan, the Ussuri River, Helsinki, Gorbachev, perestroika, glasnost, Poland in 1989, Hungary's border opening, the Berlin Wall on November 9, 1989, German unification, the Charter of Paris in November 1990, and the Soviet dissolution on December 25, 1991. Source anchors: containment; Vietnam; perestroika; Berlin Wall; Charter of Paris; Soviet Union.

Analysis: Containment and Vietnam let Bobbitt distinguish battlefield defeat from Long War success, because delay in Southeast Asia bought time for Indonesia, South Korea, Japan, Thailand, and Taiwan. Perestroika and the Berlin Wall show communism losing its claim inside the Soviet sphere, while the Charter of Paris and Soviet Union dissolution give the parliamentary nation-state a formal settlement. The chapter therefore closes the war that Chapter One taught readers to recognize as epochal.

Chapter Five: Strategy and the Constitutional Order

Summary: Bobbitt reviews the debate over the European "military revolution" by setting Michael Roberts's Belfast lecture, Sir George Clark, Geoffrey Parker, Jeremy Black, David Parrott, Gustavus Adolphus, the trace italienne, Wallenstein, the Holy Roman Emperor, Strasbourg, Breisach, Turin, Nördlingen, Breitenfeld, Lützen, Wittstock, and Poland against one another. He rejects a single date or single causal line and proposes a field relationship in which strategic change and constitutional change shape each other through imitation, contingency, resources, and choice. Source anchors: Michael Roberts; Geoffrey Parker; Jeremy Black; David Parrott; trace italienne; field relationship.

Analysis: Michael Roberts, Geoffrey Parker, Jeremy Black, and David Parrott are not just authorities; Bobbitt uses their disagreement to justify his six constitutional orders. The trace italienne and field relationship let him avoid technological determinism, because fortifications, army size, public finance, private contractors, and state administration can all be cause or effect depending on the starting point. This chapter supplies the method for the historical sequence that follows. The field relationship is Path Dependence and Institutional Drift at state scale: old constitutional arrangements persist until strategic innovations make their inherited routines inadequate.

Chapter Six: From Princes to Princely States: 1494-1648

Summary: Bobbitt begins with post-Roman Europe, Charlemagne, the Church, feudal nobility, burghers, peasants, Jews, guilds, Italian city-states, Charles VIII's 1494 invasion of Italy, mobile artillery, Naples, Milan, the Habsburg-Valois struggle, Charles V, Francis I, Henry VII, Henry VIII, the Reformation, the Ottoman threat, mercenary forces, fiscal bureaucracy, and the trace italienne. He shows how princes had to build princely states capable of taxation, administration, fortification, and diplomacy. Source anchors: Charlemagne; Italian city-states; Charles VIII; mobile artillery; Charles V; trace italienne.

Analysis: Charles VIII and mobile artillery force the transformation from personal princely rule toward the princely state, because Italian city-states and dynasts needed money, engineers, walls, and bureaucratic control to survive. Charles V and the Reformation show why universal Christendom could no longer contain the new political units. The trace italienne becomes the material sign of the change: high civic walls and local privilege give way to low bastions, centralized taxation, and state-managed defense.

Chapter Seven: From Kingly States to Territorial States: 1648-1776

Summary: Bobbitt moves from Westphalia into the era of kingly and territorial states, using Louis XIV, Richelieu, Mazarin, the Fronde, absolutism, Sweden, Holland, England, the Dutch Republic, the Glorious Revolution, William III, Parliament, public credit, the Bank of England, standing armies, cabinet war, Frederick the Great, Prussia, Austria, and the balance of power. The chapter describes how sovereignty becomes more territorial, fiscal, and legal after religious settlement gives way to dynastic and administrative competition. Source anchors: Louis XIV; Glorious Revolution; Bank of England; Frederick the Great; Prussia; balance of power.

Analysis: Louis XIV supplies Bobbitt's model for kingly concentration of authority, while the Glorious Revolution and Bank of England show how constitutional limits can produce stronger credit and military endurance. Frederick the Great and Prussia connect disciplined armies to territorial administration rather than sectarian monarchy. The balance of power then becomes the external counterpart to states whose legitimacy rests on controlling territory, taxation, and professional force. The Bank of England material makes Financial Infrastructure part of constitutional survival because credit expands what the state can finance and sustain.

Chapter Eight: From State-Nations to Nation-States: 1776-1914

Summary: Bobbitt traces the state-nation and nation-state through the American Revolution, French Revolution, levée en masse, Napoleon, Great Britain, Castlereagh, the Congress of Vienna, the American Civil War, Lincoln, Lee, Grant, Sherman, Gettysburg, the Minié ball rifle, railways, telegraphy, Bismarck, Prussia, Königgrätz, Gravelotte, Sedan, German social insurance, Moltke's warning, Cavour, and Italian unification. He argues that industrial war required popular mobilization and that national welfare became a source of state legitimacy. Source anchors: levée en masse; Napoleon; Lincoln; Minié ball; Bismarck; Sedan.

Analysis: The levée en masse and Napoleon show how revolutionary citizenship produced new armies and tactics, while Lincoln and the Minié ball show the American Civil War turning Union preservation into nation-making through industrial violence. Bismarck and Sedan make Prussia the European model for a nation-state that trades welfare, unity, railways, conscription, and military success for popular loyalty. The chapter explains why 1914 could become a war over rival kinds of nation-state.

Chapter Nine: The Study of the Modern State

Summary: Bobbitt pauses to criticize constitutional law texts for ignoring war and war studies for ignoring constitutions. He defines the State through constitutional law, strategy, history, legitimacy, violence, territorial jurisdiction, temporal jurisdiction, the monopoly on domestic violence, external defense, Habsburg Spain, the Netherlands, Austro-Hungary, Palestinians, Kurds, nuclear weapons, international communications, computers, and the nation-state's weakening ability to provide security, welfare, economic growth, and national legal cohesion. Source anchors: constitutional law; strategy; legitimacy; monopoly on violence; Palestinians; Kurds.

Analysis: Constitutional law and strategy become paired tasks, and the chapter is a case for State Capacity because Bobbitt asks whether each constitutional form can still pay, recruit, command, legitimize, and protect under the strategic environment it faces. Allocating decisions inside society and defending that allocation against outsiders are the two sides of capacity, so a state-form is tested by what it can execute, not only by what it promises. Legitimacy and monopoly on violence explain why Bobbitt will not treat law as ornament or war as an accident. Palestinians and Kurds illustrate how the nation-state still attracts stateless peoples even as existing nation-states struggle with nuclear weapons, international communications, and economic management that their constitutional form cannot easily control, which is the capacity gap that pushes the nation-state toward the market-state successor.

Chapter Ten: The Market-State

Summary: Bobbitt announces that the nation-state's crisis comes from strategic innovations that helped win the Long War: nuclear weapons, international communications, and rapid mathematical computation. He contrasts the princely state's promise of security, kingly state's promise of internal stability, territorial state's promise of material wealth, state-nation's promise of political rights, and nation-state's promise of welfare with the market-state's promise to maximize opportunity. Examples include Clemenceau's "total war," Roosevelt's atomic bomb decision, Saddam Hussein, Japan, Germany, Israel, Iraq, India, Pakistan, East European television, Bretton Woods, Eric Helleiner, Margaret Thatcher, Ronald Reagan, Bill Clinton, Tony Blair, and George W. Bush. Source anchors: nuclear weapons; total war; Saddam Hussein; Bretton Woods; maximize opportunity; Bill Clinton.

Analysis: Nuclear weapons and total war demonstrate that the nation-state perfected its own means of mass protection and mass destruction, only to make civilian protection less credible. Saddam Hussein and Bretton Woods show the same problem in military and economic form: borders cannot reliably contain coercive weapons or mobile capital. Bill Clinton and maximize opportunity mark the new constitutional bargain, where government supplies tools, markets allocate many outcomes, and welfare guarantees recede. The chapter frames the market-state as a new Legitimacy and Succession claim: the right to rule shifts from guaranteeing national welfare to enabling opportunity.

Chapter Eleven: Strategic Choices

Summary: Bobbitt examines the United States after the Long War and criticizes inherited mission terms such as deterrence, compellance, and reassurance. He discusses Pentagon White Papers, Jacob Viner, Bernard Brodie, Thomas Schelling, Sir Michael Howard, nuclear strategy, extended deterrence, central deterrence, Canada, Saddam Hussein's evacuation of Kuwait, Slobodan Milosevic's abandonment of Kosovo, the Gulf War, the Indian chief of staff's lesson about nuclear weapons, Paul Bracken, Fred Iklé, rogue states, peer competitors, and the problem of defining American interests after victory. Source anchors: deterrence; compellance; reassurance; Bernard Brodie; Thomas Schelling; Kosovo.

Analysis: Deterrence, compellance, and reassurance matter because Bobbitt thinks they migrated from nuclear strategy into conventional planning after their old targets had changed. Bernard Brodie and Thomas Schelling provide precise Cold War concepts, while Kosovo and Kuwait show force used for limited political purposes that need explicit doctrine. The chapter presses the United States to identify threats and interests before bureaucratic habit turns victory in the Long War into strategic drift.

Chapter Twelve: Strategy and the Market-State

Summary: Bobbitt lays out how the market-state should adapt strategy when threats include WMD, terrorism, cyber weapons, environmental collapse, migration, disease, famine, critical infrastructure attack, capital flight, and information penetration. He examines alliances, defense, deception, intelligence, rapid computation, market incentives, privatized functions, executive secrecy, public revenue, nuclear proliferation, the American military, allied reassurance, NATO, Japan, Germany, Russia, China, rogue states, and peer competitors. He stresses vulnerability-based defense and the need for strategies that match a state promising opportunity rather than guaranteed welfare. Source anchors: vulnerability-based defense; cyber weapons; critical infrastructure; NATO; Japan; Germany.

Analysis: Vulnerability-based defense names the move away from Cold War target lists toward protection of systems that cyber weapons, WMD, and terrorist actors can exploit. NATO, Japan, and Germany show why reassurance cannot vanish without encouraging proliferation among advanced allies. Critical infrastructure gives the market-state a new object of security: not just borders or armies, but networks whose failure can undo opportunity, capital confidence, and public trust.

Chapter Thirteen: The Wars of the Market-State

Summary: Bobbitt concludes Book I by distinguishing entrepreneurial, mercantile, and managerial market-states and by warning that their competition could yield low-intensity conflicts, larger anarchy, or cataclysm. He discusses Washington, Tokyo, Berlin, shareholder interests, stakeholder companies, labor markets, capital markets, social safety nets, privatization, NGOs, multinational corporations, G-7, IMF, WTO protests, CARE, Amnesty International, terrorism, environmental funds, Panama, Haiti, Somalia, Serbia, Iraq, Afghanistan, mass refugee migrations, and ethnic cleansing. Source anchors: entrepreneurial; mercantile; managerial; stakeholder company; IMF; ethnic cleansing.

Analysis: Entrepreneurial, mercantile, and managerial are the rival forms that may repeat the nation-state's earlier split among fascism, communism, and parliamentarianism. The stakeholder company and IMF show how market-state legitimacy travels through firms, finance, and institutions outside ordinary voting. Ethnic cleansing and Somalia force Bobbitt's security question: if markets and NGOs cross borders, intervention cannot be explained solely as national conquest or classic war.

Chapter Fourteen: Colonel House and a World Made of Law

Summary: Bobbitt begins Book II with Colonel E. M. House, Woodrow Wilson, the Inquiry, Versailles, collective security, balance of power, national self-determination, nested constituencies, the League of Nations, the U.N. Charter, universal law, the General Assembly, the Security Council, veto power, the Gulf War, Stanley Hoffmann, Michael Howard, Clausewitz, Grotius, Nuremberg, Katyn Forest, Saddam Hussein in Kuwait, and Margaret Thatcher in the Falklands. He argues that international law grows from constitutional assumptions rather than floating above state history. Source anchors: Colonel House; Woodrow Wilson; the Inquiry; League of Nations; Security Council; Nuremberg.

Analysis: Colonel House and Woodrow Wilson let Bobbitt locate twentieth-century international law inside American constitutional ideas about constituency and national identity. The Inquiry and League of Nations show the attempted translation of those ideas into world order, while the Security Council reveals the compromise with great-power strategy. Nuremberg matters because it proves law after war can legitimate victors only when it creates rules they may later have to respect.

Chapter Fifteen: The Kitty Genovese Incident and the War in Bosnia

Summary: Bobbitt pairs Kitty Genovese's murder in Queens with Bosnia to explain how nation-states convert moral failure into political responsibility. He discusses Genovese's neighbors, American crime politics, Srebrenica, ethnic cleansing, Sarajevo, the Yugoslav Wars, Bosnia, Serbia, Kosovo, NATO bombing, Article 2(7), domestic jurisdiction, the League of Nations, Ethiopia, Libya, Mussolini, concentration camps, Cambodia, the Khmer Rouge, Somalia, Mali, and humanitarian intervention. Source anchors: Kitty Genovese; Srebrenica; ethnic cleansing; Kosovo; Article 2(7); Khmer Rouge.

Analysis: Kitty Genovese gives Bobbitt a domestic analogy for the nation-state's welfare promise: citizens blame the State when society fails to protect them. Srebrenica, ethnic cleansing, and Kosovo then transfer that logic to international society, where sovereignty shields atrocities until outside force pierces it. Article 2(7) and the Khmer Rouge show why institutions built for nation-state sovereignty often cannot answer mass murder conducted inside recognized borders.

Chapter Sixteen: The Death of the Society of Nation-States

Summary: Bobbitt argues that the Wilsonian society of nation-states is dying because the U.N. system cannot consistently handle sovereignty, human rights, intervention, WMD, and great-power responsibility. He examines Article 2(4), Article 51, Security Council enforcement, Article 42, Article 43, Korea in 1950, U.S. command under the U.N. flag, the Gulf coalition, NATO in Kosovo, Entebbe, Tehran, Libya, Dominican Republic, Grenada, Haiti, Panama, the ICJ reform proposal, Daniel Patrick Moynihan, Robert Bork, and the failure of a universal superior law model. Source anchors: Article 2(4); Article 51; Korea; NATO in Kosovo; ICJ; Robert Bork.

Analysis: Article 2(4) and Article 51 let Bobbitt show how the U.N. Charter's text has been stretched because states will not surrender violence to a world sovereign. Korea and NATO in Kosovo prove that actual enforcement depends on state or alliance command, not a standing U.N. army. The ICJ and Robert Bork sharpen the choice between abandoning international law and rebuilding it as a consensual, market-state practice rather than a universal code. The enforcement gap is a State Capacity problem at international scale: legal authority exists, but executable violence still depends on coalitions and capable states.

Chapter Seventeen: Peace and the International Order

Summary: Bobbitt opens the historical survey of peace settlements by connecting Colonel House, Kitty Genovese, and Srebrenica to the creation of constitutional orders for international society. He states that peace conferences ending epochal wars ratify the dominant constitutional order and allocate rights, duties, institutions, amendment methods, dispute procedures, and standards of legitimacy. Examples include the French Revolution, Napoleon, Prospero Colonna's defense of Milan in 1521, Charles VIII's 1494 invasion, the trace italienne, Augsburg, Westphalia, Utrecht, Vienna, Versailles, and the Peace of Paris. Source anchors: peace conferences; Prospero Colonna; Milan; Augsburg; Utrecht; Peace of Paris.

Analysis: Peace conferences become the international counterpart to Bobbitt's domestic story of war and constitutional change. Prospero Colonna and Milan recall the strategic pressures that created princely states, while Augsburg, Utrecht, and Peace of Paris mark settlements where states accept new rules for one another. The chapter supplies Book II's core claim: international law is derived from the constitutional forms of the states that survive epochal war.

Chapter Eighteen: The Treaty of Augsburg

Summary: Bobbitt treats the Peace of Augsburg as the constitutional settlement of the society of princely states. He discusses Commynes, Machiavelli, Charles VII's French reforms, Charles VIII's 1494 invasion of Italy, Naples, Milan, Valois-Habsburg rivalry, Charles V's failed Respublica Christiana, Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis, Scandinavia, Reformation kings, Russia's Ivan III and Ivan IV, Henry VII, Henry VIII, cuius regio eius religio, Catholic and Protestant princes, and non-interference. Source anchors: Peace of Augsburg; cuius regio eius religio; Charles V; Respublica Christiana; Machiavelli; non-interference.

Analysis: Peace of Augsburg matters because cuius regio eius religio turns religious division into a rule for state society rather than a mere truce. Charles V and Respublica Christiana represent the defeated universal order, while Machiavelli and non-interference represent the practical habits of rulers in a world of separate princely states. Bobbitt uses the treaty to show international law beginning as a rule about who may decide religion inside a territory.

Chapter Nineteen: The Peace of Westphalia

Summary: Bobbitt presents Westphalia as the peace of the kingly state after the Thirty Years' War, religious exhaustion, dynastic consolidation, and secular diplomacy. He discusses Ferdinand of Habsburg, Protestant Bohemia, Catholic and Protestant powers, Sweden, France, Spain, the Holy Roman Empire, Richelieu's raison d'état, the Dutch revolt, the treaties of Münster and Osnabrück, territorial sovereignty, sovereign equality, ambassadorial practice, and the declining authority of pope and emperor. Source anchors: Westphalia; Thirty Years' War; Münster; Osnabrück; raison d'état; sovereign equality.

Analysis: Westphalia and the Thirty Years' War move the society of states beyond Augsburg's princely religious compromise into a secular order of kingly sovereignty. Münster and Osnabrück give Bobbitt treaty evidence for rights and recognition among rulers, while raison d'état explains why Catholic France could fight beside Protestants against Habsburg power. Sovereign equality emerges here as a practice tied to successful kingly states, not as an eternal legal axiom.

Chapter Twenty: The Treaty of Utrecht

Summary: Bobbitt reads Utrecht as the settlement that ratified the territorial state and the balance of power after the wars of Louis XIV. He discusses the War of the Spanish Succession, Bourbon France, Habsburg claims, Britain, the Dutch, Spain, the Spanish Netherlands, limited monarchy, parliamentary finance, commercial empire, public debt, the asiento, Gibraltar, dynastic succession, and the effort to prevent France and Spain from being joined under one crown. Source anchors: Utrecht; balance of power; Spanish Succession; Gibraltar; asiento; limited monarchy.

Analysis: Utrecht and balance of power show the society of states accepting territorial equilibrium as a constitutional rule, not merely a diplomatic preference. Spanish Succession and Gibraltar supply concrete stakes: dynastic inheritance had to be constrained when it threatened the system's security. The asiento and limited monarchy connect the settlement to commercial and fiscal forms of power, especially Britain's capacity to turn credit, trade, and naval force into international authority.

Chapter Twenty-One: The Congress of Vienna

Summary: Bobbitt treats Vienna as the settlement of the state-nation after the wars of the French Revolution and Napoleon. He discusses Castlereagh, Metternich, Tsar Alexander, Talleyrand, Great Britain, Austria, Prussia, Russia, France, Napoleon, the Concert of Europe, periodic congresses, legitimacy, compensation, the balance of power, the Holy Alliance, Poland, Saxony, the German Confederation, and the inclusion of defeated France in the new order. Source anchors: Congress of Vienna; Castlereagh; Metternich; Talleyrand; Concert of Europe; German Confederation.

Analysis: Congress of Vienna matters because Castlereagh, Metternich, and Talleyrand used settlement rather than annihilation to stabilize a Europe transformed by Napoleon. The Concert of Europe gives institutional form to the state-nation's international order, where great powers meet periodically to manage crises. The German Confederation and restored France show Bobbitt's point that peace settlements allocate functions and legitimacy as much as territory.

Chapter Twenty-Two: The Versailles Treaty

Summary: Bobbitt describes Versailles as an attempted constitution for the society of nation-states that failed to settle the Long War. He discusses Woodrow Wilson, Colonel House, the Fourteen Points, self-determination, the League of Nations, reparations, Germany, Austria-Hungary, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Yugoslavia, mandates, Lloyd George, Clemenceau, Harold Nicolson, John Maynard Keynes, Lansing, the United States Senate, collective security, and the unresolved claims that helped open the way to renewed war. Source anchors: Versailles; Fourteen Points; self-determination; reparations; League of Nations; Keynes.

Analysis: Versailles and Fourteen Points show Wilson's nation-state principles entering treaty form, but self-determination and reparations pull in opposite directions when applied to Germany, Eastern Europe, and imperial possessions. League of Nations embodies House's collective security but lacks the American commitment and strategic settlement needed to enforce it. Keynes matters because his disgust signals that the peace did not legitimate the order it claimed to found.

Chapter Twenty-Three: The Peace of Paris

Summary: Bobbitt treats the 1990 Peace of Paris as the settlement that finally ended the Long War by accepting parliamentary institutions and market methods across the great powers. He discusses James Baker, George H. W. Bush, Gorbachev, German reunification, Poland, Czechoslovakia, Hungary, the CSCE, the Charter of Paris, NATO, the Soviet Union, Britain, France, Germany, the United States, New World Order language, and the contrast between Paris in 1990 and Versailles in 1919. Source anchors: Peace of Paris; James Baker; Gorbachev; Charter of Paris; German reunification; CSCE.

Analysis: Peace of Paris and Charter of Paris give Bobbitt the formal close to the Long War because the defeated Soviet side accepted parliamentary and market principles instead of being excluded. James Baker and Gorbachev replace Wilson and House in a less punitive settlement, while German reunification shows the constitutional answer becoming practical in Europe. CSCE participation turns the peace into an international constitution rather than a bilateral victory document.

Chapter Twenty-Four: Challenges to the New International Order

Summary: Bobbitt begins the society of market-states by describing market orthodoxy and its rival forms. He discusses capital markets, flexible labor, free trade, privatization, tax incentives, infrastructure, welfare cuts, Margaret Thatcher, Tony Blair, Bill Clinton, George W. Bush, Washington's entrepreneurial model, Tokyo's mercantile model, Berlin's managerial model, Singapore, Hong Kong, Taiwan, South Korea, Samsung, Hyundai, Daewoo, Soziale Marktwirtschaft, stakeholder companies, VAT, India, Turkey, Egypt, and globalization protests. Source anchors: Washington; Tokyo; Berlin; Samsung; Soziale Marktwirtschaft; globalization.

Analysis: Washington, Tokyo, and Berlin are concrete models of how market-states can all promise opportunity while organizing labor, capital, welfare, and corporate power differently. Samsung and Soziale Marktwirtschaft show that these are institutional arrangements, not slogans: firms, banks, unions, and states allocate risk in different ways. Globalization becomes the domestic pressure that may push rival market-state forms into international competition.

Chapter Twenty-Five: Possible Worlds

Summary: Bobbitt sketches possible international futures for the market-state rather than predicting one. He discusses an entrepreneurial world, a mercantile world, a managerial world, regional blocs, global free trade, cyber conflict, industrial espionage, sabotage, intervention, coalition warfare, multinational corporations, NGOs, market accreditation, international law, criminal cartels, cocaine, tobacco, human rights, environmental groups, financial markets, and the possibility that private actors rather than ministries will shape legitimacy. Source anchors: possible worlds; regional blocs; industrial espionage; criminal cartels; cocaine; NGOs.

Analysis: Possible worlds is Bobbitt's way of turning Washington, Tokyo, and Berlin into alternative international orders. Regional blocs and industrial espionage show how rival domestic economic forms can become security disputes without looking like classic invasion. Criminal cartels, cocaine, and NGOs test the market-state's criteria for legitimacy, because actors with money, networks, and operational capacity may resemble public institutions without having public accountability. The scenario method makes this chapter a Speculative Governance exercise: Bobbitt uses imagined futures to discipline present institutional choices.

Chapter Twenty-Six: The Coming Age of War and Peace

Summary: Bobbitt argues that market-state war will often involve terrorism, WMD, cyber attacks, systemic collapse, intervention, and coalitions rather than only great armies. He discusses Hiroshima, Nagasaki, German nuclear failure, V-2 rockets, the Manhattan Project, Japan's intact land forces in 1945, North Korea, India, Pakistan, Iran, Iraq, nuclear proliferation, biological weapons, chemical weapons, computer viruses, financial panics, energy famines, epidemics, environmental catastrophes, refugee flows, and shared defenses. Source anchors: Hiroshima; Nagasaki; V-2; Manhattan Project; computer viruses; financial panics.

Analysis: Hiroshima, Nagasaki, V-2, and the Manhattan Project let Bobbitt connect the Long War's decisive technologies to future insecurity. Computer viruses and financial panics extend the idea of attack to systems that can collapse without a battlefield. The chapter matters because it makes intervention and shared defenses a practical answer to proliferation and systemic threats, not an optional moral add-on to old strategy. Computer viruses, financial panic, epidemics, and refugee flows make Crisis Governance the normal operating problem of market-state security.

Chapter Twenty-Seven: Peace in the Society of Market-States

Summary: Bobbitt closes the main text by arguing that a society of market-states needs legitimate institutions for collective goods, intervention, information sharing, and cooperation among states and nonstate actors. He discusses NATO, the European Union, U.N. limits, International Criminal Court debates, NGOs, multinational corporations, shared intelligence, missile defense, cyber defense, nanosensors, environmental protection, human rights, G-7, IMF, WTO, terrorism, Srebrenica, Kosovo, Iraq, Serbia, Somalia, Haiti, Panama, Afghanistan, and the need to avoid both cataclysm and paralysis. Source anchors: collective goods; NATO; International Criminal Court; shared intelligence; missile defense; Kosovo.

Analysis: Collective goods gives Bobbitt his preferred market-state answer: states must produce security, law, intelligence, and defenses that markets alone will not provide. NATO and shared intelligence are mechanisms for cooperation, while International Criminal Court debates show the unresolved legal form of that cooperation. Kosovo anchors the problem in a real intervention where sovereignty, human rights, alliance force, and legitimacy collide. Shared intelligence, missile defense, and cyber defense make Information and Coordination an institutional requirement, not just a technical improvement.

Epilogue

Summary: The epilogue returns to W. H. Auden's "The Shield of Achilles" and the image of Thetis seeing a world of barbed wire, rape, knifing, and impersonal violence rather than heroic protection. Bobbitt connects Achilles, Hephaestos, Thetis, "an artificial wilderness," the "thin-lipped armorer," the market-state, modern war, mass society, and the failure of inherited shields to protect civilians. The ending asks readers to see constitutional change as tragic as well as analytical. Source anchors: Auden; Achilles; Hephaestos; Thetis; artificial wilderness; thin-lipped armorer.

Analysis: Auden, Achilles, Hephaestos, and Thetis make the book's title literal: the shield built for the warrior displays the society that war and statecraft have made. Artificial wilderness and thin-lipped armorer strip away any romance from constitutional adaptation; the devices that protect one order can expose ordinary people to mechanized cruelty. The epilogue gives Bobbitt's legal and strategic history a moral image rather than a policy checklist.

Postscript: The Indian Summer

Summary: Written after September 11, 2001, the postscript applies the book's claims to Al Qaeda, Osama bin Laden, New York, Washington, Afghanistan, NATO, Russia, international terrorism, virtual statehood, telecommunications, transit, rapid computation, WMD, coalition warfare, shared intelligence, shared surveillance, missile defense, cyber defense, World Trade Center towers, commercial airliners, Pearl Harbor, and the phrase "Indian Summer." Bobbitt argues that September 2001 may mark the first battle of a new war for the society of market-states. Source anchors: September 11; Al Qaeda; Osama bin Laden; virtual state; NATO; Indian Summer.

Analysis: September 11 and Al Qaeda let Bobbitt test his market-state account against a nonterritorial enemy that has hierarchy, money, laws, alliances, fighters, welfare, and intelligence but no contiguous territory. Osama bin Laden and virtual state make deterrence and retaliation insufficient, because identity and location can be concealed. NATO and Indian Summer point to Bobbitt's answer: use the temporary opening after violence to build cooperative defenses before separate self-help turns into wider war.

Appendix: A Note on Eurocentrism

Summary: Bobbitt answers the charge that his history is Eurocentric by acknowledging Steven Weinberg's point that the Tai Ping Rebellion killed far more people than the Crimean War and by admitting that early chapters focus on European examples. He argues that the State is a European political idea, that the society of states emerged in Renaissance Europe, that strategic innovation mattered where European states competed, and that the form later spread to the Americas, Africa, and Asia. Source anchors: Steven Weinberg; Tai Ping Rebellion; Crimean War; European political idea; Renaissance Europe; society of states.

Analysis: Steven Weinberg and the Tai Ping Rebellion prevent Bobbitt from pretending that European wars were always the largest human disasters. Crimean War and Renaissance Europe clarify the narrower object of the book: not all violence, but the development of the State and society of states that became global. European political idea is the key qualification that keeps the history focused while admitting what it omits.

Appendix: A Note on Causality

Summary: Bobbitt rejects the idea that he has found a general historical law. He describes repeated patterns of epochal war, constitutional crisis, strategic innovation, peace settlement, and state imitation, but insists that decisions by statesmen remain real choices constrained by culture, resources, and what he calls the genetic material of the State. He cites Rey Koslowski and Friedrich Kratochwil on actors reproducing or altering systems, then names Al Qaeda, asymmetrical warfare, telecommunications, commercial airliners, the World Trade Center, and WMD as examples of new forms and tactics. Source anchors: historical law; genetic material; Rey Koslowski; Friedrich Kratochwil; asymmetrical warfare; World Trade Center.

Analysis: Historical law is rejected so the book's patterns do not become determinism. Genetic material gives Bobbitt a way to discuss constraints without denying agency, while Rey Koslowski and Friedrich Kratochwil support his claim that practices reproduce systems. Asymmetrical warfare and World Trade Center show why choices now matter: market-state technologies can produce different wars depending on the legal and strategic decisions states make.

Appendix: A Note on Periodicity

Summary: Bobbitt explains that his periods and forms are deliberately sharpened, like the black border on a hemline in a Sargent painting that dissolves when viewed up close. He names princely, kingly, territorial, imperial, national, and market forms; bureaucratic establishments; citizens' expectations; sources of legitimacy; transnational institutions; transition cases; older survivals; counterexamples; taxonomy; and the reader's possible amendments. The note defends broad categories as useful constructions rather than rigid boxes. Source anchors: Sargent painting; princely; kingly; territorial; market forms; taxonomy.

Analysis: Sargent painting is Bobbitt's metaphor for why princely, kingly, territorial, and market forms can be valid at long range even if close cases blur. Taxonomy is treated as a tool for seeing how bureaucracy, legitimacy, expectations, and institutions cluster, not as a rule that every state must fit neatly. This appendix helps police the book's own method after nearly one thousand pages of period labels.
