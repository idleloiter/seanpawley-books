Pylon

Position in the vault

This note is backed by a local extracted source and remains part of the vault's crosslinked book layer.

Detailed overview

Pylon builds its New Valois air-meet weekend out of two linked spectacles: Feinman Airport, a reclaimed lake-bottom monument covered with purple-and-gold bunting and Colonel H. I. Feinman's name, and Mardi Gras, whose confetti, Grandlieu Street crowds, newsboys, floats, and late-night drinking keep pressing against the airport story. The novel begins not with Roger Shumann in the air but with Jiggs staring at $22.50 boots he cannot pay for, counting out two dollars and ten cents, and asking the clerks to remove the boots from the window so that the object is at least provisionally his. That opening gives the book its social scale. The air race is a public civic dedication, an advertising event, a newspaper event, and a tourist entertainment, but for Shumann, Laverne, Jack Holmes, Jiggs, and the little boy it is also rent, food, train fare, shoes, cigarettes, prize percentages, and the next field where a machine can be qualified.

Faulkner's narrative logic moves through proximity rather than exposition. The unnamed reporter first sees the flying family at the hangar and then cannot stop trying to interpret them: Laverne in greasy coveralls and later in skirt and trenchcoat, Shumann coaxing performance out of obsolete racers, Holmes making delayed parachute drops with a sack of flour, Jiggs tending engines and thirst, and the boy Jack Shumann punching anyone who repeats the cruel question of his paternity. The reporter's language turns them into creatures of oil, wire, cylinders, and wing fabric, but the facts he learns keep rehumanizing them. Their arrangement is not a bohemian joke or a mere scandal; it is a travelling economy made out of racing commissions, parachute purses, hotel bills, mechanical repair, sexual loyalty, and a child who belongs to a name even when biological certainty is impossible.

The book repeatedly contrasts mechanical fluency with institutional speech. The announcer's amplified voice turns races, parachute drops, and crashes into a continuous entertainment feed. Hagood, the city editor, wants news instead of the reporter's intoxicated lyricism, yet the newspaper reduces Burnham's death, Shumann's racing, and the final recovery failure into headlines and copy. Feinman's airport committee treats Burnham's death as a printing-cost problem and later uses the language of license, public promise, and civic authority to allow Shumann to fly Matt Ord's dangerous rebuilt aircraft. Sales can say the machine is unsafe, Ord can say it should be burned like a mad dog, but Feinman, his secretary, the American Aeronautical Association setting, and the paying crowd create a path by which a licensed but unstable airplane can be put back into the air.

Shumann's flying is not romanticized as simple bravery. The first race shows his skill in exact technical terms: an obsolete ship, a three-hundred-seventy-five cubic inch class, pylons taken closer than Bullitt and Ott dare, and second money won by turns rather than straightaway speed. The fatal race repeats that pattern under worse conditions. The Ord-modified airplane has a burble in the tailgroup, a weight-distribution problem solved only by the reporter lying in the tail for a field hop and by rigging a sandbag on a cable. Shumann begins the Vaughn Trophy race wide, high, and cautious, then comes in behind Ord, and the fuselage breaks near the pylon. The lake, the seawall, the seaplane slip, the sunken mole of city refuse, the police launch, the dredgeboat, and the searchlights turn the moment of flight into a long night of salvage.

The reporter's attachment to Laverne gives the novel a second crash that is emotional rather than aerodynamic. He offers his room, gives money, borrows from Hagood, signs the note for the Ord airplane, buys ice cream for the boy, hides cash in a toy airplane, and tries to imagine himself as a household name, bed, towel, and food rather than a lover who could displace Shumann or Holmes. Laverne sees more clearly than he does. After Shumann dies, she eats at the lunchwagon, sends money toward getting the body to Ohio if it is recovered, leaves the boy with Doctor Carl Shumann, and tells the old man that she is pregnant with a child that is not Roger's. Her choice is brutal but not careless: Jack Holmes cannot earn what Shumann earned, she cannot guarantee food, clothes, or medicine, and she understands that Shumann's parents can give the boy a home only if she disappears from him.

The final section turns the title's racing object into a moral object. The pylon is a turning marker, a point around which speed and risk become measurable; it is also what the newspaper men, the reporter, Laverne, Holmes, Jiggs, Ord, Hagood, Feinman, Sales, Doctor Shumann, and the boy keep circling without being able to make one another whole. The "scavengers" are not only the gulls over the lake or the small boats around the dredge. They are also the newspapermen making jokes in the lunchstand kitchen, the copyboy salvaging sentimental copy from the reporter's wastebasket, the airport crowd feeding on spectacle, and the institutions that convert wreckage into headlines, legal permission, funeral arrangements, or civic pride. The ending refuses the reporter's ornate memorial version of Shumann and leaves instead his bitter copy, Laverne's absence in Ohio snow, and the old doctor's cry over money hidden in the child's toy airplane.

Core concepts and linkages

  • Technological Change: racing aircraft, superchargers, parachute exhibitions, airport reclamation, radio/amplifier announcements, and newspaper deadlines make aviation modernity operational rather than symbolic.
  • Moral Economy: Jiggs's boots, bus fare, prize money, lunch checks, the toy airplane cash, and Laverne's decision about the boy expose survival obligations beneath the air show's public glamour.
  • Informal Institutions: dice marriage, flying-circuit household rules, reporter favors, city-editor loans, airport waivers, and note-signing workarounds decide outcomes outside formal family, safety, and legal categories.
  • Language and Ideology: headlines, announcer patter, civic dedication language, sentimental copy, and the reporter's lyrical counter-language compete to define what the crashes mean.
  • Logistics and Throughput: buses, hangars, qualifying races, repair time, salvage boats, dredges, trains, and prize schedules determine whether people, aircraft, bodies, and money keep moving.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: the child Jack Shumann, uncertain paternity, Shumann's legal name, Doctor Shumann's household, and the destroyed toy airplane make inheritance depend on recognition rather than proof.

Section-by-section notes

Dedication of an Airport

Summary: Jiggs begins the novel outside a store window in New Valois, staring at boots priced at twenty-two and a half dollars while last night's confetti, purple-and-gold bunting, and airport placards still cover the town. He bargains with two clerks, can pay only two dollars and ten cents instead of the two dollars and twenty-five cents required for ten percent, and insists that the boots be taken out of the window before he leaves for the Bluehound bus to Feinman Airport. On the bus he identifies himself as Roger Shumann's mechanic, reads the Airport Dedication Special, studies Colonel H. I. Feinman's engraved civic self-advertisement, and rides through Grandlieu Street, liveoaks, swamp cabins, sawgrass, Lake Rambaud reclamation, and the modernistic airport whose concrete runways form twin F's. In the hangar he rejoins Shumann, Laverne, Jack Holmes, and the little boy, helps with the supercharger, watches the boy attack anyone who asks who his old man is, and sees Laverne move from greasy coveralls to skirt and shirt before the races. The section then stages the first day of competition: the American Aeronautical Association program, the 200 cubic inch dash, Shumann's 375 cubic inch race against Myers, Bullitt, Ott, and Grant, his second-place finish by taking pylons tighter than the faster ships, and Holmes's delayed parachute jump with a sack of flour as the beacon flashes the airport's F in Morse code. Source anchors: "$22.50 boots"; "Bluehound to Feinman Airport"; "Lake Rambaud"; "twin F's"; "Who’s your old man"; "375 cubic inch"; "sack of flour"; "dot-dot-dash-dot."

Analysis: The opening makes "$22.50 boots," "Bluehound to Feinman Airport," and Shumann's obsolete racer parts of the same economy of display and insolvency. Jiggs wants the store-window boots because they certify a flyer identity he cannot afford, while Feinman's plaque and "Lake Rambaud" reclamation show a city buying a monumental aviation image and stamping it with "twin F's." The hangar material gives Shumann, Laverne, Holmes, and the boy practical specificity before the reporter can mythologize them: supercharger work, grease, parachute harnesses, and "Who’s your old man" establish a household organized around machines and public risk. Shumann's "375 cubic inch" run also sets the book's technical measure of value, because he can beat Bullitt and Ott only on the pylons, where courage, skill, obsolete equipment, and economic desperation meet in one turn. The opening is therefore Technological Change seen from the mechanics' side: a new aerial infrastructure exists, but its workers experience it as debt, grease, timing, and danger.

An Evening in New Valois

Summary: The reporter returns to the newspaper office with copy about Lieutenant Frank Burnham, whose rocket-plane crash has produced the headline "FIRST FATALITY OF AIR MEET. PILOT BURNED ALIVE." Hagood, the city editor, tells him that he has an instinct for events but brings back only dead information, prompting the reporter to pour out the story of Laverne, Roger Shumann, Jack Holmes, Jiggs, and the boy born on an unrolled parachute in a California hangar. He recounts Laverne leaving an Iowa or Indiana schoolhouse, Shumann teaching her parachute work, Holmes sharing the household, the dice roll that made Shumann the legal husband, the child's name, and the family's coast-to-coast, Canada, and Mexico existence on racing commissions and parachute purses. Hagood rejects the story as news unless someone murders Laverne and the child in front of the grandstand, orders the reporter to arrive at Feinman Airport by ten the next morning, and sends him home. The reporter then wanders toward Grandlieu Street amid Mardi Gras noise, newsboys crying Burnham's death, and his own fixation on Laverne, the boy, Shumann, and Holmes. Source anchors: "FIRST FATALITY"; "Hagood"; "Lieut. Frank Burnham"; "California hangar"; "dice"; "Canada and Mexico"; "ten oclock"; "Grandlieu Street."

Analysis: This section gives the reporter his function in the book by making "Hagood" demand one kind of writing while the reporter compulsively produces another, and it is a case for Language and Ideology because the press vocabulary decides which deaths become public and in what form. "FIRST FATALITY" and "Lieut. Frank Burnham" are conventional news, but the reporter's account of the "California hangar," the "dice," Laverne's schoolhouse departure, and the "Canada and Mexico" circuit tries to turn the flying family's arrangement into a form of life the newspaper cannot print, so the printable idiom and the unofficial one sort the same events into different kinds of truth. Hagood's refusal matters because it keeps the reporter professionally attached to Feinman Airport while leaving his strongest interest unofficial and obsessive. "Grandlieu Street" and the Burnham newsboys also show how New Valois consumes death as a public cry almost immediately, preparing for the later conversion of Shumann's crash into copy, photographs, and crowd talk.

Night in the Vieux Carré

Summary: After the first day, the reporter brings Shumann, Laverne, Holmes, Jiggs, and the sleeping boy through Grandlieu Street into the Vieux Carre, carrying bags and trying to house them in his own rooms. They pass trolley bells, automobiles, serpentine, confetti, Toulouse Street, one-way arrows, narrow balcony streets, a newsboy, and a dive where the reporter buys a gallon jug of counterfeit absinthe colored by an old woman with paregoric or laudanum. Hagood later drives across town in his roadster, reaches the alley called The Drowned, shouts up at the reporter's window, and learns from Jiggs that the flying family is inside while the reporter drunkenly claims he has been fired and is hosting his "two husbands." In the room, Holmes drinks, needles the reporter about Laverne, kicks him after the reporter admits he wants to sleep with her, and the reporter eventually crawls out, leaves the key on the table, locks himself out, wanders to the Market, fails at coffee, vomits, buys a morning paper, and reads headlines into delirious pylon images. Source anchors: "Toulouse"; "New Valois absinth"; "The Drowned"; "Hagood"; "two husbands"; "bootjack"; "Market"; "FARMERS REFUSE."

Analysis: The Vieux Carre section translates the airport's public machinery into the reporter's private room, where the same pressures become drunken hospitality, sexual humiliation, and locked doors. The gallon of "New Valois absinth," "Hagood's" visit to "The Drowned," and Holmes's violence over Laverne expose how the reporter's fantasy of sheltering the group immediately becomes another unstable performance. Jiggs and the "bootjack" are crucial because they make the room physical and comic even as the reporter collapses; the boots must come off, the key must be placed on the table, the "Market" coffee must be attempted. The room is governed by Informal Institutions rather than domestic law: temporary hospitality, sexual loyalty, shame, debt, and violence set the rules before any formal relationship can name them. The delirious "FARMERS REFUSE" newspaper headings at dawn connect "Toulouse" and Grandlieu Street's printed death culture to the reporter's own mind, which can no longer separate farmers, bankers, pylons, Iowa, Laverne, and the boy.

Tomorrow

Summary: Jiggs wakes under Shumann's foot at half past eight and discovers that the reporter is missing, the key is still on the sticky table, the new boots have been scratched, and Hagood's ten o'clock message has not been delivered. Shumann, Laverne, Holmes, the boy, and Jiggs leave the room, find the reporter collapsed in the locked doorway, take six dollars and seventy cents from him for bus fare, and then let the money fall back when Holmes refuses to eat and sleep on the reporter's charity. At Feinman Airport the pilots are pulled into a committee meeting where the businessmen, the announcer Hank, and Feinman's representatives impose a two-and-a-half percent cut from contestants and paid performers to reprint programs after Burnham's death. While Shumann and Holmes lose repair time, Jiggs goes on a drinking spree, the supercharger and valves are mishandled, Holmes punches Jiggs and the reporter, Leblanc the policeman intervenes, the reporter gives Jiggs three quarters, returns the house key to Laverne, calls Hagood, and then hears over the amplifier that Shumann has engine trouble, cuts the switch, and noses over in a forced landing near the lake. Source anchors: "half past eight"; "six dollars and seventy cents"; "two and a half percent"; "Hank"; "valves"; "Leblanc"; "three quarters"; "nosed over."

Analysis: This section turns "half past eight" into a chain of practical failures rather than a clean restart. The reporter's missing body, the "six dollars and seventy cents," and Jiggs's inaccessible jug show how the previous night's charity has created new obligations that no one can manage without hurting someone else. The "two and a half percent" program levy, delivered through "Hank" and the committee, is one of the book's sharpest institutional moments: Burnham's death becomes a cost shifted onto pilots like Shumann and performers like Holmes, costing them the time needed to check "valves" and repair the ship. The levy makes the air meet a Capital Allocation problem at the smallest scale, with printing costs, repair time, prize hopes, and performer percentages competing inside one morning. Shumann's "nosed over" forced landing then follows from money, delay, mechanical neglect, and public schedule, not from a single heroic mistake, while "Leblanc" and Hagood keep the reporter tied to the city systems that he is trying to escape through the flying family.

And Tomorrow

Summary: The reporter takes Shumann to Matt Ord's neat little house and asks for a replacement airplane after the valves, longerons, and rudderpost damage have ended Shumann's original chance at the final day. Ord refuses to let Shumann fly the rebuilt Saint Louis machine because its modification produced a dangerous tailgroup burble during landing, even though Sales had approved the license and Ord himself survived a test only by jamming the stick forward and groundlooping. The reporter and Shumann then work around Ord: they borrow money from Hagood, bail Jiggs out of the Bayou Street station, go to Blaisedell, make a field hop with the reporter lying in the tail to test weight distribution, rig a sandbag on a cable, sign a note involving Shumann, the reporter, and Shumann's father, and bring the airplane back to Feinman Airport. Feinman, his secretary, Sales, Ord, the American Aeronautical Association setting, and the committee argue over whether a licensed but unsafe aircraft can be grounded; Feinman waives qualification, the secretary uses the note and license against Ord, and Shumann enters the Vaughn Trophy race against Ord's Ninety-Two, where the fuselage breaks near the pylon and Shumann goes into the lake. Source anchors: "Matt Ord"; "tailgroup burble"; "Sales"; "Blaisedell"; "sandbag"; "Feinman"; "Vaughn Trophy"; "fuselage broke."

Analysis: This section is the mechanical and legal core of the novel because Shumann's death is built out of named decisions by "Matt Ord," "Sales," "Feinman," the secretary, Shumann, and the reporter, and it is a case for Informal Institutions because the crash is produced by workarounds that the formal rules exist to prevent. Ord's account of the "tailgroup burble" gives the machine a precise defect, while the "Blaisedell" field hop and "sandbag" convert that defect into a workaround that lets Shumann believe he can manage the airplane. Feinman's use of the license, the waived qualification, and the note shifts responsibility away from caution and toward public spectacle: the American Aeronautical Association setting and the paying crowd create a path by which a licensed but unstable aircraft is put back into the air through negotiated exceptions rather than through a safety ruling anyone is willing to own. When the "fuselage broke" in the "Vaughn Trophy" race, Shumann's earlier skill at pylons returns in fatal form: he is close enough to challenge Ord, experienced enough to choose the empty lake over the crowded land, and trapped in a machine everyone has already argued about.

Lovesong of J. A. Prufrock

Summary: After Shumann's crash, the beach between the boulevard and the seaplane slip fills with police, newspapermen, airport officials, small boats, oystermen, searchlights, a police launch, and a dredgeboat trying to locate the airplane near the sunken mole made of paving shards, fallen walls, and discarded automobile bodies. Jug the photographer has plates of the crash, the reporter phones in details about the fuselage, the tail, the parachute that could not have opened, the cockpit hatch, the lake bottom, crabs, gars, and the probable need for a diver, but he cannot absorb Laverne's command that he go away. Ord finds him, burns the note signed for the aircraft, calls him a bastard, and tells him to get Laverne away if he can. The reporter finds Jiggs in the hangar eating with his injured face and shiny boots, sends him to pay Laverne's lunchwagon check, borrows more money from Hagood at a golf course, helps Jiggs pawn the boots and buy a toy airplane, sandwiches, candy, Boy's Life, The Ladies' Home Journal, and an air-war pulp magazine, hides one hundred seventy-five dollars inside the toy, and watches the train leave with Laverne, Holmes, and the boy. Source anchors: "seaplane slip"; "police launch"; "dredgeboat"; "sunken mole"; "Jug"; "burns the note"; "toy aeroplane"; "$175."

Analysis: The Prufrock title fits the reporter's paralysis, but the section is grounded in salvage labor and small transactions: "seaplane slip", "dredgeboat", "sunken mole", "burns the note", and "toy aeroplane" mark the passage from public recovery work to private attempted repair. Shumann's body is not recoverable in any dignified immediate way, so everyone handles substitutes: copy for Hagood, plates for "Jug", a lunchwagon check for Laverne, a destroyed note for Ord, and gifts for the boy. The reporter's love for Laverne becomes practical only when Jiggs helps convert cash into an object the child will carry, and Jiggs's sale of the boots reverses the opening store-window desire. The train departure leaves the reporter outside the family he tried to join, while "$175." inside the toy becomes a delayed Moral Economy message that only Ohio will decode.

The Scavengers

Summary: At midnight the dredge and police launch abandon the lake search, the newspapermen retreat to a lunchstand kitchen with Coca-Cola, whiskey, cards, and blackjack, and the reporter, without an overcoat, refuses to drink while the others speculate crudely about Shumann, Laverne, Holmes, the boy, money, burial, and where the family will go. When one reporter guesses that Laverne may take the boy to Shumann's relatives and leave him there, the reporter lies that Shumann was an orphan, then runs through the Terrebonne and the Bienville Hotel to find Jiggs and confirm the destination: Myron, Ohio, Doctor Carl Shumann, and the boy's grandparents. At dawn an airplane drops a wreath far from the likely crash site, gulls gather, the reporter resumes drinking, and the narrative follows Laverne, Holmes, and the boy through snow to Myron, where Holmes waits at the station and Laverne leaves the sleeping child with Doctor Shumann. She tells the doctor that she is pregnant with a child that is not Roger's, that the two thousand dollars would have settled them, and that most of the first day's prize has been left to send Roger's body to Ohio if it is found. Doctor Shumann destroys the toy airplane, discovers the hidden money, argues with his wife over whether Roger or Laverne hid it, burns it, claims the boy, and Hagood later reads both the copyboy's sentimental "Last Pylon" reconstruction and the reporter's bitter final copy weighted by an empty whiskey bottle. Source anchors: "midnight"; "lunchstand kitchen"; "Myron, Ohio"; "Doctor Carl Shumann"; "not Roger's"; "first day's prize"; "Last Pylon"; "empty whiskey bottle."

Analysis: The last section identifies the scavengers as readers, reporters, institutions, and grieving survivors who all try to take usable meaning from Shumann's wreck after "midnight." The "lunchstand kitchen" talk about Laverne, Holmes, money, burial, and the boy forces the reporter to protect the family with a lie about Shumann being an orphan, but Jiggs's "Myron, Ohio" answer proves that the reporters' cruel guess is partly right. Laverne's conversation with "Doctor Carl Shumann" gives the book's economic facts their final human weight: the two thousand dollars, the "first day's prize," the unborn child "not Roger's," and the need for food and medicine explain the choice without making it gentle. The destroyed toy airplane and burned $175 complete the movement from Jiggs's boots to the child's inheritance, while the "Last Pylon" copy and the "empty whiskey bottle" show the reporter rejecting both sentimental news language and the possibility of clean mourning. Jack's transfer to Myron is Legitimacy and Succession reduced to a household crisis: Shumann's name, not biological certainty, makes the old doctor claim the boy.
