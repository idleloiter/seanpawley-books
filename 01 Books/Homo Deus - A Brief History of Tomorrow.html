Detailed overview

Harari builds Homo Deus as a sequel to the history of Sapiens: once famine, plague, and war have been converted from divine punishments into technical problems, the successful animal begins looking for a new agenda. The opening chapter moves through Beauvais in 1694, the Black Death, smallpox in Mexico and Hawaii, the World Health Organization's eradication campaign, the Great Leap Forward, obesity statistics, terrorist spectaculars, and Google's Calico project to show that modern power comes from treating catastrophe as something measurable, funded, engineered, and administered. The same move introduces the book's three future projects: life extension, biochemical happiness, and godlike upgrading.

The first part then turns backward and asks what kind of creature earned the power to set those projects. Harari refuses a heroic human story. He describes the Anthropocene through extinct megafauna, domesticated pigs and chickens, Nayaka animism, the Garden of Eden, Gadhimai sacrifices, Harlow's monkeys, and industrial meat production, making human supremacy look like a system for turning other sentient beings into raw material. The next chapter tests the usual defenses of human specialness, especially the soul, consciousness, fairness, and intelligence, before settling on the capacity to create intersubjective realities such as money, nations, gods, and corporations.

The middle of the book explains how these intersubjective realities became large enough to rule continents. Sumerian temple gods, Egyptian pharaohs, Lake Fayum, the papyrus bureaucracy, Aristides de Sousa Mendes in Bordeaux, Mao's grain reports, Nyerere's collective farms, the Berlin Conference, schools, marks, and scriptures all show a recurring mechanism: fiction becomes durable when writing, money, office procedure, and ritual make millions of people behave as though the same paper reality is real. Harari's target is not fiction as such; his test is suffering. Gods, states, currencies, and corporations matter as tools, but they cannot feel pain. Pigs, peasants, patients, and soldiers can.

The book then describes the modern settlement between science and humanism. Science supplies power but not values; humanism supplies values by relocating authority from God, scripture, dynasty, or natural law into human experience. Harari makes this relocation concrete through the adulterous woman who now talks to a therapist rather than a priest, Rousseau's Emile, gay parades in Jerusalem, Charlie Hebdo, democratic elections, Duchamp's Fountain, Toyota customers, Professor Leif Andersson's enhanced chickens, Beethoven and Chuck Berry, Otto Dix's war paintings, Angela Merkel and Reem, liberalism, socialism, evolutionary humanism, and the liberal recovery after 1989. The result is an order in which the voter, customer, and viewer are supposed to know best.

The final part attacks the factual assumptions beneath that order. Neuroscience and behavioral economics turn the free individual into a composite of genes, hormones, neurons, split hemispheres, robo-rats, transcranial helmets, the experiencing self, and the narrating self. Automation then turns the same attack into institutional pressure: drones, cyber-wars, Google's self-driving cars, algorithmic trading, Watson, robotic pharmacists, Mindojo, David Cope's EMI and Annie, Oxford job-risk estimates, Facebook Likes, Waze, Cortana, Kindle telemetry, Angelina Jolie's BRCA1 decision, and $3,000 genetic testing all show authority passing from self-report to external calculation.

Harari closes by considering two possible heirs to liberal humanism. Techno-humanism still wants to preserve a superior human being, but it risks engineering only the mental abilities wanted by armies, markets, and bureaucracies while neglecting smell, dreams, doubt, attention, empathy, and other forms of consciousness we barely understand. Dataism goes further, treating organisms and machines as data-processing systems and valuing anything by its contribution to data flow. Its lineage runs from Darwin and Turing through Hayek, the Soviet bread queue, Lysenko, the Internet, Aaron Swartz, smart car-pools, Wikipedia, the Internet-of-All-Things, Google, Facebook, DNA sequencing, and machine learning. The book ends not with a prophecy but with three questions about organisms as algorithms, intelligence without consciousness, and algorithms that know humans better than humans know themselves.

Major people, societies, and motivations

  • Yuval Noah Harari (author-narrator): Israeli historian who writes the book as a big-history narrative that dates, quantifies, and names examples from Sumer, Egypt, and the crusades to Silicon Valley. His stated method is deliberately anti-prophetic: history is studied "not in order to predict the future, but to free yourself of the past," and his predictions are framed as ways to discuss present choices rather than destiny.
  • Homo sapiens: the collective protagonist and antagonist of the book. Harari narrates humanity as the animal that conquered famine, plague, and war through flexible large-scale cooperation on shared fictions, and that is now pursuing immortality, bliss, and divinity in order to upgrade itself into Homo deus — while warning that the projects of upgrading may make ordinary humans functionally irrelevant.
  • Liberal humanism: the modern society the book scrutinizes. Its doctrine — the voter knows best, the customer is always right, beauty is in the eye of the beholder, if it feels good do it — relocates authority from God and scripture into human experience, and is the creed whose factual foundations Harari's final part tries to demolish.
  • Socialist and evolutionary humanism: the two rival sects of the humanist schism. Karl Marx and Vladimir Lenin anchor socialism ("Communism is power to worker councils, plus electrification of the whole country"), treating the party and the trade union as the knowers; Adolf Hitler anchors evolutionary humanism, treating war as the school of life, the trench as a source of political authority, and natural selection as the mandate for supermen.
  • Fritz, Duncan, and Yankele (invented dialogues): Harari's three fictional boys who ask their fathers why the rules hold — why Jews may not eat pork, why Nazis killed the Jews, why Britons should defend Muslim rights. The dialogues reduce religion to its functional core: any creed that gives superhuman legitimacy to human order, from Leviticus to natural selection to human rights.
  • Martin Luther: the spiritual truth-seeker of the chapter on the odd couple. His Ninety-Five Theses, nailed at Wittenberg on 31 October 1517 against Johannes Tetzel's indulgences, stand for spirituality as a journey that rebels against institutional religion — even though his revolt ended by founding new institutions.
  • Lorenzo Valla: the Catholic priest and linguist who proved in 1441 that the Donation of Constantine was forged, by showing it used words unknown in fourth-century Latin and a consular date that never occurred (30 March, Constantine's fourth consulship in 315, combined with Gallicanus's first consulship in 317). He is Harari's model of science checking religion's factual claims rather than its values.
  • Jean-Jacques Rousseau: author of Émile, the "eighteenth-century bible of feeling." His line that he finds the rules of conduct "in the depths of my heart" is the founding quotation for humanism's turn inward.
  • Aristides de Sousa Mendes: Portuguese consul in Bordeaux in 1940 who issued thousands of visas against orders, helping spirit up to 30,000 people out of Nazi-occupied France; the positive face of paper reality — a rubber stamp outrunning the Portuguese Foreign Ministry's will.
  • Mao Zedong: his Great Leap Forward (1958–61) is Harari's case of writing corrupting reality into the worst famine in history, as officials exaggerated grain reports by a stroke of a pen and the government sold the rice the country needed to eat.
  • Julius Nyerere: the idealistic Tanzanian president who copied Mao's model with Ujamaa collective farms — villages that existed mainly in government documents in Dar es Salaam — and turned "Africa's biggest food exporter" into a net importer within a decade.
  • Angela Merkel and Reem: Merkel's 17 July 2015 confrontation with the teenage Palestinian refugee in Germany, whose tears and her firm "politics can be tough" reply, followed by her u-turn on asylum, is Harari's case that liberalism has no settled way to weigh conflicting feelings (refugees' hopes vs anxious German parents).
  • Otto Dix and Tom Lea: the twentieth-century war artists — Dix's The War (1929–32) and Lea's That 2,000 Yard Stare (1944) — whose feeling-subject paintings Harari opposes to Jean-Jacques Walter's and Pieter Snayers' Olympian battle canvases; the shift from war-as-tactics to war-as-experience is his proof that humanism governs perception itself.
  • Nicolae Ceauşescu and Ion Iliescu: the Bucharest square demonstration of 21 December 1989, where the live-transmitted booing of Ceauşescu toppled a state in a few minutes — only for the masses to lose power to Iliescu and the National Salvation Front, because organizing, not numbers, wins revolutions.
  • Woodrow Wilson and Charles W. Eliot: Wilson's 1918 Senate argument for women's suffrage and Harvard president Eliot's 1917 claim that "democratic armies fight better" make the liberal point that universal rights were proclaimed at the same historical juncture as universal conscription — rights were attached to bodies that armies and factories needed.
  • Professor Sanjiv Talwar: the State University of New York robo-rat researcher whose remote-controlled rats "work for pleasure" — the chapter's proof that a manipulated desire feels indistinguishable from a free one from the inside.
  • Sally Adee: the New Scientist journalist who tested the US air force's transcranial stimulator in a sniper simulator, described the silence of her inner voices as a "near-spiritual experience," and asked who she was apart from "the angry bitter gnomes that populate my mind."
  • Roger Wolcott Sperry and Michael Gazzaniga: the split-brain researchers whose patients — the teenage draughtsman who answered "automobile race" in Scrabble tiles, patient WJ fighting his own hand, and PS who explained the snow shovel as "you need a shovel to clean out the chicken shed" — show the left brain's internal interpreter inventing plausible stories it does not actually know.
  • Daniel Kahneman and Donald Redelmeier: their 14°C cold-water experiment, in which 80 per cent of volunteers chose to repeat a longer, worse trial, and their study of 154 colonoscopy patients in Toronto established the peak-end rule and split the self into the experiencing self and the duration-blind, story-spinning narrating self.
  • David Cope: the UC Santa Cruz musicology professor whose programs EMI and Annie compose Bach-style chorales and haiku; at a public challenge in Oregon, audiences judged EMI's piece to be genuine Bach and Larson's human piece to be computer-made — the case that artistic sanctuary is not safe from algorithms.
  • Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne: Oxford researchers whose 2013 study "The Future of Employment" estimated that 47 per cent of US jobs are at high risk of automation by 2033 — the book's standard statistic for the coming "useless class."
  • Ray Kurzweil, Aubrey de Grey, Bill Maris, and Peter Thiel: the Silicon Valley immortality lobby. Kurzweil joined Google as a director of engineering in 2012 and Google launched Calico ("to solve death") in 2013; Maris told a January 2015 interviewer that living to 500 is possible and that Google Ventures was investing 36 per cent of its $2 billion portfolio in life sciences; Thiel announced he prefers to "fight" death.
  • Angelina Jolie: her 14 May 2013 New York Times essay about a double mastectomy after a BRCA1 test showed an 87 per cent probability of breast cancer is the book's demonstration that authority is already shifting from feeling to genetic number-crunching.
  • Aaron Swartz: Dataism's "first martyr," whose 2011 JSTOR download via MIT, his "Guerilla Open Access Manifesto," his prosecution, and his suicide on 11 January 2013 supply the new religion's founding saint and its novel value, freedom of information.
  • Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine, and Ara Norenzayan: their 2010 survey of psychology's leading journals found that in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 96 per cent of sampled individuals were WEIRD (Western, educated, industrialised, rich, democratic) — the evidence that science's map of the mind covers an unrepresentative sliver.
  • Thomas Nagel: his 1974 article "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" is Harari's proof that no algorithm about echolocation can deliver the subjective experience of being a bat — a limit on both techno-humanism's upgrade project and Dataism's reductionism.
  • Giuseppe Mazzini: the nineteenth-century liberal nationalist who celebrated each nation's unique communal experience alongside each individual's — Harari's bridge from liberal individualism to nationalism and its step from uniqueness to superiority.
  • Societies as protagonists: Sumer's temple-gods (Enki and Inanna as brands and legal persons in Uruk, Lagash, and Shurupak), pharaonic Egypt (the living-god pharaoh and the crocodile god Sobek organising the Lake Fayum project), the Soviet Union (centralised data processing, Lysenko's Siberian wheat, the London bread queue), Nazi Germany (evolutionary humanism), modern China and Japan (the growth creed substituting for ideology), and Silicon Valley (where the next religions are being brewed).

Themes and concepts to track

  • The modern covenant: humans trade cosmic meaning for power — "shit happens" replaces providence, and the universe has no script but no limits either.
  • Intersubjective reality: gods, nations, corporations, and money exist only in shared imagination yet rule the objective world; the test of their reality is whether they can suffer.
  • The religion question: religion defined functionally as superhuman legitimacy, so liberalism, communism, Nazism, and AI-worship all count as religions.
  • The three paths of human upgrading: biological engineering, cyborg engineering, and engineering of non-organic beings.
  • Intelligence decoupling from consciousness: intelligence becomes cheap and mandatory, consciousness becomes expensive and optional.
  • Organisms as algorithms: emotions as biochemical data processing, which dissolves the line between organic brains and electronic circuits.
  • The knowing system: external algorithms (Google, Facebook, Waze, Cortana) coming to know the user better than the user knows themselves, shifting authority from self-report to calculation.
  • The useless class: humans losing economic and military value, and the elite temptation to upgrade only the first-class carriages.
  • Techno-humanism vs Dataism: one still wants a superior human, the other retires Homo sapiens like the horse when the car arrived.
  • History as liberation: studying the past not to predict but to loosen the grip of inherited scripts — feudalism, lawns, and humanism alike.

Core concepts

  • Artificial Intelligence: the book's central technology. Watson, Deep Blue, Google's autonomous cars, algorithmic trading, Mindojo, Mattersight, and the 90-per-cent-versus-50-per-cent lung-cancer statistic all show non-conscious intelligence outperforming conscious humans; the key move is that "intelligence is decoupling from consciousness," so armies and corporations want intelligence but treat consciousness as optional.
  • Speculative Governance: the question animating the whole book — who holds authority once the voter, customer, and viewer stop knowing best. The Waze trajectory (oracle → agent → sovereign) and the three threats to liberalism (uselessness, collective-but-not-individual value, upgraded elites) are its governing examples; techno-humanism and Dataism are its candidate successor religions.
  • Intelligence: the book redefines intelligence as the thing that lets humans cooperate and later be outperformed. Intelligence is shown to be neither the soul nor consciousness nor toolmaking (clever animals and clever machines both have it), and the urgent question becomes whether intelligence or consciousness is worth more once they separate.
  • Language and Ideology: the engine of the book — Sapiens are unique because they alone create intersubjective realities through language, from Sumerian goddess loans to the Euro and "the infidel." Ideology is treated as a myth-system that organizes large numbers (priests, scripts, marks, the Berlin Congress) and that science alone cannot replace.
  • Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy: humanism is read as a culture and identity-system (the feeling self, the sectarian wars between liberal, socialist, and evolutionary humanism), and legitimacy is relocated from God and dynasty into human experience — with the three-way leg (inner voice, party, or natural selection) unresolved.
  • Political Economy: the growth creed as religion, credit as trust in the future (bankers versus vampire bats), capitalism and communism read as distributed versus centralised data-processing systems, and the "useless class" of labor rendered redundant by algorithms as the twenty-first century's political-economy problem.
  • Technological Change: biotech, genetic engineering (three-parent embryos, designer babies), brain–computer interfaces, and instruction algorithms as forces that convert inherited doom into engineered projects — immortality, bliss, and divinity as the new agenda, with medicine's healing-to-upgrading slide (Viagra, plastic surgery) as the mechanism.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: the functional definition (superhuman legitimacy) that makes the odd couple of science and religion a working partnership: science supplies power, religion supplies order, and the "modern covenant" between them is what is coming apart.
  • Commercial Society: the customer as supreme authority ("the customer is always right"), growth as the number one commandment, free-market capitalism as a win–win reframing of the zero-sum pie, and the supermarket/yang-yin of modernity.
  • Platform Governance: Google, Facebook, Waze, Cortana, and the Internet-of-All-Things as the new sovereigns; the shift of authority from individuals to networked algorithms that classify, predict, and steer — including Facebook's Likes study (70 Likes beat friends, 300 Likes beat spouses) and Kindle telemetry.
  • Information and Coordination: writing and money as the technologies that broke the brain's data-processing limits and let fictions scale (pharaoh's papyri, marks, scriptures); in Part III the same lens turns democracy and dictatorship into competing data-processing systems where elections, parties, and parliaments are judged by processing efficiency.
  • State Capacity: the state as an intersubjective fiction that forces reality to fit its documents — Mao's grain reports, Nyerere's paper villages, the Berlin-drawn African borders, school marks — while in Part III governments are "overwhelmed by data" and politics becomes mere administration.
  • Legibility: the chapter 4 test — "Can it suffer?" — makes the reduction of people, peasants, and animals to written records an ethical problem; paper reality becomes dangerous when the meter, the mark, and the census replace the measurable body.
  • Ecological Constraint: the book's warning that growth is a race against ecological collapse — the International School of Beijing's $5 million dome, Kyoto 1997 and Paris 2015, and the hi-tech Noah's Ark for the rich being "one of the biggest threats to the future of humankind."

Chapter-by-chapter notes

1 The New Human Agenda

Summary: Harari opens at the dawn of the third millennium by arguing that famine, plague, and war have lost their old status as uncontrollable necessities. He moves from Beauvais in April 1694 and the French famine of 1692-94 to Estonia, Finland, the Great Leap Forward, and the claim that since 1974 China has been free of famine; he then contrasts 850 million malnourished people with 2.1 billion overweight people and says obesity killed more than famine and malnutrition in 2010. Plague receives the same treatment through the Black Death, Yersinia pestis, Francisco de Eguia's smallpox in Cempoallan and Tenochtitlan, Captain Cook's Hawaii, Spanish Flu, AIDS, SARS, Ebola, teixobactin, and the WHO's smallpox eradication in 1979. War is recast through 2012 mortality figures, suicide, diabetes, cyberwar, Rwanda's coltan mines, Silicon Valley, terrorism, and 9/11. Only after this inventory does Harari introduce the new agenda of ecological danger, immortality, happiness, and divinity, naming Aubrey de Grey, Ray Kurzweil, Google Calico, Bill Maris, Peter Thiel, lawns, Marx, Nixon, Thatcher, and the study of history as a way to loosen inherited scripts. Source anchors: Beauvais famine; Francisco de Eguia; WHO smallpox; 2012 mortality; Google Calico; lawns; Chekhov Law.

Analysis: The Beauvais famine, Francisco de Eguia, WHO smallpox, and 2012 mortality figures do the same job in different domains: they turn inherited dread into a comparative ledger that modern institutions can manage, which is the core claim of technological change — catastrophe converted from divine script into funded engineering. Google Calico, Bill Maris, Kurzweil and Thiel then convert death from a metaphysical destiny into a venture-backed problem, which is why the chapter can move from famine and war to immortality without changing its basic logic. Because the three projects (immortality, bliss, divinity) each require deciding which humans count and who controls the upgrade, they are speculative governance problems before they are medical ones. The lawns example and the discussion of Marx, Nixon, and Thatcher explain why Harari writes history here: not to predict Europe in 2050, but to show that arrangements that feel natural, such as suburban grass or capitalist economics, were made by people and can therefore be remade by people with new tools. The war section's confidence that nuclear weapons made great-power war "inconceivable" is precisely the claim the fictional scenario of The 2020 Commission Report on the North Korean Nuclear Attacks Against the United States exists to stress-test.

2 The Anthropocene

Summary: The second chapter measures human power from the viewpoint of other animals. Harari contrasts wild wolves, lions, African buffalo, and penguins with hundreds of millions of dogs, cats, cows, and chickens, then treats the last 70,000 years as an Anthropocene in which Sapiens, ships, planes, oil tankers, rats, wheat, sheep, and influenza broke older ecological boundaries. He uses Australian extinctions, American megafauna losses, mammoths, rabbits, and Stone Age hunters to show that the damage began before factories. The chapter then compares animist relations with animals among the Nayaka, Danny Naveh's elephant who always walks alone, the serpent and Bil'am's donkey in the Bible, Eve as a possible Semitic snake-word, Rainbow Serpent stories, and the Genesis attack on forager religion. Domestication becomes a story of pigs in gestation crates, Stanley Curtis's joystick pigs Hamlet and Omelette, Harry Harlow's cloth mother monkeys, John Watson, milk and meat industries, the Jerusalem Temple, Gadhimai in Nepal, Utnapishtim, Noah, Rabbi Yehuda HaNasi, Jain and Buddhist compassion, Nestle research, Newton at Woolsthorpe, and industrial farms using vaccines, hormones, air conditioning, and automatic feeders. Source anchors: Anthropocene; Nayaka elephant; gestation crates; Hamlet and Omelette; Harlow monkeys; Gadhimai; Nestle R&D.

Analysis: The Anthropocene anchor matters because it makes human domination geological rather than merely moral or political: the extract's own arithmetic (about 200,000 wild wolves against more than 400 million domesticated dogs, 50 million penguins against 20 billion chickens) turns the relationship into a counting problem studied under intelligence — whose mental life counts at all. The Nayaka elephant, Bil'am's donkey, and Rainbow Serpent stories preserve a memory of animal personhood that the agricultural and biblical order suppresses, while gestation crates, Hamlet and Omelette, and Harlow monkeys supply evidence that domesticated animals retain inherited needs even when industrial farms meet their biological maintenance requirements. Gadhimai, the Jerusalem Temple, and Nestle R&D connect religion, sacrifice, science, and commerce: each is part of the "agricultural deal" that lets humans treat sentient animals as raw material while justifying the hierarchy through language and ideology — a system that becomes the modern order of culture, identity, and legitimacy, and that prepares the question of how superhuman algorithms might treat ordinary humans.

3 The Human Spark

Summary: Harari asks what, if anything, makes Sapiens superior to other animals, and he rejects the soul as incompatible with evolution. He cites Gallup polling on American beliefs about evolution, contrasts Darwin with relativity and quantum mechanics, explains why an indivisible soul cannot emerge through gradual mutation, and then turns to the poorly understood relation between consciousness and intelligence. Brain scans, electrodes, Bill Clinton neurons, Homer Simpson neurons, traffic jams, Wall Street, and rain clouds all show how hard it is to explain subjective experience as a by-product of data processing. The chapter then tests animal minds and human cooperation through Frans de Waal's capuchin cucumber-and-grape experiment, the Ultimatum Game, Frederick II of Prussia's invasion of Silesia in 1740, ancient Egypt, John the crusader after Hattin in 1187, the Pope, Godfrey of Bouillon, minstrels, Myanmar's cancelled banknotes under Ne Win, the USSR's Belavezha Accords on 8 December 1991, North and South Korea, Google, the European Union, and Dreamtime. Source anchors: Gallup evolution; indivisible soul; Homer Simpson neuron; capuchin grapes; Frederick II; John the crusader; Belavezha Accords; Dreamtime.

Analysis: Gallup's polls on evolution, the indivisibility-of-the-soul argument, and the Homer Simpson neuron let Harari remove the most familiar religious and mental answers to human uniqueness, while leaving intelligence itself underdetermined: by most definitions humans were already the most intelligent toolmakers a million years ago, yet stayed ecologically insignificant. The capuchin cucumber-and-grape experiment and the Ultimatum Game narrow the fairness and emotion gap with other primates, while Frederick II, John the crusader, the Belavezha Accords, and Dreamtime identify the specific human power that remains — not soul or mind, but the flexible large-scale cooperation that lives in language and ideology: imagined orders such as money, nations, gods, and corporations that can outlive individual bodies and rewrite material life. The chapter's aside that North and South Korea differ not by genes or climate but because the north is dominated by "very different fictions" is a direct point of contact with the official-identity thesis of The Cleanest Race.

4 The Storytellers

Summary: This chapter explains how shared fictions became administratively powerful. Harari distinguishes objective, subjective, and intersubjective reality, then shows how Sumerian gods such as Enki and Inanna operated like brands or legal persons owning fields, slaves, loans, dams, canals, salaries, and temples in Uruk, Lagash, and Shurupak. Writing and money broke the limits of biological memory, allowing pharaohs, papyri, scribes, Memphis, Nubia, Elvis, Madonna, Justin Bieber, and Egyptian bureaucracy to coordinate huge numbers of people. The Lake Fayum project under Senusret III and Amenemhat III, Crocodilopolis, Sobek, Petsuchos, and 50 billion cubic metres of water demonstrate what an algorithmic society could build without wheels or iron. Harari then follows paper reality into Aristides de Sousa Mendes's visas in Bordeaux in 1940, Mao's false grain reports during the Great Leap Forward, Julius Nyerere's Ujamaa villages in Tanzania, the Berlin Conference of 1884, school marks, Oxford, Shakespeare, holy scripture, Nebuchadnezzar, Cyrus, Herodotus, Thucydides, Sima Qian, and the test that real entities can suffer. Source anchors: Enki and Inanna; Lake Fayum; Crocodilopolis; Sousa Mendes; Mao grain reports; Ujamaa villages; Berlin Conference; suffering test.

Analysis: Enki and Inanna show that corporations and states are not modern oddities but old intersubjective tools with offices, property, and debts, so the growth of paper reality is the growth of information and coordination beyond biological memory: writing and money let pharaoh, Elvis, and the EU exist as brands that outlive their bodies. Lake Fayum and Crocodilopolis demonstrate the constructive side of algorithmic order — an artificial lake of 50 billion cubic metres built without wheels or iron by networked scribes — while Sousa Mendes, Mao's grain reports, Nyerere's paper villages, and the Berlin Conference of 1884 show how documents can save lives, distort harvests, move peasants, or impose borders, a theme central to legibility and to state capacity. The suffering test — "Can it suffer?" — then turns fiction into an ethical problem: Harari does not tell readers to abandon fictions, but insists that paper realities be judged by what they do to bodies capable of pain.

5 The Odd Couple

Summary: Harari argues that modern science does not simply defeat religion; it needs meaning-producing stories and often strengthens them. He defines religion as any system that gives superhuman legitimacy to human order, so Jewish pork taboos, an SS officer named Fritz defending Aryan selection, and a liberal MP named Duncan defending Muslim rights can all be religious in this technical sense. Spirituality is separated from religion through the Zen warning to kill the Buddha on the road and Martin Luther's revolt against indulgences, Johannes Tetzel, Wittenberg, and the Ninety-Five Theses on 31 October 1517. Science enters when facts cannot by themselves decide values, as in the Three Gorges Dam on the Yangtze, the Chinese river dolphin, abortion disputes, !Kung and Inuit naming practices, the Donation of Constantine, Lorenzo Valla's 1441 exposure, the Uganda Anti-Homosexuality Act of 20 December 2013, Leviticus, Deuteronomy under Josiah, and later Second Temple rabbis. The chapter closes by asking why truth often loses to order, comparing early modern European violence with relative toleration in Cairo and Istanbul, and identifying modernity as a pact between scientific power and humanist religion. Source anchors: Fritz SS officer; Duncan MP; Wittenberg 1517; Three Gorges Dam; Lorenzo Valla; Uganda Act; Cairo and Istanbul.

Analysis: Fritz, Duncan, and Yankele make the definition of religion functional rather than devotional — any system that confers superhuman legitimacy on human order counts, so liberal rights and Nazi selection laws are analyzed with the same tools as pork taboos, engaging language and ideology and religion and legitimacy simultaneously. Wittenberg 1517 and Lorenzo Valla show two different attacks on institutional authority, one spiritual and one philological, but the Three Gorges Dam and the Uganda Anti-Homosexuality Act reveal that better facts still need a value source before they can guide action — science can falsify the claims about Leviticus' provenance but not the judgment that humans ought to obey God. The Cairo-and-Istanbul comparison sharpens the institutional point that religion prizes order and science prizes power rather than truth, which is why the odd couple can cooperate and why the "deal" the next chapter names is both necessary and fragile.

6 The Modern Covenant

Summary: The sixth chapter names the modern bargain: humans give up belief in a cosmic plan and receive unprecedented power. Harari contrasts older scripted worlds with the modern claim that things happen without divine meaning, then explains how science and economic growth became partners through credit, trust in the future, and the belief that the pie can expand. He moves from medieval dysentery cures to Ebola shares in Tekmira and BioCryst, vampire bats that loan blood without interest, India's population growth, Pakistan, Egypt, Congo, Myanmar, Soviet Five Year Plans, Narendra Modi's Gujarat, Recep Tayyip Erdogan's Justice and Development Party, Shinzo Abe's Abenomics, Deng Xiaoping's cat, Singapore ministerial salaries, Mexican carers, capitalism's command to reinvest profits, Minecraft, Settlers of Catan, and Civilization. The ecological bill appears through raw materials, energy, knowledge, steam engines, computers, Beijing pollution, the International School of Beijing's $5 million dome, greenhouse-emissions rates, Kyoto in 1997, Paris in 2015, and the image of a high-tech Noah's Ark. Source anchors: modern covenant; Tekmira Ebola; vampire bats; Deng Xiaoping; Singapore salaries; International School dome; Kyoto 1997; Noah's Ark.

Analysis: The modern covenant anchor explains why power and anxiety arrive together: a world without a cosmic plan can pursue cures, engines, and growth, but it cannot borrow meaning from destiny, so it must manufacture trust in the future through credit — Tekmira's and BioCryst's soaring Ebola shares against the medieval response of prayer. Vampire bats, Deng Xiaoping's cat, Singapore's GDP-pegged ministerial salaries, and civilization-style games turn commercial society from a financial habit into a shared creed spanning markets, communist parties, and developmental states, which is the growth religion Harari analyzes through political economy. The International School of Beijing's $5 million dome, the unchanged trajectory of emissions, Kyoto 1997, Paris 2015, and the hi-tech Noah's Ark expose the weakness of that creed: knowledge may expand the pie, but the atmosphere and the class structure may still turn growth into an escape vessel for those rich enough to board — the ecological constraint the creed prefers to discount.

7 The Humanist Revolution

Summary: Harari explains how modernity avoided meaninglessness by making humanity, rather than God, the source of authority. He starts with God-fearing Syria and the atheist Netherlands, then contrasts a medieval adulterous woman consulting a priest with a modern woman consulting a friend, therapist, Freud, Jung, or the DSM about how she feels. Rousseau's Emile, marriage for love, homosexuality debates, the Jerusalem gay parade, Charlie Hebdo on 7 January 2015, the Egyptian Journalists Syndicate, democratic voters, Pope Urban II, the Wars of the Roses, Clovis's ampulla at Rheims, Gregorian chant, Marcel Duchamp's Fountain, Toyota's perfect car, Leif Andersson's enhanced farm animals, Panda Dung tea, Wilhelm von Humboldt, Joyce's Ulysses, Survivor, the Tin Man, Captain Kirk, Huckleberry Finn, Otto Dix, Tom Lea, Angela Merkel and Reem, liberalism, socialist humanism, evolutionary humanism, Occupy Wall Street, China, radical Islam, Muhammad Ahmad the Mahdi, Dayananda Saraswati, Pope Pius IX, Hong Xiuquan, Marx, Lenin, electricity, Google, Michel Foucault, and Donna Haraway all appear in a long account of humanism's rise and vulnerability. Source anchors: Rousseau Emile; Jerusalem gay parade; Duchamp Fountain; Panda Dung tea; Otto Dix; Merkel and Reem; Marx and Lenin; Silicon Valley.

Analysis: Rousseau's Emile, the therapist who asks "how do you feel about what happened?", and the Jerusalem gay parade — whose religious opponents object that the parade "hurts our feelings" rather than that God forbids it — show authority moving inward into feelings even where religious actors remain present, which is culture, identity, and legitimacy rebuilt around the voter, customer, and viewer who know best. Duchamp's Fountain, Toyota, Professor Leif Andersson's enhanced animals, Panda Dung tea, Otto Dix and Tom Lea extend that same source of authority into aesthetics, markets, education, and war, so the chapter does not merely describe private ethics; it describes a civilization governed by the formula Knowledge = Experiences × Sensitivity — a complete language and ideology of feeling. Merkel and Reem, Marx and Lenin, and Silicon Valley identify the weakness of that civilization: feelings collide across groups with no arbitrating standard, humanist sects fight over which humans count, and radical Islam and revived theism have nothing to say about genetics and AI, leaving the next religion to emerge from laboratories — with direct political economy consequences for factory, welfare, and market life.

8 The Time Bomb in the Laboratory

Summary: This chapter turns the life sciences against liberalism's two factual claims: free will and the indivisible self. Harari contrasts Locke, Rousseau, and Thomas Jefferson with genes, hormones, neurons, deterministic chains, random atomic decay, and a uranium-linked robot, then argues that humans act according to desires they do not choose. Brain scanners predicting switch presses, Professor Sanjiv Talwar's robo-rats at the State University of New York, electrodes that make rats turn left or climb ladders, US military brain-chip experiments for PTSD, Hadassah Hospital's depression implants, transcranial direct current stimulators at an Ohio air force base, Sally Adee's sniper simulator, and the attention helmet show desires becoming manipulable. The chapter then divides the self through 37 trillion cells, split-brain epilepsy surgery, Roger Wolcott Sperry, Michael Gazzaniga, Scrabble tiles spelling "automobile race," patient WJ, the chicken claw and snow shovel, Daniel Kahneman's 14 degrees Celsius cold-water test, Donald Redelmeier's 154 colonoscopy patients, Rabin Medical Center in Tel Aviv, 2,428 Swedish birth memories, Jamestown, Borges's "A Problem," Don Quixote, Trento and Trieste, the Isonzo battles, Caporetto, Mussolini, and the Scottish Parliament building. Source anchors: uranium robot; Talwar robo-rats; Sally Adee; Sperry split-brain; Kahneman cold water; Isonzo battles; Scottish Parliament.

Analysis: The uranium robot and Talwar's robo-rats attack the idea that a desire is free simply because the organism feels it from the inside — determinism and randomness divide the whole cake, leaving no crumb for freedom, which is why brain scanners can predict a switch-press before the subject knows they intend it. Sally Adee and the attention helmet show why that attack matters politically: once inner voices can be muted or amplified, customers may buy the will they later report as authentic. Sperry's split-brain patients, Kahneman's cold-water experiment, the Isonzo battles and "our boys didn't die in vain," and the Scottish Parliament's tenfold budget overrun extend artificial intelligence beyond machines, because external systems can model, steer, and outperform the narrating self's claims about choice. And because Harari here proves the self itself is "an imaginary story, just like nations, gods and money," the chapter opens the vault's identity-making bridge to The autobiography of Malcolm X, where a self is visibly written, converted, and rewritten across a life.

9 The Great Decoupling

Summary: Harari identifies three practical threats to liberalism: mass humans may lose economic and military usefulness, systems may value humans collectively but not individually, and upgraded elites may separate biologically from everyone else. He begins with the French Revolution's levée en masse of 23 August 1793, the Declaration of the Rights of Man, Charles W. Eliot, Woodrow Wilson and women's suffrage, then argues that drones, cyber-worms, autonomous weapons, nuclear plants, chemical installations, and fully autonomous air combat weaken the old link between citizenship and military value. In the economy, intelligence separates from consciousness through self-driving cars in Seoul, Google's August 2015 accident, algorithmic traders reacting to the 23 April 2013 Associated Press Twitter hoax, the Flash Crash of 6 May 2010, legal search systems, fMRI lie detection, Mindojo, Watson's 2011 Jeopardy! victory, lung-cancer diagnosis, San Francisco's robotic pharmacy, Mattersight, David Cope's EMI and Annie, Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne's 47 percent job-risk estimate, Nick Bostrom's pi-maximizing superintelligence, artificial pancreas trials at Yale, Google and Novartis contact lenses, Pixie Scientific smart diapers, Microsoft Band, Quantified Self, Shlomi Shavan's "Arik," Bedpost, Angelina Jolie's BRCA1 mutation and 87 percent risk, Facebook's 86,220-person Likes study, Waze, Cortana, Kindle telemetry, North Korean hackers, $3,000 genetic testing, sixty-two billionaires, and Japan, South Korea, India, Brazil, and Nigeria. Source anchors: levee en masse; AP Twitter hoax; Watson Jeopardy; EMI and Annie; Frey Osborne; BRCA1 87 percent; Facebook Likes; Waze; genetic testing cost.

Analysis: The levée en masse of 1793, Charles W. Eliot, and Woodrow Wilson explain why liberal rights once served armies and factories: mass states needed motivated soldiers and workers, so every pair of hands had value. The AP Twitter hoax (a 150-point, $136 billion Dow fall in sixty seconds), the Flash Crash, Watson's Jeopardy! win, the San Francisco robotic pharmacist that filled 2 million prescriptions without an error, EMI and Annie, and Frey and Osborne's 47 percent estimate show that the same material support can vanish when algorithms trade, diagnose, compose, teach, drive, and fight better than unenhanced people — the mass-uselessness scenario that The Sovereign Individual predicts from megapolitics and that The End of Power frames as power diffusing away from institutions. Angelina Jolie's 87 percent BRCA1 figure, Facebook's 86,220-volunteer Likes study (ten Likes beat work colleagues, three hundred beat spouses), Waze, Kindle telemetry, and the $3,000 genetic test make information and coordination politically destabilizing: if external systems know bodies and preferences better than self-report, and only the rich can buy the best upgrades, equal human authority loses its material base — a double shift of technological change and political economy in which Japan's falling birth rate and Brazil's first-class-versus-third-class-train model are the two poles.

10 The Ocean of Consciousness

Summary: Harari distinguishes techno-humanism from Dataism and treats the former as an attempt to create Homo deus while preserving important humanist commitments. Techno-humanism looks back to the Cognitive Revolution, Sapiens DNA, the intersubjective realm, gods, corporations, cities, empires, writing, money, the moon landing, evolutionary humanism, Hitler's selective breeding, genetic engineering, nanotechnology, and brain-computer interfaces, but Harari warns that we lack a map of the mind we hope to upgrade. He compares visible light between 400 and 700 nanometres with infrared, radio waves, ultraviolet, X-rays, and gamma rays, then names sub-normative minds, WEIRD psychology, Joseph Henrich, Steven Heine, Ara Norenzayan, Harvard psychology students, Kalahari hunter-gatherers, shamans, monks, ascetics, Neanderthals, bats, echolocation, Thomas Nagel, Caravaggio, whales, limbic systems, Bach, Mozart, Voyager, Chuck Berry, Columbus, Magellan, Martin Seligman, Ed Diener, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, archaic smell, mushrooms, supermarkets, smartphones, lucid dreams, the US army attention helmet, Ritalin, Prozac, Cipralex, a Mormon clinic fantasy, Joseph Smith, George Clooney, Romeo, Juliet, Montagues, and Capulets. Source anchors: techno-humanism; WEIRD psychology; Thomas Nagel bat; whale songs; positive psychology; attention helmet; Cipralex; Mormon passion operation.

Analysis: Techno-humanism keeps Harari inside the humanist inheritance because it still wants upgraded humans rather than a non-human successor — a second Cognitive Revolution achieved by rewiring the genome and brain. Henrich, Heine, and Norenzayan's WEIRD-psychology study, Thomas Nagel's bat, whale song, and the sub-normative-versus-normative map of the mind expose the ignorance behind that ambition: scientists have studied a narrow sliver of human consciousness and know even less about animal or extinct mental states, and the systematic export of psychiatric categories that shape experience is exactly what Crazy Like Us documents. The attention helmet, Ritalin, Prozac, Cipralex, the $100,000 Mormon passion operation, Romeo and Juliet make speculative governance unavoidable, because technologies that tune attention, guilt, depression, sexual desire, or romantic attachment require someone to decide which desire should be amplified — an authority problem made harder by the WEIRD limits of culture, identity, and legitimacy, since we lack even the map of the mind we are proposing to engineer.

11 The Data Religion

Summary: The final chapter presents Dataism as a religion born from Darwin's organisms-as-algorithms and Alan Turing's electronic algorithms. Harari says Dataism interprets the economy, politics, science, and art as data-processing systems, comparing free-market capitalism with distributed processing and Soviet communism with central processing through Friedrich Hayek, bread prices, the stock exchange, New York Times headlines, Moscow ministries, Odessa kolkhozes, Red October bakery, Trofim Lysenko, Siberian wheat, Gorbachev's aide in London, Thatcherism, and bread queues. Politics receives the same treatment through democracies, dictatorships, the Roman Empire, elections, parties, parliaments, the Internet, cyber 9/11, NSA surveillance, Russia, Iran, North Korea, Kim Jong-un, Ali Khamenei, Putin, Barack Obama, Auschwitz, Hiroshima, the Great Leap Forward, billionaires, and market forces. The chapter then compresses history into processors, variety, connections, and free movement; moves through the Cognitive Revolution, agriculture, writing, money, empires, universal religions, and scientific globalization; and gives Dataism commandments through the Internet-of-All-Things, Ray Kurzweil's The Singularity is Near, Aaron Swartz, JSTOR, smart car-pools, 1 billion private cars, Wikipedia, Japanese tourists, Facebook Likes, No More Woof, Beethoven's Fifth, Chuck Berry, DNA sequencing, wearables, Google, Facebook, machine learning, mammoths, Chinese river dolphins, and the closing questions about organisms, intelligence, consciousness, and self-knowing algorithms. Source anchors: Darwin Turing; Hayek prices; Lysenko wheat; London bread queue; Internet-of-All-Things; Aaron Swartz; smart car-pool; No More Woof; closing questions.

Analysis: Darwin's organisms-as-algorithms and Turing's electronic algorithms are the hinge that lets Dataism collapse biology and computer science into one account of data flows, with exactly the same mathematical laws applying to carbon and silicon. Hayek's dispersed prices, Lysenko's Siberian wheat, the Red October bakery's 3.5 million loaves, and Gorbachev's aide astonished that "nobody is in charge of supplying bread to London" explain why Harari treats capitalism, communism, democracy, and dictatorship as competing processing systems rather than only ideologies: the relevant test is how fast, freely, and accurately information moves, which relocates political economy inside artificial intelligence. Internet-of-All-Things, Aaron Swartz, the smart car-pool (50 million communal cars replacing 1 billion private ones), No More Woof, and the closing questions about organisms, intelligence and consciousness make platform governance devotional: Dataism promises health, mobility, openness, and self-knowledge while judging Homo sapiens by the same functional yardstick humans used on mammoths, wolves, chickens, and Chinese river dolphins — the species-level future that Seveneves dramatizes through survival rather than relinquishment.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • "Sugar is now more dangerous than gunpowder": in 2012 about 56 million people died worldwide; 620,000 died from human violence (120,000 war, 500,000 crime), while 800,000 committed suicide and 1.5 million died of diabetes.
  • Obesity ledger: in 2010 famine and malnutrition killed about 1 million, obesity about 3 million; in 2010 terrorists killed 7,697 people worldwide. "For the average American, Coca-Cola poses a far deadlier threat than al-Qaeda."
  • Smallpox: WHO declared eradication in 1979; in 1967 it still infected 15 million and killed 2 million a year; by 2014 nobody was infected.
  • Beauvais famine: 2.8 million French (~15 per cent) starved 1692–94 while Louis XIV dined at Versailles; famine then took Estonia (1695), Finland (1696), and Scotland (1695–98).
  • The new human agenda: immortality, bliss, divinity — the third project (divinity) subsumes the other two; three paths: biological engineering, cyborg engineering, and non-organic beings.
  • Google Calico: launched 2013 with a stated mission "to solve death"; Maris on Google Ventures investing 36 per cent of its \(2 billion portfolio in life sciences; Thiel's "\)2.2 billion" private fortune and his "fight it" posture toward death.
  • The three-parent baby: Alana Saarinen, born 2000 in Michigan with nuclear DNA from her parents and mitochondrial DNA from a third woman; Britain legalized the treatment on 3 February 2015.
  • Organisms are algorithms: "99 per cent of our decisions — including the most important life choices concerning spouses, careers and habitats — are made by the highly refined algorithms we call sensations, emotions and desires."
  • The suffering test: "How do you know if an entity is real? Very simple — just ask yourself, 'Can it suffer?'"; corpses of the argument: Zeus, the euro, and a bankrupt bank cannot; a wounded soldier, a famished peasant, and a cow separated from her calf can.
  • Lake Fayum: 50 billion cubic metres of water held by an artificial lake built by Senusret III and Amenemhat III (ruled 1878–1814 BC) without wheels, iron, or dynamite; by comparison Lake Mead holds at most 35 billion.
  • Mao's grain reports: fabricated reports of agricultural surges led Beijing to export the rice China needed; outcome described as "the worst famine in history."
  • Berlin Conference: Europe divided Africa "as if it were a pie" in 1884 on an empty map; "when the written fantasies of European bureaucracies encountered the African reality, reality was forced to surrender."
  • Sousa Mendes: the "angel with the rubber stamp," issued thousands of visas in Bordeaux 1940 against orders; up to 30,000 saved.
  • The 2012 Gallup numbers on Darwin: 46 per cent of Americans in 2012 believed God created humans in their current form within the last 10,000 years; 15 per cent accepted natural selection alone.
  • Belavezha Accords: 8 December 1991, 2 p.m., a dacha near Viskuli — "pen touches paper — and abracadabra! The Soviet Union disappears."
  • Ceauşescu's last speech: 21 December 1989, Bucharest; three conditions held communist Romania together and all three failed — a YouTube-visible collapse of a 20-million-person state.
  • Kahneman cold water: 80 per cent of volunteers chose to repeat the longer, worse trial; the peak-end rule makes the narrating self duration-blind.
  • The peak-end rule: 154 colonoscopy patients in Toronto; 2,428 Swedish birth memories (90 per cent positive, 28.5 per cent describing the worst pain imaginable).
  • Isonzo / Our Boys Didn't Die in Vain: eleven Isonzo battles for "Trento and Trieste"; almost 700,000 Italian dead; the sacrifice principle that fueled the Scottish Parliament cost overrun (£40 million estimate → £400 million actual).
  • Frey and Osborne: 47 per cent of US jobs at high risk by 2033; archaeologists safe at 0.7 per cent because they combine hard pattern recognition with low profits.
  • Facebook Likes study: 86,220 volunteers; 10 Likes outperform work colleagues, 70 outperform friends, 150 outperform family, 300 outperform spouses; "our personal data is the most valuable resource most humans still have to offer, and we are giving it away for email services and funny cat videos."
  • Waze as oracle → agent → sovereign: "When everybody uses the same oracle, and everybody believes the oracle, the oracle turns into a sovereign."
  • $3,000 genetic test: the cost of the Jolie test; 1 billion people live on under $1 a day and another 1.5 billion on $1–2; sixty-two billionaires own as much as the poorest 3.6 billion.
  • Dataism's tally of history: 70,000 years of Sapiens processing; four stages (Cognitive Revolution, agriculture, writing and money ~5,000 years ago, globalization from 1492); the union of all the world's groceries — Middle Eastern wheat, Andean potatoes, New Guinean sugar, Ethiopian coffee.
  • Aaron Swartz: RSS at fourteen, "Guerilla Open Access Manifesto" (2008), JSTOR download via MIT, suicide 11 January 2013; Dataism's first martyr and its new value, "freedom of information."
  • The book's three closing questions: Are organisms really just algorithms, and is life just data processing? What's more valuable — intelligence or consciousness? What happens when non-conscious algorithms know us better than we know ourselves?
  • The Ark Syndrome: "People who believe in the hi-tech Ark should not be put in charge of the global ecology, for the same reason that people who believe in a heavenly afterlife should not be given nuclear weapons."
