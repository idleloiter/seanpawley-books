Knight's Gambit (Vintage)

Buy on Amazon — Knight's Gambit (Vintage)

Position in the vault

This note belongs with Faulkner's Yoknapatawpha material on Gavin Stevens, Chick Mallison, rural law, inheritance, kinship injury, and the gap between formal proof and local knowledge. Read it beside Intruder in the Dust because Stevens' legal intelligence is again tested by evidence that is social, bodily, remembered, and morally compromised rather than neatly procedural.

Detailed overview

Knight's Gambit gathers six Gavin Stevens stories set in and around Jefferson, Yoknapatawpha County, and nearby rural settlements such as Frenchman's Bend, Mottstown, Okatoba County, and the river bottoms. The collection is built from murder cases, inheritance disputes, insurance plots, jury resistance, and domestic coercion, but Faulkner rarely lets the puzzle remain merely mechanical. In each story, Stevens has to read what county institutions cannot quite register: a horse's habits, smoke in a sealed box, a trotline paddle, a man's refusal to acquit, the wrong way to mix a cold toddy, or a young aristocrat's scheme involving a vicious stallion.

The early stories make Stevens' brilliance depend on knowing local custom deeply enough to notice deviation. In Smoke, Judge Dukinfield's sealed office, Uncle Job's chair, Doctor West's cigarettes, Granby Dodge's will, and Anselm Holland's abnormal horse converge because Stevens understands courthouse habits and family property hatred. In Hand Upon the Waters, the fact that Lonnie Grinnup would pull a skiff along a trotline rather than use a paddle is enough to crack open Tyler and Boyd Ballenbaugh's insurance murder. These plots are detective stories, but their evidence comes from everyday practice.

Several stories turn on people whom courts and towns misread because they are poor, mentally disabled, socially marginal, or nearly voiceless. Monk Odlethrop is railroaded into prison for a killing he did not understand and later manipulated into killing the warden by Terrel's jailhouse rhetoric about farming in the free world. Lonnie Grinnup, the last degraded remnant of the Grenier name, lives in a tin-and-canvas hut with the deaf-and-dumb Joe, and only Joe's silent vengeance finally answers Boyd Ballenbaugh. Ellie Pritchel, the dim-witted wife in An Error in Chemistry, only gains legal weight after death, when her killing exposes Joel Flint's attempt to disappear as Signor Canova.

Faulkner also uses the collection to test Stevens' own relation to justice. He is a county attorney, Harvard man, chess player, translator of scripture into Greek, and local insider who knows both the courthouse square and the back roads. Yet he does not always convict, punish, or even disclose what he knows. In Hand Upon the Waters, he refuses to tell the sheriff exactly how Boyd ended up on the trotline; in Monk, he knows Terrel used Monk but cannot prevent the governor's pardon machine; in Tomorrow, his first case fails because Stonewall Jackson Fentry's private grief will not bend to the law's public categories.

The title novella, Knight's Gambit, shifts from rural crime to a domestic chess problem involving Mrs. Harriss, Max Harriss, her daughter, Captain Sebastian Gualdres, the Cayley girl, Rafe McCallum's dangerous stallion, and Gavin's own buried courtship from 1919. It is the collection's longest and most self-revealing piece because Chick Mallison watches Gavin solve a present danger while discovering that his uncle once wrote two letters in the wrong envelopes, lost the woman who became Mrs. Harriss, and is now old enough to act differently. The case becomes less about prosecuting a crime than preventing one without humiliating the living more than necessary.

The six pieces therefore make Stevens a reader of residue: smoke, mud, hoof knowledge, signatures, gossip, insurance forms, old names, and failures of speech. Faulkner's narrative logic is not that law defeats violence cleanly. It is that violence leaves behind habits and materials, and Stevens' best work comes when he can identify which small local fact does not belong.

Section-by-section notes

Smoke

Summary: Smoke begins with Anselm Holland, Old Anse, an outsider who marries into two thousand acres near Jefferson and leaves twin sons, Young Anse and Virginius, locked in a long quarrel over their mother's land. Old Anse dies after violating the family cemetery, his will gives the property to Virginius if Judge Dukinfield is satisfied that Virginius paid the taxes, and Judge Dukinfield is then shot silently in his courthouse office while Uncle Job sits outside. Gavin Stevens brings the Grand Jury, the Holland twins, Granby Dodge, Uncle Job, Doctor West's unusual cigarettes, a Memphis thug, a pistol with a silencer, and the brass box into one room, then uses staged pipe smoke to force Granby to expose himself as the man behind both murders. Source anchors: Anselm Holland, Young Anse, Virginius, Judge Dukinfield, Uncle Job, Doctor West, brass box, Granby Dodge.

Analysis: Anselm Holland, Young Anse, and Virginius give the story its property wound: the land is the object through which father, sons, and cousin continue or repair family violence. Judge Dukinfield and Uncle Job make the courthouse office look sealed, so Doctor West's cigarette clue and the brass box turn Legibility into a contest over routine: Stevens reads air, smell, and office habit where ordinary eyewitness proof fails. Granby Dodge's self-effacement hides calculation until the staged smoke makes his fear visible as confession.

Monk

Summary: Monk follows Monk Odlethrop, also called Stonewall Jackson Odlethrop, from the pine hills, Mrs. Odlethrop's shotgun house, old Fraser's whiskey making, and a Jefferson filling station into a murder conviction that he barely understands. A bad trial sends him to the penitentiary, and five years later one of the actual killer's companions confesses, allowing Uncle Gavin to secure a pardon that Monk refuses because he has attached himself to the warden and is knitting him a sweater. Monk then kills Warden Gambrell with the warden's own pistol and dies on the scaffold saying he is going into the free world to farm, a phrase Stevens later traces to Terrel, a violent convict who manipulated Monk with talk of God, land, trains, poor country people, and freedom. Source anchors: Monk Odlethrop, Stonewall Jackson, Mrs. Odlethrop, Fraser, filling station, Warden Gambrell, Terrel, free world.

Analysis: Monk Odlethrop is treated by the court as a responsible murderer before anyone asks whether he can understand responsibility, exposing a failure of Legibility in legal categories that record the act but cannot read the actor. Mrs. Odlethrop, Fraser, and the filling station teach him only loyalty to whoever feeds him. Warden Gambrell and Terrel then weaponize Language and Ideology by turning "free world" and farming into a lethal instruction Monk can obey but not comprehend.

Hand Upon the Waters

Summary: Hand Upon the Waters opens at Lonnie Grinnup's river hut, where a man with a gunny sack and a youth find Lonnie's trotline sagging under a body while Joe, the deaf-and-dumb man Lonnie raised, can only make urgent sounds. Gavin Stevens attends the inquest at the grist mill, notices that the youth says he rowed the skiff back with a paddle, follows Tyler Ballenbaugh's intervention at the burial road, and discovers in Mottstown that Tyler holds a five-thousand-dollar life insurance policy on Lonnie with double indemnity. Stevens then goes alone to the hut, finds Tyler and Boyd Ballenbaugh searching the dead man's place, draws Boyd's admission that he killed Lonnie for money, watches Boyd shoot Tyler, and is saved when Joe drops from a tree onto Boyd before the killer later turns up dead on the trotline. Source anchors: Lonnie Grinnup, Joe, trotline, grist mill, Tyler Ballenbaugh, Boyd Ballenbaugh, Mottstown, paddle.

Analysis: Lonnie Grinnup and Joe matter because the county's legal apparatus almost misses a murder committed against people with little standing and almost no speech. The trotline and paddle make Informal Institutions evidentiary: Stevens knows the local fishing practice well enough to see that the paddle stages an outsider's version of the death. Tyler and Boyd Ballenbaugh turn the case into insurance fraud, while Joe's final act supplies a rough communal answer that Stevens refuses to translate fully for the sheriff.

Tomorrow

Summary: Tomorrow returns to Gavin Stevens' first case, when he defends Bookwright for killing Buck Thorpe after Thorpe tries to elope with Bookwright's seventeen-year-old daughter from Frenchman's Bend. The case appears easy because Thorpe is a brawler, gambler, whiskey man, cattle thief, already married, and found with a half-drawn pistol, but one juror, Stonewall Jackson Fentry, refuses to acquit. Gavin and Chick investigate Fentry's hills home, hear from Rufus Pruitt and Mrs. Pruitt about Fentry's poverty, sawmill work at Ben Quick's, the dying woman he married, the baby he raised with goat's milk, and the Thorpe brothers who came with legal papers to take the child. Isham Quick then identifies Buck Thorpe as that stolen child grown into a bad man, explaining why Fentry cannot vote Bookwright free. Source anchors: Bookwright, Buck Thorpe, Frenchman's Bend, Stonewall Jackson Fentry, Rufus Pruitt, goat's milk, Isham Quick, Thorpe brothers.

Analysis: Bookwright and Buck Thorpe look at first like a familiar Southern honor killing, but Stonewall Jackson Fentry's vote breaks that easy reading. Rufus Pruitt, goat's milk, and the Thorpe brothers reveal a Moral Economy the verdict form cannot express: Fentry judges from the care he gave the abandoned child who became Buck, not from sympathy with the adult criminal. Frenchman's Bend and Isham Quick show public guilt colliding with a private claim that Fentry refuses to let death erase.

An Error in Chemistry

Summary: An Error in Chemistry begins when Joel Flint telephones the sheriff to say he has killed his wife, Old Man Wesley Pritchel's dim-witted daughter Ellie, at Pritchel's remote farm. Flint is a Yankee carnival pitchman who drinks corn whiskey straight, while Pritchel is a violent recluse guarding a clay pit that three northern buyers want for road material; the sheriff finds Ellie's body, a shotgun, three dead squirrels, blood near the truck, and Pritchel locked inside a room. Flint escapes jail, a Billboard scrap is found at his house, an insurance adjustor named Workman reports a five-hundred-dollar policy, and Gavin catches the disguised Flint posing as Pritchel when he tries to stir sugar into raw whiskey instead of making a proper cold toddy. The real Pritchel is found under the stable feed room, shaved and dressed as Flint, with a ledger of Signor Canova clippings and posters recording Flint's old career as a master of illusion. Source anchors: Joel Flint, Wesley Pritchel, Ellie, clay pit, three dead squirrels, Billboard, Workman, Signor Canova.

Analysis: Joel Flint and Signor Canova make the murder a performance about disappearance, not simply an insurance killing. Wesley Pritchel, Ellie, the clay pit, and three dead squirrels supply the local setting that Flint tries to manipulate by posing as the bereaved father. The Billboard scrap, Workman, and the cold toddy mistake matter because Stevens solves the case through cultural and bodily knowledge: a lifelong local drinker would not put sugar straight into whiskey, so the false Pritchel reveals the failed magician beneath the makeup.

Knight's Gambit

Summary: The title novella starts at Gavin Stevens' chessboard when Max Harriss drags his sister into the room demanding action against Captain Sebastian Gualdres, the Argentine guest who may marry Mrs. Harriss after first attracting the daughter. The crisis widens through the Harriss plantation, the late millionaire Harriss, the daughter's jealousy, the Cayley girl and her returned ring, Captain Gualdres' riding, Max's humiliation with horses and fencing, Memphis surveillance through Robert Markey, and Rafe McCallum's sale of a lethal stallion. Stevens realizes Max has planted the stallion where Gualdres expects his blind night mare, prevents the trap, then later steers Max toward enlistment and sees Gualdres marry the Harriss daughter and leave for Argentina before Pearl Harbor and 1942 wartime movement carry Chick toward military training. The ending discloses Gavin's own 1919 betrothal to the woman who became Mrs. Harriss, the two wrong envelopes, Heidelberg, Paris, a Russian woman, and Gavin's belated claim that age has improved him. Source anchors: Max Harriss, Captain Gualdres, Mrs. Harriss, Cayley girl, Rafe McCallum, lethal stallion, Robert Markey, Pearl Harbor.

Analysis: Max Harriss, Captain Gualdres, and the Cayley girl give Stevens a live problem of youth, pride, sex, and class before it becomes a corpse. Rafe McCallum's lethal stallion turns the chess title literal: Max moves another creature as a weapon, and Stevens reads horse, stable, blind mare, and rider before the trap closes. Mrs. Harriss, Robert Markey, Pearl Harbor, and Gavin's 1919 letters expose Elite Formation as moral training gone wrong, because inherited status equips Max to stage violence while Gavin's older authority lets him prevent it without public ruin.
