Detailed overview

Jacob McDonough treats Berkshire Hathaway less as a legend about Warren Buffett than as a sequence of capital accounts that changed hands, line items, owners, and uses from 1955 through 1985. The book starts with Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates and Hathaway Manufacturing Company merging into a New England textile manufacturer whose 1955 balance sheet was heavy with inventories, receivables, and property, plant, and equipment, but whose profits barely registered. Its distinctive move is to keep asking what an investor could have known from annual reports, 10-K filings, market values, tax loss carryforwards, replacement capital needs, and segment returns at the moment each decision was made.

The early Berkshire material reads like a balance-sheet autopsy of a statistically cheap but operationally poor business. Berkshire sold near net current asset value and at steep discounts to book value, yet the textile mills were fighting Japanese cotton-fabric imports, obsolete machinery, low margins, and capital expenditures that exceeded depreciation. McDonough explains why Warren Buffett's first Berkshire purchases through Buffett Partnership, Limited could be rational as a cigar-butt investment at $7.51 per share in 1962, while still becoming a costly control decision after Seabury Stanton's reduced tender offer led Buffett to buy enough stock to take over.

Once Buffett controls Berkshire, the book follows cash leaving textiles and entering businesses with better funding structures. National Indemnity gives Berkshire insurance float, not bank debt, and Illinois National Bank gives it deposit funding, high liquidity, and a one-office Rockford franchise built by Eugene Abegg. The book keeps distinguishing accounting appearance from economic usefulness: National Indemnity's tangible net worth could be rearranged into securities Berkshire would have owned anyway, while the premium over book bought underwriting profits and float; Illinois National's dividends could move up to Berkshire without an added tax because Berkshire owned more than 80% of the bank.

The middle sections widen the circle to Home and Auto, Home State operations, GEICO, Cypress, Waumbec Mills, K&W Products, Diversified Retailing, Blue Chip Stamps, See's Candy, the Buffalo Evening News, Wesco Financial, General Foods, Detroit International Bridge, and Precision Steel. These are not presented as trophies. Several purchases are shown as mistakes or near-mistakes: Hochschild Kohn is sold after Buffett, Charlie Munger, and Sandy Gottesman conclude it is not worth owning; Home and Auto's Miami expansion produces a large underwriting loss; the Buffalo Evening News suffers before its Sunday edition can displace the Courier-Express; Waumbec extends a textile business that still lacks good economics.

The most important contrast is between cheap assets and businesses that can send cash elsewhere without needing much more capital. See's Candy raises per-pound realization from $1.85 in 1972 to $5.49 in 1984 while pounds sold and store count rise far more slowly, giving Blue Chip and then Berkshire cash that can be redeployed. GEICO nearly fails after a 1975 underwriting loss of $190.9 million, but its direct-marketing expense ratio and preferred-risk franchise remain intact, letting Berkshire buy common and preferred shares when the market price reflects bankruptcy and dilution risk. The Washington Post, Affiliated Publications, and later Nebraska Furniture Mart show the same search for low-cost positions, protected local franchises, and cash generation that can survive ugly reported years.

By the conclusion, McDonough has turned Berkshire's rise into a study of opportunity cost. Berkshire's stock price from Buffett's first 1962 purchase to 1985 is spectacular, but the original textile purchase is still judged as a mistake because National Indemnity, Illinois National, Associated Retail, Disney, and American Express were available without dragging a poor textile mill along. The book's final lesson is not that book-value bargains are useless; it is that a bargain with high receivables, inventories, replacement assets, and weak margins can consume years of scarce capital, while a business with float, pricing power, disciplined underwriting, or unusually low operating costs can compound retained earnings for decades.

Major people, societies, and motivations

  • Warren Buffett: general partner of Buffett Partnership, Limited and, from 1965, Chairman of Berkshire Hathaway. The book's singular allocator, he moves capital out of textiles into marketable securities, then into insurance and banking, and finally into whole businesses — while taking only labor-market pay (a salary around $50,000 plus about $18,000 in retirement benefits, rising to $100,000 by 1982) instead of stock options. His controlling stake is the measure of ownership compounding: Buffett and his family owned 40.3% of Berkshire in 1971, 32.3% in 2006 when he announced charitable giving, and 30.7% at the end of 2019 (worth roughly $169.2 billion).
  • Charlie Munger: Buffett's partner beginning with Diversified Retailing in 1966; chairman of Blue Chip Stamps by 1976 and later Vice President of Berkshire. He is the executor of the sibling vehicles — Blue Chip and Wesco Financial — that bought See's Candy, the Buffalo Evening News, and Precision Steel, and he supplies the book's recurring aphorisms about avoiding commitment bias and staying flexible.
  • Sandy (Salomon) Gottesman: joined Buffett and Munger in forming Diversified Retailing in January 1966 to buy the Baltimore department store Hochschild Kohn; later served on Berkshire's board.
  • Seabury Stanton: Berkshire's President and Chairman, whose handling of the 1964 repurchase is the hinge of the whole story. Having orally agreed to buy BPL's block at $11.50, he mailed an offer at $11.375; Buffett refused and instead bought enough stock to seize control — turning a statistical bargain into a control investment averaging $14.86 per share by early 1966.
  • Jack Ringwalt: founded National Indemnity in 1940 and sold it (with affiliate National Fire & Marine) to Berkshire in March 1967 for $8.577 million. His disciplined underwriting operation gave Berkshire not just an insurer but roughly $19.4 million of low-cost float.
  • Eugene Abegg: the banker who built the single-office Illinois National Bank & Trust of Rockford from $250,000 of net worth and $400,000 of deposits in 1931 to $17 million of net worth and $100 million of deposits in 1969, and stayed on as Chairman. He personifies conservative, branchless banking whose high dividend payout to the parent needs little tax leakage.
  • Victor Raab: founded the Home and Automobile Insurance Company in Chicago in 1959 and sold it to Berkshire (about $2 million, September 1971). His focused one-market underwriting model stumbled once Berkshire expanded it to Miami.
  • Leo Goodwin Sr.: founded GEICO in 1936 and created its direct-to-consumer, no-agents distribution — the cost advantage (expense ratio not above 16.5% for 30 years to 1975) that survived the company's near-fatal 1975 underwriting crisis.
  • Rose Blumkin: founder of the Nebraska Furniture Mart, whose family retained 10% of the business (with an option, later exercised, to buy back another 10%) when Berkshire bought 80% for $55 million in September 1983; her ultra-low margin, high-turnover model (gross margin "not much more than" 22.2%, operating expenses 16.5% of sales) is the book's benchmark low-cost operator.
  • Eugene Meyer and Katharine Graham: the family that controlled the Washington Post Company from Eugene Meyer's 1933 purchase until 2013; Katharine Graham, in control from 1963, steered the paper through the Pentagon Papers, Watergate, and a television-license fight that depressed the stock Berkshire bought in 1973.
  • Edward Butler Sr.: started the Buffalo Sunday News in December 1873 and the daily Evening News in 1880; the Buffalo lineage that entered the Sunday circulation war Blue Chip joined by buying the Evening News in 1977.
  • Manuel Moroun / Central Cartage: outbid Wesco for control of the Detroit International Bridge Company at $24 per share in 1977 (20% above Wesco's average cost), ending the attempt to take the Ambassador Bridge private and handing Wesco a $1 million realized profit.
  • Corporate vehicles as institutional actors: Berkshire Hathaway itself (the 1955 merger of Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates and Hathaway Manufacturing), Buffett Partnership Limited, Diversified Retailing, Blue Chip Stamps, and Wesco Financial form an interlocking set of legal "societies" whose ownership percentages, consolidation status, accounting methods, and regulatory positions determine how cash can move — tax-free inter-corporate dividends above 80% ownership, equity versus cost method accounting, and state insurance regulation that stripped voting rights from Berkshire's GEICO stock.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Bidirectional opportunity cost: the book repeatedly prices what capital was not invested in (Disney, American Express, Illinois National bought directly) against what Berkshire actually did with the textile mill as its platform.
  • Statistical cheapness versus economic quality: selling at 37.2% of book value or 73.3% of net current assets means nothing if receivables, inventories, and replacement capital eat every freed dollar.
  • Float as pseudo-equity: insurance reserves, trading-stamp liabilities, and Travelers Cheques let a firm "borrow" money at zero or negative cost and invest it — the core of Financial Infrastructure in this book.
  • Accounting presentation versus economic substance: cost method, equity method, unconsolidated subsidiaries, realized versus unrealized gains, and non-taxed tax provisions all reshape reported profits without changing intrinsic value.
  • Winner-take-all local franchises: dominant Sunday newspapers, a preferred-risk cost advantage, a border toll gateway, and a $300-mile trade-area furniture store compound precisely because competition cannot be rebuilt cheaply.
  • Regulatory and political ecosystems as constraint and moat: Illinois unit-banking law, FDIC guarantees, insurance regulation, broadcast-license renewals, and minimum-wage differentials decide which businesses keep their capital and pricing power.
  • Path dependence and institutional drift: Berkshire begins as a die‑hard New England textile mill, drifts into securities and insurance, and only becomes a conglomerate because its float, plural subsidiaries, and retained earnings re-make the platform the mill left behind.
  • What could have been known: every decision is reconstructed from the annual reports, 10-Ks, market prices, and segment data an investor actually had at that date.

Core concepts

  • Capital Allocation: the book reads Berkshire's history as a sequence of dated choices about where scarce cash, float, retained earnings, and attention should go — the note is the vault's defining case that a business is its allocation record, not its manufacturing label.
  • Financial Infrastructure: insurance float, bank deposits, tax loss carryforwards, marketable securities, and parent-subsidiary dividends become Berkshire's compounding machinery, and the mechanisms (cost of float, dividends-free-above-80%-ownership, cost-method accounting) are shown line by line.
  • Commercial Society: See's Candy, Nebraska Furniture Mart, Illinois National, GEICO, and the Buffalo Evening News show how local franchises and customer economics shape value; See's rising per-pound realization with flat volume is the demonstration that trust and brand can outrun unit growth.
  • Chokepoints and Gateways: Detroit International Bridge and local newspapers illustrate the attraction of scarce access points with pricing power; a toll bridge's 20%+ returns on assets and the Ambassador Bridge's ~85% share of commercial traffic make the concept concrete.
  • Technological Change: Japanese textile competition, obsolete machinery, and changing distribution economics explain why book value can hide economic decline and why replacement capital, not depreciation, is the real cost of staying in business.
  • Information and Coordination: annual reports, 10-K filings, segment returns, market prices, and hindsight bias frame what investors could know at each decision point; the book is explicitly built to suppress victory-lap hindsight.
  • Path Dependence and Institutional Drift: the original 1955 mill, its tax shields, its cost basis, and its platform keep constraining outcomes for two decades; the conclusion's opportunity-cost argument rests on the fact that Berkshire's path foreclosed direct purchases of National Indemnity, Illinois National, Disney, and American Express.
  • Institutions: Illinois' single-office banking law, FDIC deposit insurance, insurance regulation (which directed Berkshire to give up GEICO voting rights), GAAP rules, and the post-1980 regulatory stance all determine which financial forms survive and which capital can move.
  • Industrial Policy: the 1955 report's contrast between Japanese wages below $0.15/hour and a $1.00 U.S. minimum wage, unit-banking restrictions, and broadcast-license renewal politics show public policy deciding which industries retain capital and pricing power.
  • Economy, Firms, and Infrastructure: the book repeatedly treats Berkshire's balance sheet as a node in the wider economy — float, deposits, and payments infrastructure convert household savings into investable capital, and Berkshire's 1980s insurance retrenchment only works because diversified earning power absorbs the industry-wide downturn.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

I. The Preface

Summary: McDonough opens by placing Berkshire Hathaway beside Warren Buffett's shareholder letters, biographies of Buffett, and the annual reports of Berkshire, Blue Chip Stamps, Wesco Financial, and GEICO, then narrows his own task to financial-statement reading. He uses John Maynard Keynes's warning about inductive arguments, the football-film comparison with SportsCenter highlights, and the practitioner who studies a lineman's block or a quarterback's coverage read to explain why the book will focus on filings rather than heroic retellings. He admits his admiration for Berkshire and Buffett, warns that hindsight can make probabilities look easier than they were, and flags opportunity cost, including interest rates and investments not made, as a limit on the study.
Analysis: The John Maynard Keynes quotation, SportsCenter highlights comparison, and opportunity cost caveat tell the reader how to use the rest of the book: as a dated capital allocation record rather than a victory lap. McDonough's admission of hindsight bias matters because later sections will judge Berkshire at moments such as 1957, 1962, 1967, 1974, and 1976, when annual reports, market prices, and interest rates gave incomplete evidence, which is precisely the information and coordination problem the book keeps stage-managing. The references to Berkshire Hathaway annual reports and Buffett shareholder letters also explain why the note-by-note reconstruction emphasizes reported assets, marketable securities, float, and taxes instead of personality anecdotes.
Source anchors: John Maynard Keynes; SportsCenter highlights; Berkshire Hathaway annual reports; Buffett shareholder letters; hindsight bias; opportunity cost.

II. The Prologue

Summary: The prologue sets up the puzzle: Buffett built Berkshire from three failed or declining businesses, namely Berkshire Hathaway textiles, Diversified Retailing's Hochschild Kohn department store, and Blue Chip Stamps' trading-stamp operation. Berkshire's textile operations closed in 1985, Hochschild Kohn went out of business in 1984, and Blue Chip's trading-stamp revenue fell 92.6% from $124.2 million in 1969 to $9.2 million in 1982. McDonough then contrasts Berkshire's $21.7 million 1964 market value and $553.5 billion 2019 value with the 1965 Fortune 500 leaders, especially General Electric, whose 1965 revenues, profits, Thomas Edison lineage, and later Jack Welch era made it look untouchable.
Analysis: Hochschild Kohn, Blue Chip Stamps, and Berkshire's textile shutdown define the starting materials as flawed operating businesses rather than obvious engines of wealth — the raw case of path dependence and institutional drift, where a conglomerate's eventual form is inherited from three declining platforms. The comparison with General Electric and Jack Welch gives McDonough a measuring stick: Berkshire did not simply beat a weak peer, it overtook a company with vast 1965 revenue, profit, reputation, and managerial prestige, the same reversal-of-fortune that Good Profit later documents from inside GE's rival Koch Industries. The $124.2 million to $9.2 million fall in Blue Chip's stamp revenue also prepares the reader for the book's recurring distinction between a shrinking legacy business and the cash, float, or securities that can be extracted before the legacy business disappears.
Source anchors: Hochschild Kohn; Blue Chip Stamps; $124.2 million; $9.2 million; General Electric; Jack Welch; the three failed businesses.

III. The Textile Mill 1955-1962

Summary: This section reconstructs Berkshire Hathaway before Buffett as a capital-intensive New England textile mill formed in 1955 by Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates and Hathaway Manufacturing Company. In 1955, inventories were 41.6% of assets, property, plant, and equipment was 30.2%, receivables were 9.7%, and net income was only $300,722 on $55.2 million of assets. From 1955 through 1961 the company cumulatively lost $1.5 million on $441.4 million of sales, while paying $9 million of dividends, repurchasing $7.8 million of shares, and spending $15.1 million on capital expenditures. McDonough adds the 1955 annual report's warning about Japanese workers earning less than $0.15 per hour against a $1.00 U.S. minimum wage, then shows why the 1957 low near $5 per share still looked far below liquidation value.
Analysis: Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates and Hathaway Manufacturing Company matter because their combined assets were tangible but not productive enough to earn adequate returns. The 41.6% inventories figure and $15.1 million of capital expenditures show why reported book value did not equal freely usable cash; shareholders' money was tied up in a financial infrastructure of cloth, machinery, and replacement spending whose plumbing did not return the capital it absorbed. The $9 million dividends, repurchases, and $5 per share low explain the dual nature of the capital allocation problem: the business was bad, but at a severe enough price the balance sheet could still support a profitable statistical investment. The Japanese fabrics warning and the wage differential make the mill's decline a case of technological change and industrial policy operating together — a commodity producer losing to cheaper foreign labor in an environment where domestic wage regulation raises the floor — which is why book value hides economic decline.
Source anchors: Berkshire Fine Spinning Associates; Hathaway Manufacturing Company; 41.6% inventories; $9 million dividends; Japanese fabrics; $5 per share.

IV. The Investment 1962-1965

Summary: McDonough follows Buffett Partnership, Limited as it buys Berkshire at $7.51 per share near the end of 1962, when Berkshire had $16.5 million of net current assets, $32.5 million of book value, a $12.1 million market value, and $4.5 million of unused tax loss carryforwards. The company was still losing money, with a $2.2 million 1962 loss and sales down 23.4% from 1959, but management planned to use excess current assets from plant reductions and inventory cuts to repurchase stock. Seabury Stanton first discussed buying BPL's shares for $11.50, then mailed an offer at $11.375, after which Buffett kept buying until BPL controlled more than half of Berkshire and owned 552,528 shares at an average cost of $14.86 by early 1966.
Analysis: Buffett Partnership Limited and the $7.51 per share entry price show the investment first as a discount-to-assets purchase, not yet as a plan to build a conglomerate. The $16.5 million net current assets and $4.5 million tax loss carryforwards explain why a weak textile company could attract Buffett despite losses, because repurchases below value and possible tax shelter use could raise per-share worth through the firm's existing financial infrastructure. Seabury Stanton's $11.375 offer changes the situation into a control fight, and the later $14.86 average cost marks the point where the cheap-stock thesis becomes ownership of a poor business — the moment the capital allocation decision shifts from buying a bargain to being saddled with one.
Source anchors: Buffett Partnership Limited; $7.51 per share; $16.5 million net current assets; $4.5 million tax loss carryforwards; Seabury Stanton; $11.375 offer.

V. The Transition 1965-1967

Summary: After Buffett takes control in 1965, Berkshire cuts textile costs, uses tax loss carryforwards, and begins moving capital into securities and then insurance. Cost of sales falls 10.3% in 1965 while sales fall only 1.4%, and the company reports tax provisions that do not require cash payments, making free cash flow roughly $4.4 million in 1965 and $5.0 million in 1966. Berkshire pays off a $2.5 million debenture, reports $2.9 million of marketable securities in 1965 and $5.4 million at cost in 1966, then owns a 1968 portfolio with American Express, Walt Disney Productions, Investors Diversified Services, Sperry and Hutchinson, and other stocks whose market value is 118.1% above cost.
Analysis: The 10.3% cost of sales decline and tax loss carryforwards explain how a textile mill that had looked chronically poor could briefly produce deployable cash. The $4.4 million free cash flow and $2.5 million debenture repayment show Buffett first improving liquidity and removing conventional leverage before redirecting money. American Express, Walt Disney Productions, and the 118.1% gain in the marketable securities portfolio demonstrate that the key change is not textile recovery; it is capital allocation that moves funds released from textiles into assets with far better prospective returns, using a portfolio layer of financial infrastructure as the temporary parking lot for that released capital.
Source anchors: 10.3% cost of sales; tax loss carryforwards; $4.4 million free cash flow; $2.5 million debenture; American Express; Walt Disney Productions.

VI. The Acquisitions 1967-1969

Summary: This section covers Berkshire's decisive move into insurance, banking, publishing, and other non-textile assets. In March 1967 Berkshire buys National Indemnity and National Fire & Marine for $8.577 million, gaining Jack Ringwalt's Omaha property-and-casualty insurer, $6.7 million of tangible net worth, and roughly $19.4 million of float as Buffett later described it. Berkshire then adds Sun Newspapers, Blacker Printing, Gateway Underwriters, and, in 1969, 81,989 shares of Illinois National Bank and Trust Company of Rockford at $190 per share, a bank built by Eugene Abegg from $250,000 of net worth and $400,000 of deposits in 1931 to $17 million of net worth and $100 million of deposits in 1969. By the end of the decade, textiles supply less than $0.5 million after tax while insurance, banking, marketable securities, and unconsolidated subsidiaries drive the reported $8 million of 1969 earnings.
Analysis: National Indemnity and Jack Ringwalt matter because they give Berkshire a repeatable funding source through float, not just an operating subsidiary with earnings. The $19.4 million float figure turns the $8.577 million purchase into a study of cost-free or low-cost liabilities that Buffett can invest, while Illinois National Bank and Eugene Abegg show the same financial infrastructure pattern in deposit form: conservative leverage, high liquidity, and cash dividends to the parent. The state banking regime that forced Illinois National into one office, the FDIC guarantee it leaned on, and the >80% ownership rule that made its dividends tax-free to Berkshire show institutions deciding how capital moves inside a conglomerate. The $8 million 1969 earnings figure proves that the company emerging from the decade is no longer financially explained by textile margins, and that the capital allocation pivot begun in the transition is now structural.
Source anchors: National Indemnity; Jack Ringwalt; $19.4 million float; Illinois National Bank; Eugene Abegg; $8 million earnings.

VII. The Expansion 1970's

Summary: McDonough uses the 1970s to show Berkshire expanding while the textile operation shrinks. Textile sales fall to $33.4 million in 1973, while insurance net premiums written rise from $28.8 million in 1969 to $50.4 million in 1973 across National Indemnity, reinsurance, Urban Auto, and Home State operations. Berkshire issues $20 million of 8% debt due in 1993, buys Home and Automobile Insurance Company from Victor Raab, starts Cornhusker Casualty and other Home State companies, builds reinsurance volume, invests heavily in GEICO common and preferred stock after the 1975 underwriting disaster, and adds Cypress Insurance, Waumbec Mills, and K&W Products.
Analysis: The $33.4 million textile sales and $50.4 million premiums contrast shrinking old operations with insurance growth funded by retained earnings, debt, and float. The $20 million 8% debt due in 1993 shows Berkshire using financial infrastructure selectively when insurance capacity looks attractive, while Home and Automobile Insurance and Cornhusker Casualty show that internal expansion and acquisitions can fail or require retrenchment when underwriting is poor. GEICO is the section's crucial outside investment because its 1975 collapse tests whether Buffett can separate a damaged balance sheet from a still-powerful direct-marketing cost advantage — the one asset, prized by Commercial Society, that a bad underwriting year cannot erase. The whole decade is, in effect, testing whether the capital allocation thesis of the 1960s survives an industry-wide bear market in insurance pricing.
Source anchors: $33.4 million textile sales; $50.4 million premiums; $20 million 8% debt; Home and Automobile Insurance; Cornhusker Casualty; GEICO.

VIII. The Other Companies

Summary: This section follows Buffett and Munger's parallel vehicles, especially Diversified Retailing, Blue Chip Stamps, and Wesco Financial, as they buy and reshape businesses outside Berkshire proper. Diversified Retailing begins with Buffett, Charlie Munger, and Sandy Gottesman buying Hochschild Kohn, then sells it in 1969 while keeping Associated Retail; Blue Chip Stamps supplies trading-stamp float and acquires See's Candy for $35 million, the Buffalo Evening News for $35.5 million, and major interests in Wesco, Pinkerton's, and other securities. Wesco exits most of Mutual Savings and Loan, invests in General Foods, attempts to buy Detroit International Bridge before Manuel Moroun's Central Cartage wins, and acquires Precision Steel Warehouse in Franklin Park for $15.1 million.
Analysis: Diversified Retailing and Hochschild Kohn show Buffett and Munger abandoning a poor retail purchase rather than defending the original deal — the flexibility the conclusion will generalize into a rule. See's Candy gives Blue Chip a high-return, price-raising business that can distribute cash to the parent: per-pound realization rises from $1.85 to $5.49 while pounds and stores barely move, the purest commercial society case in the book of a franchise whose pricing power outperforms its physical growth. The Buffalo Evening News shows a tougher purchase whose value depends on becoming the dominant Sunday newspaper in Buffalo, a winner-take-all local franchise built on circulation, advertising, and the death of the Courier-Express. Detroit International Bridge and Precision Steel Warehouse display the same analytical habit in different forms: a scarce cross-border toll gateway with cash and pricing room in one case, and a modest but productive steel service center with double-digit returns in the other.
Source anchors: Diversified Retailing; Hochschild Kohn; See's Candy; Buffalo Evening News; Detroit International Bridge; Precision Steel Warehouse.

IX. The Conglomerate

Summary: McDonough then shows Berkshire becoming a consolidated conglomerate whose insurance group, marketable securities, and acquired operating companies dwarf textiles. Berkshire starts consolidating insurance in 1973 after insurance assets grow from $74.2 million in 1970 to $139.5 million in 1972, while textile assets decline to $12.9 million. Insurance float grows from $42.9 million in 1970 to $231.1 million in 1979 and $514.9 million in 1985, and Berkshire's common-stock portfolio includes Affiliated Publications, American Broadcasting Companies, GEICO, Time, the Washington Post Company, and other holdings with $923.2 million of pretax unrealized gains by 1985. The section also adds the Washington Post purchase during the Pentagon Papers, Watergate, and television-license pressure, plus Nebraska Furniture Mart, Rose Blumkin's Omaha business with gross margins near 22.2% and operating expenses around 16.5% of sales.
Analysis: The 1973 consolidation decision reflects the economic fact that insurance, not textiles, had become Berkshire's core source of assets and investable funds. The $231.1 million float and $514.9 million float figures show why Berkshire's financial infrastructure let it hold large equity positions while still writing less insurance when pricing was poor — under-leveraged by industry standards, Berkshire could refuse bad business while compounding the float it already had. The Washington Post Company and Nebraska Furniture Mart are used as concrete examples of Berkshire's later shape: a public security bought amid political and market fear that then repurchased its own stock (Berkshire's stake rising to 13.5% of the company by 1985), and Rose Blumkin's low-cost retail operation bought as a private subsidiary that strengthens earnings without needing the textile business — both expressions of the commercial society logic of protected local franchises, and both proof that the capital allocation machine had outgrown the mill that founded it.
Source anchors: 1973 consolidation; $231.1 million float; $514.9 million float; Washington Post Company; Rose Blumkin; Nebraska Furniture Mart.

X. The Conclusion

Summary: The conclusion argues that Berkshire's result does not excuse Buffett's original textile mistake. McDonough compares textile returns of roughly 1% over 1970-1985 with the insurance group's 13.6% 1970s return on equity, Illinois National's 15.8%, and Associated Cotton's roughly 20% return on capital, then says Buffett could have bought better businesses directly instead of using Berkshire as the vehicle. He develops American Express as the central counterexample: Berkshire owned American Express after the salad-oil scandal period, while the company had Travelers Cheque float of $572.5 million in 1965, a growing credit card business, 423 offices, 84,234 selling outlets, and a brand proven during World War I and the 1933 Bank Holiday. The closing figures put Berkshire's stock at $2,730 in 1985 versus Buffett's $7.51 initial 1962 purchase, a 29.2% compound annual rate, but stress that the real engine was redeploying capital into float, marketable securities, and high-return subsidiaries.
Analysis: The 1% textile returns, Illinois National, and Associated Cotton comparisons convert the ending into a capital allocation calculation instead of a celebration of the Berkshire chart — an explicit opportunity-cost audit in which the mill's persistently weak returns are weighed against the compounding Berkshire might have had if National Indemnity, Illinois National, Associated Retail, Disney, and American Express had been bought directly. American Express and Travelers Cheque float sharpen that calculation because they show a high-quality financial infrastructure business available in the same era without the drag of obsolete looms, inventories, and weak margins; its $572.5 million of interest-free float was richer than Berkshire's own well before Berkshire reached it in 1985. The $2,730 stock price still matters, but McDonough uses it to separate outcome from decision quality: Berkshire succeeded because Buffett and Munger redirected retained earnings and float — overriding the path dependence and institutional drift of the mill that could otherwise have consumed them. The closing portrait of Buffett's 40.3% ownership, his $50,000-class salary, and a stake worth $169.2 billion by 2019 also grounds the book's ending in elite formation: concentrated ownership of a compounding machine, not pay packages, is what builds the fortune.
Source anchors: 1% textile returns; Illinois National; Associated Cotton; American Express; Travelers Cheque float; $2,730 stock price.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • The Buy-Berkshire bid sequence: $7.51 (late 1962) → oral $11.50 repurchase → mailed $11.375 → Buffett buys control → 552,528 shares / 54.3% by early 1966 at $14.86 average cost → BPL 69.99% by April 1969.
  • "Trading dollars": Buffett's 2017-letter framing that National Indemnity's \(6.7 million tangible net worth was fungible with securities Berkshire would have held anyway, leaving only ~\)1.9 million of goodwill to underwrite profits plus ~$19.4 million of float.
  • The dividend-leverage trick: Berkshire owned >80% of Illinois National, so its $1.2 million (1969) and $2 million (1970) dividends flowed to the parent tax-free; the bank had paid over $25 million in dividends by 1980, more than the purchase price.
  • Depreciation vs. replacement capex: Berkshire's 1955-1961 capital expenditures (\(15.1 million) exceeded depreciation (\)13.1 million) in a non-growing business — the recurring clue that "cash earnings" overstate what a declining mill can actually send upstream.
  • See's pricing power: $1.85 → $5.49 per pound (1972-1984, 9.5% CAGR) versus 3.2% volume growth and 2.1% store growth; sales $28.2 million (1971) → $135.9 million (1984, 13.0% CAGR) while operating profit after tax grew 19%/year from $2.1 million (1972) to $11.9 million (1982).
  • Buffalo Sunday war: Blue Chip paid $35.5 million (April 1977) for a paper with no Sunday edition; after losses and antitrust litigation the Courier-Express died (1982) and the News earned $14 million net on $107.9 million of sales in 1985 — 39.4% of the purchase price in a single year.
  • GEICO near-death numbers: $190.9 million underwriting loss (1975), stock $58.88 (1973) → $2.13 (1976); Berkshire bought common at $3.18 average and preferred at $9.77 in 1976, and held 38% by 1985; the loss was caused by an 109.8% loss ratio against a superb 14.4% expense ratio.
  • Published-price crash cases: Pinkerton's $91.50 → $16 (1972-1973) while sales and profits kept growing; Washington Post $38 → $14.75 (1972-1974) amid the Pentagon Papers, Watergate, and license-renewal fights.
  • Washington Post math: Berkshire paid \(22.75/share (\)10.6 million, ~10% of the company) in 1973; by end-1985 the stake was worth $205.2 million (13.5% of the company after Post buybacks).
  • Nebraska Furniture Mart efficiency: gross margin "not much more than" 22.2% against Levitz's 44.4% and Walmart's 27.3%; operating expenses 16.5% vs. Levitz 35.6% and Walmart 19.7% — low margin plus high turnover beats high margin plus high cost.
  • Ambassador Bridge: opened November 1929; ~85% of cross-river trucking; returns on assets over 20% every year 1968-1977; tolls unchanged from 1971 while sales kept compounding — pricing power held in reserve.
  • American Express as counterfactual: $572.5 million of Travelers Cheque float (1965), 60% of the Travelers Cheque market, 423 offices, 84,234 selling outlets, reliability through World War I and the 1933 Bank Holiday; Berkshire's 15,000 shares at $71 (1967) were up 224.3% by 1968.
  • The closing scoreboard: $7.51 (1962) → \(2,730 (1985) = 363.5x, 29.2% CAGR vs. 7.2% for the S&P 500 with dividends; Buffett's own equity compounded 19%/year while he earned a ~\)50,000 salary.
  • Gold comparison: Berkshire equity 20.4%/year vs. gold 20.7%/year (1965-1980, largely inflation), then 24.2% vs. 11.7% over the full 1965-1985 window — "just five years changed the story."
  • Berkshire's under-leverage as discipline: premiums never above parent equity except 1971 (108.4%); debt/equity peaked at 36.9% (1977) and fell to 6.3% (1985) — the structural reason Berkshire could refuse bad insurance pricing.
