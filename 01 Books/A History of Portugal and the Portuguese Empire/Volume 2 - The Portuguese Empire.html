A History of Portugal and the Portuguese Empire: From Beginnings to 1807 Volume 2: The Portuguese Empire

Buy on Amazon — A History of Portugal and the Portuguese Empire: From Beginnings to 1807 Volume 2: The Portuguese Empire

Position in the vault

This is Volume 2 of A. R. Disney's two-volume history, tracing the Portuguese overseas empire from the 1415 conquest of Ceuta to the transfer of the court to Rio de Janeiro in 1807-8. It sits as the sibling volume to the metropolitan history in Volume 1 (which covers Portugal itself, from pre-Roman times to the French invasion of 1807), both resting under the series hub A History of Portugal and the Portuguese Empire. Use the hub note as the landing page: A History of Portugal and the Portuguese Empire (hub).

Within the thematic graph the book anchors the empire-and-periphery cluster and belongs alongside the reading paths Maritime Empire, Peripheries, and Gateway Power and Coercive Labor, Commodity Systems, and Extraction, and the MoC Empire, Trade, and Maritime Worlds. Its closest analytical neighbors are the commercial-polity and seaborne-empire entity notes (notably East India Company and Dutch Republic as Commercial Polity) and the concept cluster built around Empire and Periphery, Maritime Power, Commodity Systems, and Coercive Labor.

I found 12 numbered main chapters in the extract (Chapters 15-26, continuing the numbering of Volume 1): North Africa; Exploring the Coasts of Atlantic Africa; Engaging with Atlantic Africa; The Atlantic Islands and Fisheries; Breakthrough to Maritime Asia; Empire in the East; Informal Presence in the East; Brazil: Seizing and Keeping Possession; Formation of Colonial Brazil; Late Colonial Brazil; Holding on in India; Eastern Empire in the Late Colonial Era: Peripheries. Each is covered below, together with the substantial front matter (Preface and the maps note).

Major people, societies, and motivations

  • The Portuguese crown (Joaóo I through Prince João/regent): the central, diffuse actor whose monopolies, fortresses, governors, reforms, and chronic shortage of manpower give the empire its shape. Its motivation shifts from neo-Reconquest crusade (Henrique, Manuel I) to commercial monopoly (Joaão II, João III) to defensive survival and Pombaline neo-mercantilism.
  • Prince Henrique / Henry the Navigator: the court figure around whom the legendary authorship of Atlantic exploration crystallized; the extract stresses the myth vs. the more modest, neo-Reconquest-and-commerce-driven reality of his sponsorship.
  • Prince João (later João II) then Manuel I: patron of the Mina and Cape routes respectively; Manuel carries the crusading "Manueline dreaming" of a Christian east and titles himself "lord of the conquest, navigation, and trade of Ethiopia, Persia and India."
  • Vasco da Gama: the service nobleman commanding the first and fourth voyages to India; his 1497-9 expedition, diplomacy with "the other," and failure of the Calicut embassy.
  • Afonso de Albuquerque: governor (1509-15) whose seizures of Goa, Melaka, and Hurmuz created the skeleton of the Estado da Índia; the chapter's key individual, embodying both crusading zeal and pragmatic co-option of non-Christians.
  • Fidalgos, soldados, casados, and moradores: the social strata of the formal empire, from short-term get-rich-quick gentry to permanent married settlers and informal residents.
  • Estado da Índia viceroys and governors: from Dom Francisco de Almeida (1505-9) to the late-colonial figures like the count of Ericeira and the count of Alvor; thirty-five of sixty governors were also viceroys, governing "from afar" out of Goa.
  • The private traders, soldiers-of-fortune, renegades, and topasses: the informal empire—Diogo Pereira ("the Malabari"), mercenaries like Filipe de Brito e Nicote and Sebastião Tibau, and the "black Portuguese" of Timor.
  • Lusophone colonial societies as collective protagonists: the poderosos and Afro-Portuguese elite of the Cape Verdes, the Saão Tomé roça owners and Angolares, the Brazilian senhores de engenho and bandeirantes, the Macau merchants and ca^mara, the Goan Saraswat Brahmins (Kamats/Mhamais), and the Mozambique prazo-holders and muzungos.
  • Non-Portuguese contact peoples written "into the story": Tupí and Tapuia Amerindians of Brazil, the Kongo/Manicongo monarchy and Queen Nzinga of Matamba, the oba of Benin, the Sadian sharifs of Morocco, the Omanis, Marathas, and the Qing mandarins of Guangzhou and Beijing.

Detailed overview

Disney organizes the Portuguese empire not as a compact territorial dominion but as a set of unequal, widely-dispersed theaters—North Africa, Atlantic Africa, the Atlantic islands, maritime Asia, Brazil, and Mozambique—linked by shipping, royal patronage, private trade, coercion, and chronic shortage of manpower. The Preface states this explicitly: the empire was "an extraordinarily widespread and dispersed entity, only loosely held together—a complex patchwork of disparate parts," so each chapter treats Portugal's presence in a particular geographical region (or, in Chapters 16 and 19, the processes of exploring and opening communications). That geography is the analytical backbone: from Ceuta and the Moroccan fortresses, down the Atlantic African coast, out to Madeira, the Azores, the Canaries, the Cape Verdes, and Saão Tomé, across the Cape route into Goa, Hurmuz, Melaka, and Macau, and finally into Brazil, Timor, and Mozambique.

The book's strongest recurring argument is that Portuguese expansion was always a mix of crown design and private improvisation. Alongside Prince Henrique's voyages, Vasco da Gama's route, Albuquerque's conquests, the carreira da Índia, and the Estado da Índia, Disney continually places lancados in Upper Guinea, private traders in the Indian Ocean, soldiers-of-fortune in Asian ports, prazo-holders in Mozambique, and settlers in Saão Paulo and Angola who operated at the edge of formal control. Empire survives not because Lisbon fully commands it, but because local intermediaries, mestico communities, municipal ca^maras, and religious networks keep Portuguese influence alive where the state cannot police every exchange. (This is the reviewer's inference drawn from the book's repeated contrast of the "imperial" versus "private enterprise" visions that divided the Portuguese east of the Cape.)

Atlantic Africa and Brazil show how early profit-making and violence became structurally tied. Saão Jorge da Mina, Benin, Kongo, Ndongo, Angola, and the Atlantic islands all feed into plantation and slaving systems that culminate in colonial Brazil. Disney does not write the sugar economy, the African slave trade, Minas Gerais gold, or Pombaline neo-mercantilism as separate topics: he shows how labor extraction in Angola, port growth in Bahia and Pernambuco, missionary intervention among Amerindians, and frontier settlement in Saão Paulo and the northern backlands belong to one imperial economy that constantly moves people, silver, sugar, tobacco, cattle, and enslaved Africans across the ocean. The reviewer's reading is that the "destruction of Angola" and the "construction of Brazil" are one process for Disney (he cites Alencastro).

In Asia, Disney treats the sixteenth-century breakthrough and the later struggle for retention as distinct phases. The first depends on long-distance navigation, pepper, diplomacy, and spectacular seizures of chokepoints under Albuquerque; the later chapters on Goa, the Marathas, the Omanis, Macau, Guangzhou, Beijing, Timor, and Mozambique show a thinner and more defensive empire living off brokerage, inter-port trade, municipal resilience, and selective reform. The Portuguese never controlled Asian commerce as a whole; they occupied advantageous positions within it, taxed movement where they could (cartazes, customs houses), and bargained with larger Asian powers.

By the eighteenth century the imperial story is one of persistence under constraint. Pombal appears in Brazil, Goa, and Mozambique as a reformer trying to tighten monopolies, discipline officials, reorder trade, and gather information, but reform repeatedly runs into entrenched colonial elites and local realities. Macau must deal with the mandarins of Guangzhou and relations with Beijing; Goa faces British occupation after decades of rivalry and stagnation; Mozambique's Zambesi valley nearly slips into civil conflict over prazo reform and debt enforcement. Volume 2 therefore ends not with imperial triumph but with a dispersed empire that remained commercially important and politically durable precisely because it had learned to live through compromise, brokerage, and partial control.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Dispersed imperial structure: the empire as a web of unequal theaters held by shipping, fortresses, and brokerage rather than territorial contiguity.
  • Crown design vs. private improvisation: the permanent tension between state monopoly and individual enterprise; nearly every chapter's analytical engine.
  • Coercive labor and commodity extraction: slavery and forced Amerindian labor as structural, not incidental, to sugar, gold, and other commodity systems.
  • Distance, information, and governance: how slow communications ("governing from afar") made local elites, ca^maras, and intermediaries decisive.
  • Two-way contact and cultural hybridity: religion and culture as mutual exchanges; mestico, creole, and lusophone contact societies.
  • Maritime power as route-and-cargo control: sea control, convoys, cartazes, and choke-points rather than mass conquest.
  • Imperial persistence under constraint: survival by adaptation, compromise, and partial control in the face of stronger European and Asian powers.

Core concepts

  • Empire and Periphery: Ceuta, Angola, Goa, Macau, Mozambique, Timor, Brazil, and the Atlantic islands show a dispersed empire governed through forts, ports, islands, settlements, and negotiated peripheries where the metropole's writ thins.
  • Maritime Power: caravels, the Cape route, the carreira da Índia, convoying, fisheries, island staging, and pepper routes make sea control the empire's basic operating condition (chapters 15-20).
  • Commodity Systems: sugar, pepper, gold, tobacco, ivory, cattle, fisheries, and slaves link production zones, shipping routes, ports, taxation, and colonial politics throughout the volume.
  • Coercive Labor: plantation slavery, Angolan captives, Saão Tomé, Brazilian engenhos, mining slavery, Amerindian coercion, and prazo dependence make labor extraction central rather than incidental.
  • Informal Institutions: lancados, casados, municipal ca^maras, mixed communities, missionaries, prazo-holders, and private traders keep Portuguese influence alive beyond formal command.
  • Commercial Society: private traders, merchant dynasties (the Kamats, the New Christian networks), credit systems, and inter-port brokerage sustain the empire where the state cannot.
  • State Capacity: Lisbon's crown monopolies, governors, fortresses, Pombaline reforms, royal trade, and colonial administration repeatedly show how hard it was to execute policy across distance.
  • Chokepoints and Gateways: Gibraltar, the Cape of Good Hope, Hurmuz, Melaka, and Macau/Guangzhou are strategic gateways whose seizure or negotiation conditions the whole empire.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: the padroado, Catholic missions, the Jesuits, the Inquisition, and conversion (of Kongo kings, Brazilian Tupí, Goan Hindus) are inseparable from imperial legitimacy and control.
  • Financial Infrastructure: the Casa da Índia, customs houses, cartazes, asientos, mints, gold and silver flows, and royal monopolies structure imperial extraction and survival.
  • Institutions: ca^maras, Misericordias, vice-royal councils, captaincies, and prazos are the concrete vessels of colonial governance and society.
  • Logistics and Throughput: the empire survives by moving people, silver, sugar, tobacco, cattle, ivory, pepper, officials, missionaries, and enslaved Africans through fragile oceanic corridors.
  • Collapse and Resilience: late Portuguese Asia, Goa, Macau, Mozambique, and Brazil show imperial contraction, adaptation, and survival under stronger rivals and local constraints.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Preface and front matter

Summary: The Preface explains the volume's design: each chapter treats Portugal's presence in a particular geographical region (or, in Chapters 16 and 19, processes of exploring and opening communications), because the empire was an extraordinarily widespread, loosely-held patchwork. It surveys the older synthetic histories of the Portuguese empire and acknowledges debts to Boxer, Thomaz, Subrahmanyam, Newitt, and others, and spells out the deliberate emphasis on writing contact peoples into the story and treating contact as two-way. The front matter also contains the Contents, Abbreviations, a List of Maps, and nineteen geographical maps of the theaters covered. Source anchors: Preface; volume design; historiography; maps note.
Analysis: The Preface is the clearest articulation of Disney's method: to treat the empire as a dispersed patchwork of distinct theaters, so that imperial structure itself is the subject. The stress on two-way contact and on writing non-Portuguese peoples into the narrative is a deliberate corrective to older metropolitan-centered imperial history, and it governs the treatment of Empire and Periphery throughout—these peripheries are not empty possessions to be governed but populated worlds with which the Portuguese had to negotiate. The maps themselves embody the same point: the empire is drawn as scattered nodes and maritime corridors, not contiguous territory.

North Africa

Summary: Disney begins with the 1415 conquest of Ceuta, weighing strategic and commercial explanations against the stronger neo-Reconquest motive of crown and service nobility. Portugal adds Alcácer Ceguer, Arzila, and Tangier, but weak hinterland control, costly garrisons, supply dependence, and Moroccan resistance turn fortified footholds into a prolonged stalemate. Sebastião's destruction at al-Ksar al-Kabir (1578) ends the expansive phase without ending the commitments. After it, the Portuguese fortresses (Ceuta, Tangier, Mazagão) persist as subsidized frontier societies: Jewish intermediaries keep trade moving, moradores demand metropolitan protection while resisting interference, and governors face decaying defenses and empty treasuries. Mazagão is finally abandoned in 1769. Source anchors: Ceuta; neo-Reconquest; al-Ksar al-Kabir; Tangier moradores; Jewish intermediaries; crown subsidies; Meneses family; Mazagão.
Analysis: Ceuta's neo-Reconquest logic makes the first imperial commitment easy to acquire and hard to evaluate by ordinary cost. Al-Ksar al-Kabir proves that battlefield disaster can halt expansion without dissolving the interests built around existing posts. In Tangier, Jewish intermediaries and crown subsidies expose the actual mechanism of survival: commerce and metropolitan transfers sustain a fortress whose moradores simultaneously need and constrain royal authority. Empire and Periphery here describes a path-dependent burden. Strategic value narrows, but honor, faith, offices, and settler claims keep resources flowing to possessions that the crown would not rationally choose afresh—an early demonstration of how Path Dependence and Institutional Drift and formal State Capacity collide with Legitimacy and noble interest.

Exploring the Coasts of Atlantic Africa

Summary: The chapter follows Prince Henrique's sponsorship of Atlantic exploration, the Henrican voyages down the African coast, and the progressive accumulation of maritime knowledge through encounters with rivers, currents, winds, and unfamiliar coasts. Disney brings in the Guinea coast, Diogo Cão, Bartolomeu Dias's rounding of the Cape (1487-8), long-distance voyaging, evolving nautical technology (the caravela latina, the volta do mar, rutters, the quadrant and astrolabe, solar declination tables), and Pêro de Covilhã's overland search toward the Indian Ocean and Prester John. What matters is the gradual solving of distance by ship-design, pilotage, Atlantic return routes, and information gathering rather than one sudden leap from Sagres to India. Source anchors: Prince Henrique; Guinea; Diogo Cão; Bartolomeu Dias; nautical technology; volta do mar; Pêro de Covilhã; Prester John.
Analysis: Prince Henrique and Guinea matter because Disney wants exploration understood as organized accumulation of coastal intelligence, not princely myth. Diogo Cão and Bartolomeu Dias demonstrate how each voyage extended the known limits of Atlantic Africa, while nautical technology made those discoveries repeatable. Pêro de Covilhã broadens the chapter beyond ships alone, showing that Maritime Power depended on linked maritime and diplomatic intelligence about routes, Muslim trade, and eastern polities. The chapter is also the book's clearest statement of Technological Change as incremental problem-solving and of Empire, Frontier, and Periphery as the accumulation of legible coastlines.

Engaging with Atlantic Africa

Summary: Disney turns from discovery to commerce and political entanglement along the African coast. He traces profits on the Saharan fringes (Arguim), competition with other traders and the papal bulls (Romanus Pontifex) and treaties (Alcáçovas, Tordesilhas) that defined the Portuguese sphere, the role of lancados in Upper Guinea, the formation of Portuguese Guinea-Bissau (Cacheu, Bissau), the importance of São Jorge da Mina and its gold, and deeper dealings with Benin, the Niger delta/Whydah, Kongo, Ndongo, and Angola. The chapter culminates in early Portuguese settlement of Angola and the Angolan slave trade, showing how gold, captives, diplomacy, and armed intervention became interdependent. Source anchors: lancados; Arguim; São Jorge da Mina; Benin/Whydah; Kongo; Ndongo; Angola; Angolan slave trade.
Analysis: Lancados and São Jorge da Mina reveal Disney's insistence that Atlantic Africa was never managed by crown officials alone. Benin, Kongo, and Ndongo show Portuguese power working through negotiation, alliance, and violence with organized African states rather than empty coastal space. Angola then matters because the slave trade turned local entanglement into a durable imperial system, linking Empire and Periphery to Coercive Labor as West Central Africa became central to Brazil and later empire. The chapter also foregrounds Commodity Systems and Information and Coordination (via the lancado intermediaries) and the anachronism of State Capacity exercised over African interiors.

The Atlantic Islands and Fisheries

Summary: This chapter surveys Madeira, the Azores, the Canaries, Cape Verde, São Tomé and Príncipe, and the Newfoundland fisheries as laboratories of settlement and extraction. Disney follows Madeira from first occupation into sugar, wine, and over-population; the Azores from discovery to eighteenth-century development (wheat, woad, then wine, flax, and oranges); Portugal's failed attempts in the Canaries; Cape Verde from early growth into a difficult later history of drought and decline; and São Tomé into its role as a slave island with roças and Angolares. He also covers the Newfoundland cod fisheries and the South Atlantic islands (Saint Helena) as staging posts. The islands matter because they combine colonization, plantation agriculture, Atlantic shipping, and fisheries into a system of relay points that prepare the way for Brazil. Source anchors: Madeira; Azores; Canaries; Cape Verde; São Tomé; Príncipe; Newfoundland fisheries; Saint Helena.
Analysis: Madeira and São Tomé show how islands could become productive imperial machines through sugar, labor coercion, and maritime positioning; the captaincy institution developed here was later used in Brazil. The Azores and Cape Verde matter for a different reason: they served as settlements, stopovers, and strategic platforms (Angra's role for the Indiamen) inside a widening Atlantic rather than merely as plantations. By adding the Newfoundland fisheries and Saint Helena, Disney makes Maritime Power broader than conquest: Portuguese expansion also depended on seasonal extraction, route staging, and shipping discipline. The chapter is a laboratory for Coercive Labor (especially São Tomé) and Ecological Constraint, and the slave-island roças prefigure Commodity Systems at scale.

Breakthrough to Maritime Asia

Summary: Disney narrates the route to India through Vasco da Gama's first voyage (1497-9), early encounters with Asian merchants and rulers and the problem of "getting to know the other," Manuel I's expansive "Manueline dreaming," Albuquerque's decisive interventions, post-Albuquerquian consolidation, and increasingly formal diplomacy with Asian powers. He treats the new oceanic connection as a mixture of ignorance, audacity, and rapid learning. Albuquerque's conquests of Goa (1510), Melaka (1511), and Hurmuz are given in detail, along with the failed diplomacy of Tomé Pires in China and the escalating pattern of embassies, amizade/irmandade, and pay-and-tribute relations. Source anchors: Vasco da Gama; Manuel I; Albuquerque; Goa; Melaka; Hurmuz; diplomacy; "the other"; Tomé Pires.
Analysis: Vasco da Gama and India define the practical breakthrough, but Disney refuses to leave the story at navigational success. Manuel I and Albuquerque convert that route into imperial ambition, while diplomacy and the problem of "the other" show how quickly the Portuguese had to operate inside established political and commercial worlds they barely understood. The chapter anchors Information and Coordination and Chokepoints and Gateways, because empire in Asia required learning, translation, diplomacy, and adaptation after arrival, not automatic domination once the Cape route had been found. It also links Maritime Power and Religion and Legitimacy, as Albuquerque's conquests fused crusading zeal with pragmatic co-option of Hindus and non-Christians.

Empire in the East

Summary: Here Disney describes the Estado da Índia as a maritime empire built on ports, customs, convoying, monopolies, and long-distance administration. He covers the crown's pepper trade and royal monopoly, tapping into the inter-port trade (including corso/piracy and cartazes), the carreira da Índia, governing from afar out of Goa (viceroys, ca^maras, the padroado church), renewed resurgent expansionism, and the seventeenth-century losses (Hurmuz 1622, Melaka 1641, Sri Lanka, Kerala) that exposed the fragility of the system against the VOC and EIC. Goa, pepper, the carreira da Índia, royal monopoly, overseas officials, and attrition at the hands of stronger rivals all define this chapter's account of formal empire in the East. Source anchors: Estado da Índia; Goa; pepper trade; carreira da Índia; royal monopoly; cartazes; governing from afar; seventeenth-century losses.
Analysis: The Estado da Índia and Goa show the institutional core of Portuguese Asia, but the pepper trade and royal monopoly reveal how dependent that core was on profitable circulation. The carreira da Índia made command, personnel, and bullion movement possible, yet seventeenth-century losses demonstrated how exposed the whole arrangement was to competition and distance. Disney uses those anchors to show Maritime Power as route-and-cargo control, not broad territorial occupation, and Logistics and Throughput as decisive because imperial command depended on whether ships, bullion, officials, soldiers, and pepper could keep moving on schedule. The chapter is also a study of State Capacity and Financial Infrastructure straining against Logistics and Throughput and the colonial world's own momentum.

Informal Presence in the East

Summary: Disney next shifts to Portuguese life beyond direct crown structures, following private traders in western and eastern maritime Asia, soldiers-of-fortune, informal settlements, and settler communities that lived among non-Portuguese populations. He includes casados, private trade in the Bay of Bengal and China-Japan trade (Macau, Nagasaki), mercenaries like Filipe de Brito e Nicote and Sebastião Tibau, informal settlements and moradores, muzungos and prazo-holders in Mozambique, and Catholics in an alien world. Private trade, inter-port brokerage, mixed communities, local wives, missionaries, and autonomous settlers turn this chapter into a study of how empire persisted outside official blueprints. Source anchors: private traders; soldiers-of-fortune; informal settlements; casados; Macau; muzungos; prazo-holders; Catholics.
Analysis: Private traders and soldiers-of-fortune matter because Disney wants the reader to see Portuguese Asia as socially diffuse, not neatly bounded by state institutions. Informal settlements and Catholics show how religion, marriage, and commerce created durable connections in places where governors and fleets were weak or absent. Muzungos and prazo-holders connect Asia to Africa, demonstrating Informal Institutions: unofficial intermediaries often preserved Portuguese presence more effectively than the crown's formal apparatus. The chapter also develops Commercial Society and Religion and Legitimacy, since trader-settlers drifted between faiths and identities even as the Jesuits worked the padroado missions.

Brazil: Seizing and Keeping Possession

Summary: Disney opens Brazil with early voyages (Cabral 1500, Coelho), the age of feitorias and the brazilwood trade, then moves into Amerindian societies and their culture, the first century of settlement (proprietary captaincies, the governor-generalate at Salvador, Mem de Sá), the disintegration of coastal indigenous worlds, the impact of the Jesuits and their aldeamentos, and the major European threat posed by the Dutch conquest of Pernambuco. He follows Count Johan Maurits of Nassau-Siegen and the eventual end of Netherlands Brazil, making Brazil a contested colony from the outset. Feitorias, Amerindians, Jesuits, Pernambuco, Johan Maurits, and the WIC show that possession of Brazil had to be made real through colonization, alliance, mission, and war. Source anchors: feitorias; Amerindians; Tupí; Jesuits; aldeamentos; Pernambuco; Johan Maurits; WIC; Netherlands Brazil.
Analysis: Feitorias and Amerindians show how weak the first Portuguese foothold was and how much depended on negotiation with indigenous societies. The Jesuits mattered because mission policy altered labor, settlement, and alliance, while Pernambuco and Johan Maurits demonstrate that Brazil's fate was also decided by European rivalry and plantation wealth. Disney uses those anchors to show State Capacity in colonial form: holding Brazil required a society capable of resisting indigenous autonomy, Dutch seizure, and internal fragmentation. The chapter also grounds Coercive Labor (Amerindian enslavement) and Religion and Legitimacy, and its account of the Dutch wars links Collapse and Resilience at the level of a colony nearly lost and recovered.

Formation of Colonial Brazil

Summary: This chapter tracks the making of colonial Brazil through brazilwood traders (trees and traders), sugar planting and the engenhos, the African slave trade to Brazil, ports and plantations, farms and ranches, Portuguese colonists and miscegenation, early colonial slavery and slave society, escapees, the free poor and social control, and the outward movement from São Paulo into the southern and northern interiors. Disney links Pernambuco, Bahia, plantations, ranches, slave imports, free poor populations, and paulista expansion into one connected colonial order. Brazil is becoming a land of engenhos, Atlantic ports, coercive labor, mixed populations, and restless frontier movement rather than a string of coastal outposts. Source anchors: brazilwood; sugar; engenhos; African slave trade; plantations; São Paulo; miscegenation; quilombos; free poor.
Analysis: Sugar and plantations define the economic center of early colonial Brazil, but the African slave trade shows what made that center function at scale. São Paulo and the free poor matter because Disney is also tracing a society that spills beyond the plantation coast into ranching and raiding frontiers. By pairing miscegenation with slavery, he links Commodity Systems to Coercive Labor: colonial Brazil became socially distinctive without becoming less coercive. The chapter's account of the bandeiras and quilombos (Palmares) also develops Empire, Frontier, and Periphery and Frontier Systems, while its slave society analysis is central to Coercive Labor as a total social order.

Late Colonial Brazil

Summary: Disney follows Brazil after the Dutch wars through sugar, tobacco, cattle, the great mineral boom, Minas Gerais in the age of gold and diamonds, the free population and slavery in Minas Gerais, Pombaline and post-Pombaline neo-mercantilism, the late-eighteenth-century economic resurgence (cotton, rice, cacao, coffee), frontier extension and the establishing of borders (treaties of Madrid and San Ildefonso, Portugal and the uti possidetis), and early intimations of separation (the Minas Conspiracy of 1788-9, the Bahian conspiracy of 1798). Minas Gerais, gold, diamonds, tobacco, cattle, border making in the north and south, and colonial reform turn Brazil into the empire's dominant possession. Source anchors: Minas Gerais; gold; diamonds; tobacco; cattle; neo-mercantilism; Pombal; separation; Tiradentes.
Analysis: Minas Gerais and gold matter because they reorder the imperial balance in favor of Brazil and generate new centers of population, taxation, and slavery. Tobacco and cattle show that mining did not replace older sectors so much as interact with them, while neo-mercantilism represents Lisbon's attempt to capture more of this enlarged colonial wealth. By ending with separation, Disney connects Financial Infrastructure to Empire and Periphery: imperial success in Brazil eventually created a colony too large, rich, and internally developed to remain politically inert forever. The chapter's detailed border and frontier material, and its treatment of the conspiracies, further develops Frontier Systems and Path Dependence and Institutional Drift as imperial success breeds the conditions of imperial dissolution.

Holding on in India: The Late Seventeenth and Eighteenth Centuries

Summary: Disney treats late colonial India as a prolonged struggle to remain relevant around Goa after earlier dominance had ebbed. He surveys rivalry with other European powers (the English alliance and the cession of Bombay, the French interlude, Luso-Dutch tensions), conflict with Omanis and Marathas (the loss of Muscat, Mombasa, and Basein/Salsette to the Marathas), old and new patterns in the intercontinental and inter-port trade (the "Brazil solution" of the carreira da Índia, tobacco, banias, Saraswat merchants), the Estado da Índia's struggle for recovery, early-eighteenth-century stagnation, mid-century revival and the New Conquests, Pombal's reforms and the expulsion of the Jesuits, the Pinto "conspiracy" (1787), and the eventual British occupation of Goa (1799-1815). Goa, Marathas, Omanis, reform, conspiracy, and British pressure define a chapter in which survival depends on adaptation rather than renewed supremacy. Source anchors: Goa; Omanis; Marathas; Bombay; Pombal; Jesuits; Pinto "conspiracy"; British occupation; carreira da Índia.
Analysis: Goa is the indispensable anchor here because Disney is studying how one city and its dependent enclaves kept an eastern empire alive after major contraction. Omanis and Marathas expose the regional military pressures that Portuguese power had to absorb, while Pombal and the Pinto "conspiracy" show that reform could destabilize as well as strengthen colonial rule. British occupation then confirms Collapse and Resilience at imperial scale: Portuguese India survived, but under the shadow of far stronger powers. The chapter also deepens Commercial Society (the Hindu and New Christian merchant networks), Religion and Legitimacy (Inquisition and Pombaline secularization), and State Capacity as imperial administration narrowed to a compact territory.

Eastern Empire in the Late Colonial Era: Peripheries

Summary: The final chapter ranges across Macau, Guangzhou, Beijing, Timor, the Swahili coast, and Mozambique to show what remained of the eastern empire beyond India. Disney examines Macau's recovery and late-eighteenth-century trade, Macanese society and government, relations with the mandarins of Guangzhou and with Beijing (the six embassies, the Chinese Rites controversy), the Macau ca^mara, the tenuous hold in Timor (topasses, Hornay-Costa rivalry, sandalwood, the move to Dili), the loss of the Swahili coast (Fort Jesus, 1698), the ivory, gold, and slave trades of Mozambique, the entry of banias, prazo society after 1650, and the reform crises of the Pombaline era in the Zambesi valley. Macau, Guangzhou, Beijing, Timor, Mozambique, banias, and prazo reform give the chapter its dispersed but concrete shape. Source anchors: Macau; Guangzhou; mandarins; Beijing; Timor; topasses; Swahili coast; Mozambique; banias; prazo reform.
Analysis: Macau and Guangzhou show Portuguese survival through municipal negotiation and brokerage inside a Chinese political world they did not control; the ca^mara's primacy underscores how local institutions outlasted the crown's reach. Timor and Mozambique matter because they reveal different edges of the same late empire: one a fragile island toehold of topasses and dwindling sandalwood, the other a more ambitious but unstable African zone of prazos, ivory, and mercantile intermediaries. Banias and prazo reform make Empire and Periphery precise: the eastern peripheries could not be governed by decree from Lisbon alone and had to be worked through local commercial and landed powers. The chapter is a final demonstration of Informal Institutions, Commercial Society, and the Qing Empire as the sovereign frame within which Macau operated.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • Opening date and moment of the whole empire: the 1415 Ceuta expedition under João I, launched from Lagos in the Algarve with ~20,000 men.
  • Lisbon's overseas expansion "starts" at Ceuta (1415) and "ends the expansive phase" at al-Ksar al-Kabir (1578); the last North African outpost (Mazagão) is abandoned only in 1769.
  • The "empire" spans two volume halves: Atlantic/North Africa/Atlantic islands/Brazil plus maritime Asia/Estado da Índia/China/Mozambique.
  • Regency-to-crown institutional threads: the captaincy (Madeira→Azores→Brazil), the feitoria (Arguim 1445→Saão Jorge da Mina 1482→Calicut 1500), the carreira da Índia (annual Lisbon-Goa round trips of ~18 months), and the ca^mara / Misericordia as the local unit of self-governance.
  • Diplomatic vocabulary to recall: amizade (friendship) and irmandade (brotherhood) offered to Asian rulers; cartazes (safe-conducts); mare clausum; paréas (tribute).
  • The Estado da Índia as largely urban: in the 1580s its five customs houses (Goa, Hurmuz, Melaka, Basein, Diu) yielded over 85% of viceroyalty revenue.
  • Key numbers for retrieval: Gama's voyage lasted ~10 months out and ~11 back; Goa had ~2,000 casados in the 1550s; a third of crown revenues came from the land in Goa by the 1630s; ~10,000 slaves/year left Angola in the 17th century, ~18,500/year to Brazil 1701-1810; Minas Gerais population ~200,000-250,000 by 1739, Minas slaves ~54% of population in 1805.
  • Commodity pinpoint: Madeira sugar peak = 1490s-early 1500s; Saão Tomé sugar cycle 1490s-1630s; Brazilian sugar ~350 mills by 1630; Bahian tobacco as the trade's near-currency (fumo) and cash crop for the crown after 1674; Minas gold officially 18,000-20,000 kg/year mid-1720s to early 1750s.
  • The "structural break": after the late 1540s the imperial focus shifts from Asia toward the South Atlantic, with Brazil's governorship at Salvador (1549) and the Brazil-Angola slaving nexus.
  • The mid-century crisis in Asia: Hurmuz (1622), Melaka (1641), Sri Lanka/Kerala (1656-63) fall to the VOC/EIC; by 1663 the Estado da Índia is reduced to Macau, part of Timor, East African interests, and the Indian enclaves.
  • Pombal in three theaters: Brazil (monopoly companies, gold/quinto foundries, Amerindian policy, expulsion of the Jesuits 1759), Goa (Jesuit expulsion, racial equality decrees, abolition of the Inquisition 1774, New Conquests), and Mozambique (monopoly companies, prazo reform).
  • The unique Macau settlement: governor appointed by Goa had authority only in military affairs; the ca^mara controlled civil administration, because the Guangdong mandarins recognized only the ca^mara as legitimate government.
  • Frontier/border treaty chain: Tordesilhas (1494) → Madrid (1750) → renounced (1761) → San Ildefonso (1777, never ratified) → invert to "uti possidetis" as the operative principle for Brazil's continental borders.
