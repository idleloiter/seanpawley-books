Pebble in the Sky

Buy on Amazon — Pebble in the Sky

Position in the vault

This book is the third locally represented volume of Galactic Empire, the early Asimov sequence that precedes the collapse-management institutions of Foundation Sequence. Read after The Stars, Like Dust and The Currents of Space, it shows the most consolidated imperial order of the three: Trantor rules the Galaxy through procurators, garrisons, a Bureau of Outer Provinces, and the brief legal fictions of local autonomy, while Earth has been reduced to a radioactive, culturally quarantined periphery whose own institutions — the High Ministry and the Society of Ancients — exercise a power the Empire will not touch. Thematically the note belongs to the vault's empire-and-periphery cluster (also mapped in Science Fiction and Speculative Orders) and connects directly to the hub's concern with radioactive Earth, distorted memory, and unsatisfied legitimacy — a useful prehistory to the imperial scale and collapse-management logic of the later Foundation sequence.

Detailed overview

Pebble in the Sky begins with an old twentieth-century Chicago tailor, Joseph Schwartz, stepping over a Raggedy Ann doll and landing thousands of years later on a radioactive Earth inside the Galactic Empire. The novel uses that impossible displacement to make Earth strange twice over: Schwartz recognizes the planet as his own lost home, while Imperial archaeologist Bel Arvardan sees it as a despised backwater whose radioactive soil may nevertheless prove that humanity began there.

The book's Earth is not a neutral archaeological site. It is a quarantined and humiliated planet ruled locally by a High Minister and the Society of Ancients, supervised externally by Procurator Ennius from an artificial Imperial enclave near the Himalayas. Earthmen live under rationing, restricted territories, anti-Outsider hatred, and the Sixty, a euthanasia custom that kills most people at sixty because the planet's usable land and food supply are too limited. Asimov makes the planet's poverty political: customs that look barbaric to outsiders are also survival mechanisms inside a world other humans have rejected.

Three scientific strands converge around Dr. Shekt's Synapsifier. Shekt presents it as a machine for improving learning capacity by altering neuroprotein transmission, but the Ancients have forced him to use it secretly on selected scientists. When Schwartz is treated by accident, his primitive skull, facial hair, wisdom teeth, open appendix, photographic memory, and new telepathic abilities make him both a living fossil and a weapon. His mind-reading is not decorative; it becomes the only way to pierce the plot's political and evidentiary deadlocks.

Arvardan's story is a conversion from liberal prejudice to embodied loyalty. He arrives from Baronn in the Sirius Sector believing in the oneness of humanity, yet he flinches at Earthmen, at the Sixty, at Pola Shekt, and at the thought of intermarriage. His love for Pola does not erase anti-Terrestrial bigotry by sentiment alone. It forces him to act against Sirian assumptions, Imperial arrogance, Lieutenant Claudy's violence, and his own instinct to treat Earth as a specimen rather than a people.

The conspiracy plot turns Earth grievance into galactic terror. Balkis, the High Minister's Secretary and real power behind the Ancients, plans to launch concentrated Common Fever virus in guided space missiles from the Temple of Senloo, then use Earth's immunity and antitoxin as leverage over a dying Galaxy. Shekt's warning to Arvardan, Schwartz's reading of Balkis's mind, and the desperate flight to Fort Dibburn all show how hard it is to make institutions act when evidence comes from despised Earthmen, a love-struck archaeologist, a telepathic displaced tailor, and a scientist already near the Sixty.

The final chapters refuse an easy heroic hearing. Ennius has the authority to save the Galaxy, but he hesitates because Balkis's counterstory is procedurally plausible: Schwartz is unknown, Shekt has hidden Synapsifier records, Arvardan may have been manipulated, and Pola sounds hysterical inside a formal inquiry. The Galaxy is saved only because Schwartz acts outside that hearing, seizes a pilot's anti-Earth hatred, and destroys Senloo before Balkis can prove his victory. The ending then imagines Earth's repair through soil convoys, labor battalions, honorary naturalization, and a future in which Earth can become "a planet among planets" rather than the Empire's radioactive pariah.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Joseph Schwartz: a sixty-two-year-old retired tailor from Chicago (1949) who is displaced across millennia between two footsteps. His arc moves from bewildered survival (recognizing Earth, evading the Sixty, and the accidental Synapsifier treatment) through horrified self-discovery (Mind Touch, telepathy, and the accidental killing of Natter) to the deliberate choice to save a Galaxy he never asked to join. He is the book's living fossil, its only archive of the lost Earth, and — once he can transmit motor commands — its decisive weapon.
  • Bel Arvardan: a thirty-five-year-old Senior Archaeologist of the Imperial Archaeological Institute from Baronn, Sirius Sector, committed to the Radiation Theory that humanity originated on one world. He begins as a tolerant Sirian who still cannot stomach intermarriage, and the plot forces him to translate belief in the oneness of humanity into action: breaking an officer's arm, loving Pola, enduring the humiliation of Fort Dibburn, and finally volunteering to rebuild Earth.
  • Dr. Affret Shekt: the fifty-six-year-old biophysicist at Chica's Institute for Nuclear Research who built the Synapsifier. The Society of Ancients suppresses his results and uses the machine on selected Zealot biologists; near his own Sixty and under constant surveillance, he risks treason to reveal the virus plot, gives the proof about F. Smitko, and provides the theory (receive and transmit brain fields) that lets Schwartz control others.
  • Pola Shekt: Shekt's daughter and laboratory technician. She is the first person to teach Schwartz language and is the novel's conscience and romantic center; her trust converts Arvardan's tolerance into commitment, and her invitation to Schwartz to read her mind provides the moral audit that turns him from suicidal Earth loyalty toward saving the Galaxy. She refuses to be bought when Balkis offers their lives, proving "her side" is humanity.
  • Lord Ennius: Procurator of Earth, a career diplomat suffocating in the Himalayan enclave. His motives are reputation, career, and the prevention of rebellion; his caution saves him from being a tyrant and nearly loses the Galaxy, and his final apology and refusal to compromise with Balkis restore his honor.
  • Balkis: the High Minister's Secretary, snub-nosed, wry-faced, and unknown to the public, who holds "the reality" of power behind the High Minister's "semblance." His motive is not Earth's freedom but his own personal dictatorship; his willed triumph at the hearing, Schwartz reads, matters to him more than safety. Balanced cruelty (Synapsifier-imposed imbecility, diluted fever virus) makes him the book's villain.
  • The High Minister: the ceremonially supreme Earthman, arbiter of Custom and the Sixty, who announces exemptions and judges quota-breakers. He is not stupid — his refusal to let Arvardan prove Earth's antiquity is argued honestly — but he never notices that Balkis rules through him.
  • Arbin and Loa Maren, with Grew: a farm family outside Chica. They conceal the crippled Grew past his Sixty (a capital violation of Custom), use the unregistered Schwartz as a third worker to meet a quota calculated for three, and feed the plot's contingency: Arbin smuggles Schwartz to the Institute, and the family's technical crime later makes them blackmailable.
  • Natter: a fruit-stand keeper and messenger for the Society of Ancients who watches Shekt and profits from the chaos of Chica; Schwartz reads his hostile Mind Touch on the highway and kills him without touching him.
  • Lieutenant Marc Claudy: an anti-Earth officer of the Chica garrison whose broken arm, slaps, and "Earthie-squaw" cruelties embody Imperial bigotry as routine; Schwartz weaponizes that same hatred to make him fly the raid that destroys Senloo.

Major linkages

  • Galactic Empire — the third represented volume; the hub's themes of consolidated imperial scale, radioactive Earth, and unsatisfied legitimacy.
  • The Stars, Like Dust and The Currents of Space — sibling novels in the sequence; here Earth's lost status is fully legible rather than a half-remembered rumour.
  • Earth as Administrative Core — the Ancients' doctrine that Earth is the appointed center of humanity, and the archaeology that makes the claim testable.
  • Empire and Periphery, State Capacity, Coercive Labor, Chokepoints and Gateways, Information and Coordination, Knowledge Preservation, Legitimacy and Succession, Technological Change, Language and Ideology — the book's principal conceptual surface.
  • Intelligence, Warning, and Institutional Self-Deception — the warning that cannot move through Ennius's hearing.
  • Collapse, Resilience, and Civilizational Survival — the soil-convoy reconstruction of Earth's epilogue.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Time displacement as social cognition: Schwartz's ordinary twentieth-century past gives him the one set of referents (Chicago, St. Louis, the two rivers) with which to read the future Galaxy.
  • Reciprocal hatred: Sirian anti-Terrestrialism and Earth's anti-Outsider hatred each justify the other; love and scientific tolerance must overcome both.
  • The Sixty and ecological arithmetic: with only twenty million people supportable, age, Census registration, and quotas turn life duration into an administered allocation.
  • Coerced science: the Synapsifier is at once a legitimate learning technology, a machine the Ancients secretly use on Zealot scientists, and the source of a bacteriological weapon.
  • The credibility problem of urgent truth: Schwartz's telepathy, Shekt's age, Pola's tears, and Arvardan's love all read as noise in a formal inquiry.
  • Mind as evidence and weapon: telepathy functions as moral audit (reading Arvardan's buried prejudice, Pola's sincerity, Balkis's purpose) and as an instrument of stasis, stun, kill, and finally motor control.
  • Reconstruction without charity: Earthmen reject relocation and choose labor battalions and soil convoys to remake their own world.

Core concepts

  • Ecological Constraint: radioactive soil and limited usable land make rationing, quotas, and the Sixty into coercive survival institutions.
  • Empire and Periphery: Ennius governs Earth from a protected artificial enclave that embodies Imperial separation from the radioactive planet it supervises.
  • Legibility: Schwartz's archaic anatomy makes his displacement readable to Shekt before anyone can explain it historically.
  • Informal Institutions: Balkis rules through the Society of Ancients, reports, and surveillance beneath the High Minister's ceremonial authority.
  • Chokepoints and Gateways: the Ancients pair virus missiles with antitoxin so a poor quarantined planet can hold the wider Galaxy at a compulsory biological gateway.
  • Information and Coordination: Balkis defeats urgent evidence by translating it into a plausible procedural counterstory before Ennius.
  • Collapse and Resilience: normal-soil convoys and labor battalions turn rescue into the long reconstruction of a damaged world.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter 1 — Between One Footstep and the Next

Summary: Joseph Schwartz, a retired tailor in suburban Chicago, walks past children and a Raggedy Ann doll while thinking of Browning, World War III, the atom bomb, his wife, daughters, grandson, and his comfortable old age. Nearby, Dr. Smith and a young assistant at a nuclear laboratory observe a platinum crucible, uranium below expected critical mass, a corona, heavy radiation, burned cabinets, silvery streaks on concrete, and a strange beam that vanishes into space. Schwartz feels dizziness between one step and the next, finds his shoe sliced impossibly smooth, discovers that the city and even familiar geography have disappeared, and reaches a farm where Arbin Maren and Loa speak an unknown language. Source anchors: Joseph Schwartz; suburban Chicago; Raggedy Ann; Dr. Smith; uranium; platinum crucible; sliced shoe; Arbin Maren.
Analysis: Joseph Schwartz's ordinary Chicago walk makes the time displacement personal before the Galaxy enters the book. Dr. Smith, uranium, and the platinum crucible supply a pseudo-scientific accident rather than prophecy, while the sliced shoe proves that Schwartz's body crossed some boundary the present cannot explain. The melted crucible and vanishing beam frame the accident as Technological Change running ahead of any institution able to contain it, so the whole novel opens inside the deeper history of the Galactic Empire. Arbin Maren and Loa matter because the first future humans Schwartz meets are not Imperial officials but anxious Earth farmers. The Raggedy Ann doll and Browning line keep his lost twentieth-century home emotionally present after the future Earth has become unrecognizable.

Chapter 2 — The Disposal of a Stranger

Summary: On the Maren farm, Grew reads about Bel Arvardan's archaeological expedition to Earth and quarrels with Arbin and Loa over the Society of Ancients, Outsiders, the Census Board, farm quotas, and the possibility that Schwartz is a useful unregistered stranger. Arbin and Loa hide Grew because his disability should have brought a premature Sixty, and they hope Schwartz can replace him as a third worker for production quotas. Schwartz wakes confused, unable to understand their speech, recognizes none of the clothing or household technology, and learns only that these people are frightened enough to use him but not cruel enough to throw him away. Source anchors: Grew; Bel Arvardan; Society of Ancients; Census Board; Arbin; Loa; farm quota; Sixty.
Analysis: Grew's news about Bel Arvardan ties the farm to the Imperial archaeological plot before Schwartz knows what Earth has become. The Society of Ancients, Census Board, farm quota, and Sixty turn Ecological Constraint into coercive survival arithmetic: Arbin and Loa hide Grew and use Schwartz because limited land and food make household kindness criminal. Earth customs are desperate and rationalized by scarcity at the same time. The same arithmetic makes Schwartz a concealed third worker, so the chapter also introduces Coercive Labor organized through registered quotas and the printable reach of a State Capacity that runs on Census registration.

Chapter 3 — One World—or Many?

Summary: Bel Arvardan arrives at the Procurator's enclave north of the Himalayas, where artificial lawns, flower gardens, a nonradioactive palace, and Imperial comfort stand apart from the radioactive wilderness of Earth. Arvardan tells Lord Ennius that Earth's radioactivity may be artificial and that life must have originated before the soil became radioactive, which supports the Radiation Theory against orthodox Merger Theory. Ennius warns him about anti-Terrestrialism in the Sirius Sector, the Book of the Ancients, Shekt, Earthmen's taboo against Outsiders reading sacred texts, and Imperial officers who treat Earthmen as inferior. Arvardan defends the unity of protein-based life but hears enough about Shekt's Synapsifier to send Ennius anxiously toward Chica. Source anchors: Bel Arvardan; Lord Ennius; Himalayas; Radiation Theory; Merger Theory; Book of the Ancients; Sirius Sector; Shekt.
Analysis: Bel Arvardan and Lord Ennius set up the book's main intellectual and administrative lines: archaeology says Earth may be original, while Imperial governance treats Earth as a radioactive problem. The Himalaya enclave makes Empire and Periphery physical by separating Ennius from the planet he rules. Radiation Theory and Merger Theory make Arvardan's science politically explosive because proving Earth's antiquity can strengthen the Ancients, while Shekt and the Book of the Ancients suggest that local myth and Imperial science may describe the same past. The forbidden Book of the Ancients, with its mythic history of a nonradioactive Earth, is an early case of Language and Ideology: sacred narrative names the same contested origin that the Ancients deploy to claim Earth as the future's Earth as Administrative Core.

Chapter 4 — The Royal Road

Summary: Arbin takes Schwartz to Chica and the Institute for Nuclear Research, hoping to use Dr. Shekt's Synapsifier without revealing names, registration, or the hidden invalid Grew. At the same time Ennius visits Shekt, complains about lead-impregnated clothing, metaboline, Earth customs, radioactive air, delegations demanding death penalties, the Sixty, and Earth's pariah status. Shekt explains the Synapsifier as a device acting on neuroprotein, nerve-cell gaps, microcurrents, dielectric constants, and learning capacity, while Ennius suggests that a safe machine could win Earth emigration concessions from the Empire. Pola Shekt enters as Shekt's daughter and technician, announces the volunteer, and sends Arbin into the experiment's machinery. Source anchors: Chica; Institute for Nuclear Research; Dr. Shekt; Synapsifier; Ennius; metaboline; Pola Shekt; neuroprotein.
Analysis: Chica and the Institute connect Arbin's illegal need for labor to Shekt's coerced science. The Synapsifier is important because its neuroprotein explanation lets Asimov turn learning, telepathy, and later mind control into one machine-driven development: a single Technological Change caught between medical promise and political surveillance. Ennius's metaboline and lead clothing show Imperial fear as bodily routine, while his emigration proposal hints at a humane solution Earth extremists will not accept. Ennius's bargaining over emigration, the receptionist's register, and Arbin's panicked refusal to sign all show how Imperial and Earth institutions alike use State Capacity to control who may cross their boundaries. Pola Shekt enters as more than a technician; she is the person who will join the scientific secret to Arvardan's political access.

Chapter 5 — The Involuntary Volunteer

Summary: Shekt examines Schwartz as an unauthorized volunteer and notices details that do not fit contemporary Earth: strange skull fissures, facial hair, chest hair, thirty-two teeth, a primitive formation, and later an open vermiform appendix. The Synapsifier treatment uses hypno-pills, a diamagnetic field, electrodes, Ullster skull analysis, microcurrent graphs, platinum hair-lets, and carefully adjusted machinery, and Schwartz survives even though many experimental animals died of brain protein denaturation. Arbin leaves terrified after being told to return in a week, Shekt lies to Ennius that the volunteer nearly died, the young technician reports the violation to the High Minister, and Schwartz begins learning language at impossible speed from Pola. Source anchors: Schwartz; unauthorized volunteer; skull fissures; facial hair; diamagnetic field; Ullster technique; High Minister; Pola.
Analysis: Schwartz's skull fissures, facial hair, and vermiform appendix turn Legibility into biological evidence: Shekt can read time displacement in the body before anyone has a historical explanation. The diamagnetic field and Ullster technique make the Synapsifier risky material science rather than magic, so the machine is a genuine Technological Change whose fruits are then seized by the Ancients. The technician's report to the High Minister and the agents watching the hospital show that medical discovery now runs inside a surveillance State Capacity, while Pola's language lessons transform Schwartz from lost patient into an active interpreter of Earth.

Chapter 6 — Apprehension in the Night

Summary: Ennius spends a sleepless night with Flora at the Procurator's palace, looking at Sirius, Arcturus, Trantor's lost sun, and the artificial gardens while worrying about Shekt, Arvardan, the Society of Ancients, the Synapsifier, and another Earth rebellion. He recalls previous uprisings, the Uprising of 750, the riots over Stannell II's attempt to place the Imperial insignia in Washenn's Council Chamber, and the problem of ruling a planet that refuses visible signs of Imperial domination. Flora argues that Earthmen have been denied equality and that Ennius should warn the High Minister about Arvardan's plan to enter Forbidden radioactive areas while requesting psychologists to test the Synapsifier threat. Source anchors: Ennius; Flora; Sirius; Society of Ancients; Uprising of 750; Stannell II; Imperial insignia; Forbidden Areas.
Analysis: Ennius and Flora make Imperial power look anxious rather than omnipotent. The Uprising of 750 and Stannell II's insignia order show that Earth can provoke real administrative crises despite being militarily weak: Stannell II's order, his assassination, and Edard's reversal are a compressed lesson in Legitimacy and Succession, since the imperial symbol breaks down precisely where Earth's counter-claim — that it is humanity's appointed center — is strongest. That same doctrine, and the Society of Ancients' "Second Kingdom of Earth," let the Ancients imagine Earth as the future's Earth as Administrative Core. The Society of Ancients and Synapsifier worry Ennius because rebellion may now involve more than ritual outrage. Flora's advice to use the High Minister against Arvardan matters because it unintentionally feeds Balkis the exact information he needs to interpret Shekt, Schwartz, and the archaeological expedition as a single Imperial plot.

Chapter 7 — Conversation with Madmen?

Summary: Arvardan travels from Everest to Washenn on a Terrestrial Air Transport Company stratospheric liner, choosing an ordinary Earth passenger route instead of Ennius's cruiser to test his own anti-Terrestrial prejudice. During the flight he watches Earth from the stratosphere, sees radioactive areas glittering in darkness, listens to an elderly couple discuss their fortieth anniversary and upcoming Sixty, and hears passengers joke about euthanasia, Census evasion, selfish old relatives, and Society of Ancients influence. When Arvardan reveals he is from Baronn in the Sirius Sector, most passengers recoil, but Creen approaches him with cynical friendliness. Source anchors: Arvardan; Everest; Washenn; stratospheric liner; Sixty; Census; Creen; Sirius Sector.
Analysis: Arvardan's flight forces him to confront Earthmen as ordinary people before he can keep them as archaeological abstractions. The Sixty and Census are horrifying because passengers discuss death with jokes, arithmetic, and resentment toward evaders: the cabin becomes a classroom of Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy, where an entire population has normalized scheduled death, and behind every joke stands the Ecological Constraint that makes dying by age popular arithmetic. Sirius Sector prejudice is mirrored by the passengers' reaction to Arvardan, proving that hatred moves in both directions. Creen's friendliness is not noble; it is opportunistic and urban, giving Arvardan a more complicated Earth than the categories of victim, fanatic, or inferior.

Chapter 8 — Convergence at Chica

Summary: Schwartz escapes from Shekt's unlocked room into Chica, explores porcelain-like buildings and unfamiliar clothing, enters a Foodomat, and receives food from Granz after Messter dismisses him as a bum. Pola discovers he is gone, searches the city, and is helped by Arvardan, who recognizes Schwartz from the Foodomat and joins her through the streets and a self-service department store. Natter, a fruit-stand owner and messenger for the Society of Ancients, hears rumors of Radiation Fever from Granz and Messter, follows the situation, and appears after the store orders all patrons to exit with registration cards. Schwartz has no card, Pola panics, and Arvardan refuses to abandon her. Source anchors: Schwartz; Chica; Foodomat; Granz; Messter; Pola; Arvardan; Natter.
Analysis: Chica turns Schwartz's language learning into public vulnerability: without money, registration, or social habits, he cannot pass. Granz, Messter, and the Foodomat show ordinary city charity and suspicion in the same scene. The registration-card check shows how a gate sustained by State Capacity — papers, vital statistics, and guards — can turn a harmless stranger into a fugitive in one evening, while Natter's flashed badge reveals that the Society of Ancients runs an Informal Institutions street network of messengers and informers beneath the official patrol. Pola and Arvardan meet through the search for Schwartz, so the romance begins as a rescue problem rather than a social encounter. Natter's role as Society of Ancients messenger makes the registration-card crisis political, because Schwartz's missing identity can expose Shekt and the unauthorized Synapsifier treatment.

Chapter 9 — Conflict at Chica

Summary: The Radiation Fever panic brings Lieutenant Marc Claudy and a decontamination squad from the Chica garrison to the department store, while Natter bargains to remove Schwartz for two hundred credits and Pola reluctantly accepts. Claudy's soldiers clear the square, seal and gas the store with disinfectant lethal to all protoplasm, examine Pola and Arvardan for infection, and treat Pola as an "Earthie-squaw." Arvardan, still assumed to be an Earthman, objects to Claudy's insults, is slapped, breaks the lieutenant's arm, and is struck down with a neuronic whip. At the garrison, his Imperial citizenship forces the colonel to apologize and release Pola, but Pola feels betrayed when Arvardan reveals that he is an Outsider from Sirius. Source anchors: Lieutenant Claudy; Radiation Fever; decontamination squad; Natter; Pola; Arvardan; neuronic whip; Earthie-squaw.
Analysis: Lieutenant Claudy embodies anti-Terrestrialism as military habit: Radiation Fever justifies lethal decontamination, and "Earthie-squaw" turns Pola into a racial insult. The garrison, the decontamination squad, and the neuronic whip make Empire and Periphery violently concrete: an Imperial institution treats Earth bodies as disposable quarantine objects. Arvardan's broken-arm response proves his instinctive decency before Pola knows he is an Outsider, which makes the later revelation more painful. The neuronic whip and colonel's apology also show how Imperial citizenship protects Arvardan in ways unavailable to Pola, exposing how unevenly State Capacity shelters the Sirian who can name himself against the Earthgirl who cannot.

Chapter 10 — Interpretation of Events

Summary: In Washenn, the High Minister and his Secretary Balkis interpret reports from Shekt's technician, Ennius, Arvardan's archaeological request, the Synapsifier, Schwartz's lack of registry, and Shekt's Assimilationist past. The High Minister has ceremonial authority over Custom, the Sixty, rationing, restricted territory, and death judgments, but Balkis holds real power through the Society of Ancients. Balkis argues that Shekt knowingly Synapsified an Outsider, that the Empire may suspect Earth's secret project, and that Arvardan's expedition to Forbidden Areas must be treated as cover for Imperial investigation. Meanwhile Schwartz is returned to the Maren farm, where Arbin, Loa, and Grew resume using him as a laborer under surveillance. Source anchors: High Minister; Balkis; Washenn; Shekt's technician; Synapsifier; Assimilationist; Society of Ancients; Maren farm.
Analysis: The High Minister and Balkis reveal Informal Institutions beneath Earth's public customs. The Sixty, rationing, and restricted territory make the High Minister symbolically central, but Balkis rules through the Society of Ancients, reports, interpretation, and surveillance. The pairing of ceremonial ruler and effective bureaucrat separates Legitimacy from real control, while Balkis's refusal to read any coincidence as innocent shows a peripheral State Capacity that re-politicizes all the information it receives. Shekt's Assimilationist past gives Balkis a lever, and the Maren farm becomes a watched holding pen because Schwartz's labor is useful while his mind and origin are dangerous.

Chapter 11 — The Mind That Changed

Summary: Schwartz spends two months on the Maren farm, grows physically stronger, learns Earth speech, plays chess with Grew, and senses a threatening Mind Touch from the Ministerial Ground beyond the South Hills. During a night-board chess game, he asks Grew about the year 827 G.E., the Galactic Era, Frankenn the First, America, Asia, Russia, Europe, Saturn's rings, the asteroid belt, millions of inhabited worlds, and whether Earth once ruled anything. Grew explains radioactive soil, twenty million Earth people, the Sixty, the Census, exceptions for the High Minister and Ancients, and the fact that disabled people like Grew should be put away. Schwartz realizes that he is sixty-two and therefore already condemned. Source anchors: Maren farm; Grew; chess; 827 G.E.; Frankenn the First; Saturn; Galactic Empire; sixty-two.
Analysis: Grew's chess game turns exposition into discovery because Schwartz verifies the Solar System before accepting that he has crossed time. The 827 G.E. date and Frankenn the First place his twentieth-century memories inside Imperial chronology, while Saturn and the asteroid belt prove this is still Earth's system. The Sixty becomes personal when Schwartz learns he is sixty-two, changing it from local custom into a death sentence that the planet's Ecological Constraint — twenty million people, nothing grows on the glowing soil — makes enforceable. Grew's ignorance of America, Asia, and the other old names shows how little of its own past the Earth of 827 G.E. has preserved, a Knowledge Preservation gap that makes Schwartz's memory the only archive left. The Mind Touch from Ministerial Ground foreshadows the Ancients' surveillance and links Schwartz's new power to political danger.

Chapter 12 — The Mind That Killed

Summary: Schwartz leaves the farm for Chica, hoping Shekt will protect him as a medical, psychological, historical, or archaeological specimen because the Sixty will kill him if he stays. Natter follows him along the highway, pretending chance companionship while carrying a weapon and planning to report Schwartz's destination. Schwartz reads Natter's hostile Mind Touch, confronts him, panics, and kills him mentally without touching him, then hides the body in the fields and continues to Chica. In the city he tests his expanding telepathy, applies for textile work, is exposed by questions about papers and registration number, stuns a suspicious personnel man, flees, and is captured with a neuronic whip. Source anchors: Schwartz; Chica; Natter; Mind Touch; Dr. Shekt; textile work; registration number; neuronic whip.
Analysis: Schwartz's flight to Chica shows that his new powers do not yet give him social knowledge. Natter and the Mind Touch force him to use telepathy defensively, and the killing turns the gift into a moral shock. Textile work matters because Schwartz tries to recover his old tailor identity, but registration number and papers reveal that the future has no place for an undocumented man: without a number he is constitutionally barred from the formal economy of Coercive Labor, and the personnel man's buzzer reads him instantly as a fugitive. The alarm, the recapture, and the neuronic whip end his brief autonomy and deliver him into the same coercive State Capacity that was already tracking Shekt.

Chapter 13 — Spider Web at Washenn

Summary: At the College of Ancients in Washenn, the High Minister learns from Balkis that Schwartz escaped, Natter is dead, the Chica alarm worked, and Schwartz has been recaptured after entering a factory openly. Balkis interprets the contradiction as evidence that Schwartz may have transmitted information or be covering other agents, and he prepares the High Minister for Arvardan's appointment. Arvardan visits the College, thinks about Pola during the meeting, explains Radiation Theory and Merger Theory, asks to enter radioactive Forbidden Areas to find artifacts older than Earth's contamination, and is denied by the High Minister. The High Minister argues that even proof of Earth as humanity's origin might worsen hatred, and Balkis has already shaped the refusal as a defensive move against Imperial espionage. Source anchors: College of Ancients; Washenn; Balkis; Natter; Schwartz; Arvardan; Radiation Theory; Forbidden Areas.
Analysis: Washenn's College of Ancients is the ideological center that makes archaeology dangerous: the Forbidden Areas are sacred, the Book of the Ancients untouchable, and the High Minister's denial is framed as unavoidable Custom, so the permit system runs on Religion and Legitimacy as much as on administration. Balkis uses Natter's death and Schwartz's recapture to fit every anomaly into an Imperial plot, while Arvardan still thinks he is pursuing science; that "spider web" reading shows a State Capacity that interprets everything, making the archaeological request part of a plot Arvardan does not know he is in. Radiation Theory and Forbidden Areas matter because the exact proof Arvardan wants would violate Earth Custom and help the Ancients politically. The High Minister's argument is not stupid; it shows that truth about origins may have destructive social effects when Earth and the Galaxy already despise each other.

Chapter 14 — Second Meeting

Summary: Arvardan visits Shekt at the Institute and receives a cold public dismissal because Shekt is being watched, but Pola secretly slips him a note telling him to ask for the Great Playhouse at eight and avoid followers. At the Shekt house, Pola enters Arvardan's car, directs him through Chica, apologizes for her earlier reaction, and asks his help because Earth is going to revolt and could destroy the Galaxy. She explains that Shekt can give the crucial details, that the house is watched, and that kissing her would be the only plausible cover if they are interrupted; she then invents a noise so Arvardan will kiss her. Arvardan admits his love, Pola warns that she is an Earthgirl, and he answers that he will become an Earthman. Source anchors: Shekt; Institute; Pola; Great Playhouse; Arvardan; Chica; Earth revolt; kiss.
Analysis: The Great Playhouse note turns Pola from rescued technician into active conspirator. Shekt's public rudeness shows how surveillance controls speech, while Pola's private route through Chica creates a narrow covert Information and Coordination channel around the Ancients' watching; the kiss-as-cover works only because watchers can read romance where they cannot read treason. The kiss matters because Asimov makes romantic cover become real commitment, forcing Arvardan to confront the anti-Earth reflexes he had carried from Sirius; his promise to become an Earthman drags Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy across the very boundary his childhood taught him was fixed. Earth revolt is still unbelievable to him, but Pola's trust moves him into the only conversation where Shekt can explain the biological weapon.

Chapter 15 — The Odds That Vanished

Summary: In Shekt's upstairs room, Arvardan hears the full plot: the Synapsifier has been used on Zealot biologists, including the dying F. Smitko, to develop an artificial strain of Common Fever virus designed to kill Galactic Outsiders while leaving Earthmen immune. Shekt explains Earth's high mutation rate, radioactive carbon, sulphur, and phosphorus in the virus, Radiation Fever as a virulent form, the ability of Earth microbes to affect unadapted Imperial soldiers, and the production of tons of crystallized virus plus antitoxin. Arvardan understands that the twenty-five-billion-to-one odds against Earth vanish if refugee traffic spreads disease through hyperspace. Before they can reach Ennius, Balkis and armed Ancients enter the house, reveal that Schwartz has been captured, and strike Arvardan down with a neuronic whip. Source anchors: Shekt; Arvardan; Common Fever; Radiation Fever; F. Smitko; crystallized virus; antitoxin; Balkis.
Analysis: Shekt's explanation turns Earth's stigma into a weapon: Common Fever and Radiation Fever supply the biological basis beneath the Galaxy's superstition about contagion. F. Smitko and the Synapsified biologists redirect legitimate science into bacteriological war, while crystallized virus and antitoxin create Chokepoints and Gateways leverage: a poor planet can threaten quadrillions by controlling the compulsory route back to survival. The virus is itself one product of Technological Change — Synapsified Zealot biologists, an artificial strain, concentrated isotope chemistry — so the machine that was to raise Earth's intelligence becomes the instrument of its blackmail. Balkis's arrival proves that the Ancients' surveillance has outpaced the Assimilationists' appeal to Imperial authority.

Chapter 16 — Choose Your Side!

Summary: Schwartz, Shekt, Pola, and Arvardan are held in the Chica Hall of Correction, where prisoners await punishment for quota evasion, living past the Sixty, subversion, and violations of Custom. Schwartz has learned from guards, jailers, and the Captain's office that he is condemned to death, but he also practices reading minds at greater distance. The four are paralyzed on slabs and begin comparing what they know: Schwartz explains that he came from a past Earth with two billion people and one inhabited planet, and Arvardan tests him with English words such as mother, father, brother, one, two, three, house, man, and wife. Arvardan recognizes the language as equivalent to fifty-thousand-year-old inscriptions from Sirius, Arcturus, Alpha Centauri, and other worlds, proving Schwartz's origin and strengthening the case that Earth is humanity's source. Source anchors: Hall of Correction; Schwartz; Shekt; Pola; Arvardan; English; Sirius inscriptions; Alpha Centauri.
Analysis: The Hall of Correction compresses all of Earth's coercive institutions around the four protagonists. Schwartz's death sentence and mind practice make him dangerous at exactly the moment he has least reason to help. English, Sirius inscriptions, and Alpha Centauri connect his private memory to Arvardan's archaeological evidence, so the time traveler becomes proof of Radiation Theory: the language preserved for fifty thousand years in inscriptions across the Galaxy — while Earth itself forgets it — makes the chapter a meditation on Knowledge Preservation, since what survives of a people is scattered, decipherable by a few, and no longer theirs. The discovery also converts Schwartz into the living evidence for the Ancients' oldest claim, that Earth is the original home of humanity and its appointed center, the mythic Earth as Administrative Core. Pola and Shekt matter because their willingness to betray the Zealots forces Schwartz to decide whether his loyalty belongs to abused Earthmen, the wider Galaxy, or humanity as a whole.

Chapter 17 — Change Your Side!

Summary: Arvardan, Pola, and Shekt argue with Schwartz, who initially refuses to help because he sees Arvardan's buried anti-Earth contempt and feels kinship with the despised planet. Pola invites Schwartz to read her mind, Shekt's mind, and Balkis's mind, while Arvardan admits his Sirian childhood prejudice but asks Schwartz to judge his adult effort to fight it. Schwartz finally accepts that the Galaxy's people are also descendants of Earth and agrees to help. He reaches for Balkis's Mind Touch, learns about guided space bullets, the imminent launch, and a five-pointed building near two rivers, which Shekt identifies as the Temple of Senloo surrounded by radioactive pockets. Balkis enters with a blaster, threatens Shekt and Pola with Synapsifier-induced imbecility and Arvardan and Schwartz with diluted fever virus, and demands to know what the Empire knows. Source anchors: Schwartz; Arvardan; Pola; Balkis; space bullets; Temple of Senloo; two rivers; blaster.
Analysis: Schwartz's refusal is necessary because the book does not let Imperial prejudice vanish just because Arvardan is heroic. Pola, Shekt, and Arvardan each offer their minds as evidence, making telepathy a moral audit as well as a plot device, and Schwartz's reversal shows how Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy decides his side: he is an Earthman, so the Galaxy must win him by proving it is an extended Earth. Space bullets and the Temple of Senloo give the conspiracy a target that can be acted upon, while the two rivers allow Schwartz's twentieth-century memory of St. Louis to matter later; the facts are locked in Balkis's mind, objects of Information and Coordination that only a mind reader can extract in time. Balkis's threats expose his politics as domination, not liberation, pushing Schwartz away from Earth vengeance toward saving humanity.

Chapter 18 — Duel!

Summary: Schwartz duels mentally with Balkis, holding the Secretary in stasis while Arvardan crawls through residual paralysis toward the blaster. Balkis nearly presses the contact, Arvardan topples him, Shekt seizes the weapon, and Schwartz strikes Balkis again when the Secretary tries to dodge. Shekt then theorizes that Schwartz can transmit motor commands as well as receive brain fields, and Schwartz learns to move Balkis's arm, make him stand, take back the blaster, and lead them out as if the Secretary were escorting prisoners. The group passes guards, uses Balkis's green official ground car, reaches Fort Dibburn, and Arvardan identifies himself at the Imperial gate as Bel Arvardan of Baronn, Sirius. Source anchors: Schwartz; Balkis; Arvardan; Shekt; blaster; motor commands; green ground car; Fort Dibburn.
Analysis: The duel makes Schwartz's Mind Touch fully active: he is no longer reading or stunning but controlling Balkis's muscles under pressure. Arvardan's crawl and Shekt's blaster seizure show that physical courage still matters even when telepathy is decisive. The green ground car is important because official symbolism hides the escape better than stealth would. Fort Dibburn should be safety, but the run-up to its gate runs entirely on the Imperial machinery of State Capacity — garrisons, frontiers, and citizen identification — and the need to name Arvardan as a Sirian citizen reminds the reader that the fort is also an Empire and Periphery enclave on Earth, where Imperial protection is filtered through status rather than truth alone.

Chapter 19 — The Deadline That Approached

Summary: Inside Fort Dibburn, the group is fed, bathed, guarded, and effectively imprisoned while Chica stirs outside after the High Minister asks about Balkis, reports trace the Secretary's ground car to the fort, and armed Zealots move toward the Imperial garrison. Lieutenant Claudy, still nursing his broken arm, humiliates Arvardan by making him crave an audience, slapping his injured cheek, insulting Pola as an Earthie-squaw, and refusing immediate access to the colonel. Schwartz wakes and reads that Balkis is speaking with the colonel on another floor, while Shekt and Pola fear Imperial treason. Arvardan confronts the colonel but alienates him by refusing to give all information before seeing Ennius, and the deadline continues to approach. Source anchors: Fort Dibburn; Chica; High Minister; Balkis; Lieutenant Claudy; Earthie-squaw; colonel; Ennius.
Analysis: Fort Dibburn exposes the weakness of relying on Imperial institutions: the protagonists escape Balkis only to face military prejudice, procedure, and personal revenge. Lieutenant Claudy's Earthie-squaw insult and slap make Arvardan pay bodily for loving Pola and for needing help from a man he previously beat, and the fort's State Capacity proves to be a personal-command system that blocks the warning it exists to receive. Evidence moving through hostile rather than neutral intermediaries — Schwartz can locate Balkis talking to the colonel, but Shekt and Pola can only fear treason — shows how fragile Information and Coordination becomes when a warning must pass through faces that despise it. Ennius becomes necessary because lower Imperial officers cannot separate Earth hatred, legal caution, and galactic danger fast enough.

Chapter 20 — The Deadline That Was Reached

Summary: Balkis appears before the colonel and offers calm cooperation, while Arvardan demands a hearing before Ennius and struggles not to lose control. Brother Cori arrives under a flag of truce from the armed Earthmen outside, Balkis tells him to disperse the crowd, and the colonel begins to admire Balkis as a credit to Earth. Arvardan returns to the cell ashamed that he has offended the colonel, while Schwartz reports that Balkis is not afraid and is driven by vanity, hatred, and a desire to triumph even after giving his enemies a chance. Ennius finally arrives with six hours left before the launch, hears Shekt's account of F. Smitko, the Synapsifier, the virus, and Schwartz's telepathy, but lets Balkis have Schwartz removed after Balkis argues that mind control makes the whole accusation suspect. Source anchors: Balkis; Brother Cori; flag of truce; colonel; Schwartz; Ennius; F. Smitko; six o'clock.
Analysis: Balkis's performance before the colonel turns Information and Coordination failure into a galactic threat. Brother Cori and the flag of truce let Balkis appear responsible while keeping the Zealots aligned, and Schwartz's reading explains why Balkis risks the hearing: he wants victory acknowledged. Ennius, F. Smitko, and six o'clock create a procedural nightmare because urgent true evidence can still be redescribed as delirium, paranoia, or Schwartz's manipulation. The hearing is thus a study in Crisis Governance: an executive with lethal lead time still weighs evidence procedurally, because acting on unattested warning feels like the greater risk, and Balkis converts every proof into a story a formal inquiry can credit — a hijacking of Legitimacy by procedural plausibility.

Chapter 21 — The Deadline That Passed

Summary: Arvardan wakes after six o'clock, believing the deadline has passed and the Galaxy is doomed because Ennius would not act on their evidence. Shekt breaks down, Pola waits beside Arvardan, and Ennius arrives guilty but still uncertain until Balkis enters triumphant and reveals that Earthmen are immune, Imperial garrisons already have virus in their air, and the antitoxin gives Earth leverage over the Empire. Ennius apologizes to Arvardan, Shekt, and Pola, refuses compromise with Balkis, and prepares to die with his family at Everest. Schwartz then enters and reveals that there is no virus in flight: after being removed from the hearing, he manipulated Balkis's mind, found Lieutenant Claudy's anti-Earth hatred, used him as a pilot, identified Senloo by its ancient St. Louis location near two rivers, and bombed the five-pointed Temple at three in the morning. Source anchors: Arvardan; six o'clock; Ennius; Balkis; antitoxin; Schwartz; Lieutenant Claudy; Senloo.
Analysis: The passed deadline lets Balkis prove himself with his own mouth, which is the evidence Ennius lacked during the hearing. Ennius's apology matters because his earlier caution nearly sacrificed the Galaxy, but his refusal to bargain shows that Imperial honor still has substance. The whole sequence shows what War, Crisis, and Collapse does to institutions: the Empire's careful procedures almost become its destruction, and only Schwartz's out-of-channel action — a mind-controlled pilot raiding Senloo — breaks a deadlock that no Crisis Governance hearing could resolve. Schwartz's use of Lieutenant Claudy is bitterly precise: Claudy's anti-Earth hatred becomes the motive that destroys the Zealots' weapon. Senloo and St. Louis bind Schwartz's old Earth memory to galactic salvation, making the time displacement essential rather than incidental.

Chapter 22 — The Best Is Yet to Be

Summary: Thirty days later, Schwartz has received the Order of the Spaceship and Sun, First Class, and Earth is quieter except for isolated Zealot bands whose leaders are dead or captured. Ennius proposes moving Earth's population to another planet, but the new plan is for Earthmen to rebuild their own world with huge convoys of normal soil, labor battalions, and the replacement of radioactive areas so the native world of humanity can become green again. Schwartz walks to Shekt's house before leaving with Arvardan for the central worlds, thinking of his dead twentieth-century Earth and the millions of planets beyond. Arvardan asks Pola to come with him as his wife, promises to return with her to Earth, claims honorary naturalization as an Earthman, and imagines forty years of labor replacing diseased soil. Source anchors: thirty days; Order of the Spaceship and Sun; Ennius; normal soil; labor battalions; Schwartz; Arvardan; Pola.
Analysis: The Order of the Spaceship and Sun gives Schwartz Imperial recognition, but the stronger ending is Earth's refusal of charity through mass relocation. Ennius, normal-soil convoys, and labor battalions turn Collapse and Resilience into reconstruction, giving Earthmen agency after a story dominated by quarantine and the Sixty. The labor battalions also redefine Coercive Labor: what was once forced quota work under the Ancients becomes chosen work of repair, the first legitimate economy shaped by Earthmen themselves inside the Galactic Empire. Arvardan and Pola's marriage makes honorary Earth citizenship a concrete commitment, while Schwartz accepts that he cannot recover Chicago but can help make future Earth livable.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • "Between one footstep and the next": the exact mechanism of Schwartz's displacement; June 1949 Chicago becomes an autumn Earth in a single step over a Raggedy Ann doll.
  • The year is 827 G.E. — 827 years since the coronation of Frankenn the First — with "an average of fifty new planets each day" achieving provincial status.
  • The Sixty: Earth supports twenty million people; a Census every ten years catches evaders; exemptions run through the High Minister and number "maybe a dozen a year"; the bone-structure check makes age unmaskable.
  • The Synapsifier: hypno-pills, a multihundred-kilowatt diamagnetic field, the Ullster skull technique, platinum hair-lets, and a brain whose possibilities number "factorial ten to the twentieth power."
  • Schwartz's anatomical proof of antiquity: winding primitive skull fissures, facial hair (it never grows after the treatment), thirty-two teeth with a homemade bridge, and an open vermiform appendix three and a half inches long.
  • Common Fever (mild, four to six days, confers immunity) versus Radiation Fever (symptoms within two hours, death in days); the virus is a nucleoprotein whose carbon, sulphur, and phosphorus are fifty per cent radioactive.
  • The twenty-five-billion-to-one odds: one planet of twenty million against two hundred million worlds of roughly five hundred quadrillion people.
  • The Temple of Senloo: a five-pointed building on an oasis of normal soil amid radioactive pockets, near the junction of two great rivers — Joseph Schwartz's St. Louis. Destroyed at three in the morning, creating "a hole, a hundred feet deep."
  • Balkis's threats of choice: the Synapsifier reduced to "an acerebral imbecile," and diluted fever virus for a week of dying.
  • Anti-Earth vocabulary to keep straight: "Earthie-squaw," "Earthie-buck," "Outsider," "Ancients"/"Brotherhood," "Forbidden Areas," "Custom," "Assimilationist," "Zealot."
  • The green light-sweeping official ground car of the High Minister's Secretary that carries the prisoners out of the Hall of Correction.
  • Imperial markers: the "Spaceship and Sun" insignia (absent from Washenn's Council Chamber thanks to Stannell II's riots), and the Order of the Spaceship and Sun, First Class awarded to only two others nonposthumously.
  • Bookends: the novel opens and closes with Browning's "Grow old along with me! / The best is yet to be," first as a Chicago old-age prayer, last as Schwartz's private poem for a new Earth and a Galaxy of stars.
