Detailed overview

Keep the Aspidistra Flying follows Gordon Comstock's attempt to resign from the money-world and shows, with almost punitive precision, how that attempted resignation depends on the very social ties he says he despises. Gordon has left the New Albion Publicity Company for Mr McKechnie's bookshop, lives on two pounds a week in Mrs Wisbeach's lodging house, works at an unfinished poem called London Pleasures, and treats the aspidistra as the emblem of lower-middle-class respectability. The adapted Corinthians epigraph announces the joke and the wound at once: money has replaced charity as the condition under which love, art, cleanliness, and ordinary dignity can function.

The novel's strongest device is repeated humiliation. Gordon counts fivepence halfpenny and a threepenny-bit, worries about tobacco, hides tea-making equipment from Mrs Wisbeach, borrows from Julia, refuses Ravelston's help, wounds Rosemary, and cannot enter a pub, restaurant, or railway carriage without converting the price of things into proof of his own degradation. Orwell makes money physical: missing shirt buttons, a pawned overcoat, cold water shaves, an oil-lamp nearly empty, bread-and-marg., dirty socks, and the small rituals by which people with little cash preserve appearances.

Advertising is the novel's other machine. The hoardings for Bovex, QT Sauce, Tru-weet Breakfast Crisps, Kangaroo Burgundy, Vitamalt Chocolate, and later Queen of Sheba's "PP" campaign are not background jokes; they are the language of the world Gordon both hates and is skilled enough to serve. His disgust at slogans sits beside his real gift for compressed verbal manipulation, which is why the return to the New Albion at the end is neither arbitrary nor merely comic. Gordon has more talent for frightening consumers than for completing London Pleasures.

Rosemary Waterlow, Philip Ravelston, and Julia Comstock give Gordon's rebellion its moral test. Rosemary loves him but cannot make a shared life out of his refusal to earn; Ravelston understands capitalism as a Socialist editor but is cushioned by inherited money and Hermione's restaurant life; Julia, working long hours and saving for small presents, grasps the problem more bluntly than either of them. Gordon's contempt for money repeatedly becomes dependence on people poorer or kinder than himself.

The book turns when a fifty-dollar cheque from the Californian Review briefly lets Gordon feel rich. Instead of freeing him, the money produces a grotesque spree at Modigliani's, Dora's room, a police cell, a five-pound fine, and the loss of his job. That fall is followed by his deliberate move downward to Mr Cheeseman's twopenny library near Lambeth Cut and Mother Meakin's filthy attic, where he tries to escape ambition, poetry, friendship, sexuality, and cleanliness by sinking beneath respectability altogether.

The final chapters do not simply capitulate to convention. Rosemary's pregnancy forces Gordon to see an actual future rather than a private pose: the six-week and nine-week foetus pictures in the public library make the baby real to him, while the women's-paper advertisements remind him that the New Albion world is still vulgar and predatory. His acceptance of the job is shameful, relieving, and partly honest. The ending with the registry office, rolled-gold ring, unfurnished flat, never-never furniture, and deliberate purchase of an aspidistra makes the plant a compromise between defeat and life.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter I

Summary: Chapter I places Gordon Comstock in Mr McKechnie's bookshop, counting fivepence halfpenny, resenting a threepenny-bit, and moving between seven thousand dusty volumes, a twopenny no-deposit lending library, and shelves of Arlen, Burroughs, Deeping, Dell, Galsworthy, Priestley, Sapper, and Walpole. He watches advertising hoardings for QT Sauce, Tru-weet Breakfast Crisps, Kangaroo Burgundy, Vitamalt Chocolate, Bovex, and Roland Butta, fields customers including Mrs Weaver and Mrs Penn, remembers Mice selling 153 copies, and thinks of Ravelston, Rosemary, the Primrose Quarterly, and London Pleasures. Source anchors: McKechnie; fivepence halfpenny; Deeping; Bovex; Roland Butta; Mice; London Pleasures.

Analysis: McKechnie and fivepence halfpenny make Gordon's anti-money creed begin as bodily irritation rather than political clarity. Deeping, Bovex, and Roland Butta bind his literary contempt to advertising's Language and Ideology: slogans command the street while the market decides which books remain visible. Mice and London Pleasures expose the contradiction in his artistic identity, because Gordon denounces commercial language yet measures himself by sales and cannot write while counting coins.

II

Summary: Chapter II sends Gordon home through Willowbed Road to Mrs Wisbeach's lodging house, where his pawned overcoat, the japanned hall tray, Flaxman from the Queen of Sheba Toilet Requisites Co., Lorenheim the vacuum-cleaner tout, and the cold top-floor room define his daily enclosure. His green-potted aspidistra survives his attempts to kill it, the dining room is crowded with aspidistras, his supper is cold beef, white bread, mousetrap cheese, Pan Yan pickle, and musty water, and his illicit tea-making precedes another failed attempt to work on London Pleasures before no letter comes from Rosemary or the Primrose Quarterly. Source anchors: Willowbed Road; Mrs Wisbeach; Flaxman; Queen of Sheba; aspidistra; Pan Yan pickle; Primrose Quarterly.

Analysis: Willowbed Road and Mrs Wisbeach make respectability a system of Legibility: rent, tea leaves, visitors, clothing, and room condition let the lodging house read Gordon's poverty and police how openly he displays it. Flaxman and Queen of Sheba introduce the commercial vulgarity Gordon despises yet later serves, while the aspidistra and Pan Yan pickle make lower-middle-class endurance comic and suffocating. The Primrose Quarterly reduces artistic hope to waiting for post in a room where concealment governs daily life.

III

Summary: Chapter III reconstructs the Comstock family as a dwindling middle-middle-class line, moving from Gran'pa Comstock and the family shop through Gordon's schooling, his father's decline, his mother's music lessons, Julia's sacrifices, Aunt Angela at Briarbrae, Uncle Walter's agencies and diseases, and Aunt Charlotte's Mental Home. Gordon remembers the New Albion Publicity Company, The Ragged Trousered Philanthropists, the aspidistra as England's flower, and the decision to leave a good job for poverty and writing, only to depend on Julia and Aunt Angela before finding McKechnie's shop. Source anchors: Comstock family; Julia; Aunt Angela; Uncle Walter; New Albion; Ragged Trousered Philanthropists; Briarbrae.

Analysis: The Comstock family and Julia show that Gordon's rebellion is financed by people already exhausted by decline. Aunt Angela, Uncle Walter, and Briarbrae make the aspidistra a family emblem before it becomes Gordon's private enemy: it stands for survival without renewal. New Albion and Ragged Trousered Philanthropists explain his doctrine, but the family's Moral Economy undercuts it, because Julia and other women convert duty, savings, and forgone choices into support for a man who calls his dependence freedom.

IV

Summary: Chapter IV follows Gordon on his Thursday afternoon off as he walks up Malkin Hill toward Paul Doring's literary tea-party, rehearsing lines about menacing wind, bending poplars, clerks, rent, rates, school-bills, Drage's furniture, and the fear of losing jobs. He has prepared one cigarette in an empty Gold Flake packet, darned his socks with Lorenheim's needle, and imagined Coleridge Grove's literary company, only to find the Dorings' house empty, no cars outside, a servant-girl watching, and later a letter claiming the party was on Saturday; enraged, he posts an obscene reply to Doring and loses another possible connection. Source anchors: Malkin Hill; Paul Doring; Coleridge Grove; Gold Flake; Drage's; servant-girl; Doring.

Analysis: Malkin Hill and Coleridge Grove show Gordon transforming every social uncertainty into proof that poverty makes him contemptible. Gold Flake and Drage's connect tiny performance rituals to his poem's attack on clerical respectability, while Doring and the servant-girl expose his most destructive habit: he would rather believe in deliberate insult than admit confusion. This chapter makes his loneliness partly self-inflicted without denying that money governs the etiquette around invitations, clothes, cigarettes, and welcome.

V

Summary: Chapter V stages Gordon's friendship with Philip Ravelston, editor of Antichrist, after Ravelston accepts Gordon's poem about a dying prostitute. Gordon refuses the comfort of Ravelston's Regent's Park flat, meets him from the pavement, talks about Mice, London Pleasures, Socialism, Marx, pubs, Roland Butta, and the deathliness of modern life, and then insists on paying the first round in a public bar where Ravelston is visibly a gentleman. The night ends with Ravelston going to Hermione and Modigliani's while Gordon returns to Willowbed Road, receives Doring's letter, and answers it with self-destructive abuse. Source anchors: Ravelston; Antichrist; Regent's Park; Marx; Roland Butta; Hermione; Modigliani's.

Analysis: Ravelston and Antichrist give Gordon his most sympathetic listener, but Regent's Park and Modigliani's expose the gulf between theoretical Socialism and inherited ease. Marx and Roland Butta let Gordon describe capitalism with real force, yet Hermione shows how quickly Ravelston can return to comfort after the pub. Their friendship reveals Commercial Society operating inside intimacy: prices, inherited income, restaurant choices, and offers of help continually rank the two men even when both want conversation to escape money.

VI

Summary: Chapter VI turns from money to sex by following Gordon through Luton Road market, where girls, cheap underwear, codfish, broccoli, rabbits, meat, eels, oranges, and electric bulbs intensify his loneliness before Rosemary appears on her way to Willowbed Road. Their quarrel over his "broken my heart" letter becomes reconciliation, and they discuss marriage, money, contraception, Mrs Wisbeach's rule against young women, Rosemary's hostel, Gordon's missing buttons, and the possibility of a Sunday trip where she might become his mistress. Source anchors: Luton Road; Rosemary; broken heart letter; Mrs Wisbeach; missing buttons; contraception; Sunday trip.

Analysis: Luton Road and Rosemary make desire social rather than private: Gordon wants a body, but he also wants to stop being invisible. Mrs Wisbeach and missing buttons show why sexuality is tied to lodging-house rules and male self-respect, while contraception and Sunday trip bring practical planning into the romance. Rosemary's generosity repeatedly meets Gordon's theory that no woman can love a man without money, and each contact between them both challenges and strengthens that theory.

VII

Summary: Chapter VII recounts the Sunday outing funded by Gordon's nine shillings and five borrowed shillings from Julia, beginning at Paddington Station and moving by train to Slough, bus to Farnham Common, and walking through Burnham Beeches. Gordon and Rosemary enjoy mist, rabbits, beech leaves, jay feathers, country roads, and private happiness before hunger drives them to Crickham-on-Thames and the Ravenscroft Hotel, where a costly lunch, upper-middle-class eyes, and a waiter destroy Gordon's budget. Their attempted lovemaking in the woods fails, Rosemary offers to risk pregnancy, Gordon confesses he has only eightpence, and she quietly pays fares and buys him Gold Flake. Source anchors: Julia; Paddington Station; Burnham Beeches; Crickham-on-Thames; Ravenscroft Hotel; eightpence; Gold Flake.

Analysis: Julia and Paddington Station show that even Gordon's day of freedom begins with debt and accounting. Burnham Beeches briefly offers a world outside hoardings and lodging houses, but Ravenscroft Hotel and eightpence return every feeling to price. Gold Flake is Rosemary's most tender act in the chapter because she protects Gordon's public pride while knowing exactly how absurd it is, and that tenderness makes his obsession with masculine payment look both painful and cruel.

VIII

Summary: Chapter VIII begins with a fifty-dollar cheque from the Californian Review, which Gordon cashes at the Westminster Bank while promising himself that five pounds will go to Julia. The money immediately becomes visions of claret, beer, a suit, Rosemary, Brighton, and literary success, so he summons Ravelston and Rosemary to Modigliani's, spends recklessly, drinks too much, moves through Soho and Dora's room, and ends the night singing toward sobriety. Julia's fiver is carried as a supposed sacred reserve, but the chapter shows Gordon's sudden money turning into self-display, lust, restaurant performance, and loss of control. Source anchors: Californian Review; fifty dollars; Westminster Bank; Julia's fiver; Modigliani's; Rosemary; Dora.

Analysis: Californian Review and fifty dollars give Gordon what he claims would restore art and dignity, but Westminster Bank only starts a faster collapse. Julia's fiver is the moral marker: he knows what repayment would mean and still lets Modigliani's and Dora consume the windfall. Rosemary matters here because she is present for the spree but cannot stop Gordon from turning good luck into theatrical ruin, proving that money without discipline does not free him from the money-god.

IX

Summary: Chapter IX wakes Gordon in a white-tiled police cell after the drunken night, with a kind constable, a snarling sergeant he struck, a Black Maria, a magistrate named Mr Croom, and the sentence of five pounds or fourteen days. Flaxman and Ravelston visit; Ravelston pays the fine, gives Gordon a bath, clean clothes, pyjamas, socks, underclothes, and a bed in Regent's Park, while Gordon remembers Dora, Julia's unpaid money, and the humiliation of having called himself a poet in court. The chapter ends by making his job loss inevitable: Mrs Wisbeach knows, McKechnie is vulnerable to gossip, and Gordon takes another lower-paid step toward Lambeth. Source anchors: police cell; constable; Black Maria; Mr Croom; Flaxman; Ravelston; Dora.

Analysis: The police cell and Black Maria turn Gordon's abstract wish to sink into an actual civic process of Legibility: charge sheet, magistrate, fine, newspaper item, and public record translate his private drama into the state's standard category of drunken disorder. Ravelston and Flaxman rescue him in different idioms, while Dora shows that Gordon cannot even spend dissipation gracefully. Mr Croom strips the rebellion of grandeur because the court reads "poet" not as vocation but as absurd evidence attached to another drunk.

X

Summary: Chapter X follows Gordon after the police-cell disgrace into Lambeth, where he takes a thirty-shilling job under Mr Cheeseman in a twopenny library and rents an eight-shilling bed-sitting-room from Mother Meakin in Brewer's Yard. He imagines the underground world of tramps, beggars, criminals, prostitutes, tea-drunkards, and slum lodgers as a place below ambition, while Cheeseman treats books as merchandise, suspects till-money theft, and occasionally offers Smith's Potato Crisps. Ravelston visits the filthy attic, sees the haddock smell, ragged boys, dirty crocks, dead aspidistra, and buggy ceiling, while Rosemary and Julia press Gordon to return to Mr Erskine and the New Albion. Source anchors: Lambeth; Mr Cheeseman; Mother Meakin; Brewer's Yard; Ravelston; Mr Erskine; New Albion.

Analysis: Lambeth and Brewer's Yard show Gordon trying to make failure into a chosen destination rather than an accident. Mr Cheeseman turns literature into pure resale value, which humiliates Gordon more thoroughly than McKechnie's shop because there is no cultivated cover left. Ravelston, Mr Erskine, and New Albion keep pulling him upward, so the chapter tests whether his war on money can survive contact with people who refuse to let him disappear.

XI

Summary: Chapter XI opens with spring mocked by Lambeth pavement rubbish, cabbage leaves, cherry stones, orange peel, and the aspidistra's new green shoots, while Gordon has spent three months in Mr Cheeseman's library reading Tit Bits, Answers, Peg's Paper, The Gem, The Magnet, Home Notes, and The Girl's Own Paper. Rosemary arrives looking ill and tells him she is pregnant; customers interrupt with murder books and sex books, and the conversation turns to marriage, a five-pound abortion, the New Albion, Mr Erskine, and the need to decide whether he will return to paid respectability. In the public library he studies six-week and nine-week foetus images, then reads women's-paper advertisements for face-creams, halitosis, Guinness, Night Starvation, Breakfast Crisps, and Vitamalt before telephoning Rosemary to say he will take the job. Source anchors: Lambeth; Tit Bits; Rosemary; five-pound abortion; New Albion; foetus; Night Starvation.

Analysis: Lambeth and Tit Bits show how far Gordon has let his mind flatten before Rosemary arrives with a future he cannot treat as pose. The five-pound abortion and foetus create a Moral Economy that his anti-money creed cannot dissolve: Rosemary's pregnancy establishes claims of care, protection, and shared consequence that outweigh his cultivated failure. New Albion and Night Starvation make the surrender exact, because Gordon recognizes the advertising world as obscene yet also recognizes that Rosemary and the child cannot live on his refusal to provide.

XII

Summary: Chapter XII closes with Gordon and Rosemary's registry-office marriage, Ravelston as witness, a toothless professional witness, lunch in a Soho restaurant, and Gordon's return to the New Albion at four pounds ten a week under Mr Warner. Gordon now writes copy for Queen of Sheba's April Dew campaign, especially the "PP" slogan for Pedic Perspiration, while he and Rosemary furnish a small flat off the Edgware Road with never-never furniture, Julia's occasional table, Ravelston's crockery, a rolled-gold wedding ring, a kitchenette, and a view toward Paddington Station. Their first married quarrel concerns whether to buy an aspidistra for the front window, and Gordon insists on the plant just before Rosemary feels the baby move. Source anchors: registry office; Ravelston; Mr Warner; PP; Edgware Road; rolled-gold ring; aspidistra.

Analysis: The registry office and rolled-gold ring make the ending deliberately modest rather than romantic: marriage requires paperwork, lunch, borrowed witnesses, wages, and payment plans. Mr Warner and PP show Gordon's talent surviving inside the trade he despises, while Edgware Road and the aspidistra reveal Informal Institutions rebuilding his life around household expectations of provision, respectability, and continuity. The plant still marks conformity, but Rosemary and the baby convert that conformity into the ordinary decency Gordon had tried to escape.
