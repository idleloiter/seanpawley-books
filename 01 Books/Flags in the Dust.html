Flags in the Dust

Buy on Amazon — Flags in the Dust

Position in the vault

''Flags in the Dust'' is, on the extract's own evidence, Faulkner's unshorn Ur-version of the novel later cut to about 110,000 words and published in 1929 as ''Sartoris''. The title-page apparatus of this extract lists it explicitly as ''Sartoris (1929) [Flags in the Dust (1973)]'', and the Library of Congress entry calls the whole ''An uncut version of the work published in 1929 under title: Sartoris''. It is Faulkner's first Yoknapatawpha novel — the Editors' Note printed after the text says it was "the first of his novels to be set in what he later called 'my own little postage stamp of native soil'" — and the Saratory family legend it fixes here (Colonel John Sartoris, Redlaw, the derringer, the railroad, the doomed bayard twins) is the seedbed the rest of the corpus replays. Per 06 Plans/book-affinity.md its kindred siblings are Sanctuary, The Unvanquished, Absalom, Absalom!, and The Reivers, all marked [high]. In the vault graph the book sits inside the Faulkner/Sartor corpus cluster, advances the Identity, Memory, and Cultural Legitimacy and Testimony, War Memory, and Broken Official Narratives paths, and feeds the Identity, Culture, and Social Formation and War, Revolution, and Civilizational Breakdown maps. It is the source-twin of ''Sanctuary'' (both share the Horace Benbow–Belle–Little Belle triangle) and the reverse-echo of ''The Unvanquished'' (the same Colonel John Sartoris whose murder ''The Unvanquished'' retells from Bayard's side).

Detailed overview

Flags in the Dust is organized around a Mississippi family living under the weight of its own heroic dead. The opening scene makes the method clear: old man Falls brings Colonel John Sartoris into old Bayard's bank office as if memory were a physical visitor, and the dead colonel's pipe, teeth marks, military aura, and legend dominate the room more completely than the living banker or the pauper who has walked from the county Poor Farm. The novel's family history is not background; it is an active pressure on money, manners, horses, servants, courtship, grief, and recklessness.

The Sartoris household is built out of old Bayard, Miss Jenny, Simon, Isom, returning soldiers, horses, gardens, verandas, and rituals of deference that survive into an industrializing postwar world. Simon's carriage, top hat, linen duster, matched geldings, and refusal to use the hitching post keep the family theatrically linked to an older order, while Miss Jenny's fights over salvia beds, hoes, Isom's borrowed uniform, and Bayard's wartime damage puncture that order with comedy. Faulkner's social world is precise: Black servants, poor whites, bankers, merchants, Benbows, returning veterans, and townspeople all know the gestures by which rank is performed.

The novel's World War I aftermath enters through Bayard Sartoris and Horace Benbow in different registers. Bayard's war has intensified the Sartoris appetite for speed, risk, and death, while Horace arrives by train in badly fitting khaki, guarding a fragile glass object from Venice and babbling about art, his sister Narcissa, and the difficulty of re-entering Jefferson. Their returns are not heroic completions. They expose a generation unable to fit either the old county codes or the new postwar arrangements.

Faulkner works by counterpointing comedy and doom. Simon's schemes, the Second Baptist church money deputation, Brother Moore's greasy notebook, Uncle Bird, Miss Jenny's sharp tongue, and Horace's ornate letters sit beside grief for dead Sartorises, old Bayard's deaf isolation, Narcissa's emotional guardedness, and young Bayard's destructive momentum. The narrative repeatedly turns household management and local farce into signs of a larger family disorder.

The five-chapter structure moves from ancestral presence to domestic comedy, returned-veteran estrangement, public/community entanglement, and Horace's frustrated interior life. Chapter One reaches from old man Falls and the dead colonel's pipe through the carriage ride, Aunt Jenny's Civil War stories, old man Falls's yarns about the flight from the Yankees and the derringer, and young Bayard's night-time return with the story of John's death over France. Chapter Two plays marriage plot and community farce across the Sartoris veranda and the Beaumont/Caspey world and culminates in Bayard's stallion ride and arrest. Chapter Three is the longest: Horace's re-entry, his affair with Belle, young Bayard's rib-crushing car smash and convalescence, old man Falls treating the wen, and Byron Snopes's anonymous letters and theft. Chapter Four moves from the October church-deputation comedy to the sorghum-mill and 'possum-hunting idyll, the Thanksgiving dinner, old Bayard's death in the overturned car, Bayard's refuge with the MacCallums, and his Christmas ride away from home. Chapter Five follows Horace's letters from his rented house with Belle and Little Belle, Bayard's wanderings and death testing an inventor's spring-winged aeroplane at Dayton, and — the same June day Narcissa's child is born — Simon's murder and Miss Jenny's discovery at the cemetery, ending on the naming of the child "Benbow Sartoris." The extract ends with the 2006 Noel Polk textual note, Joseph Blotner's annotations, and a short Faulkner biography, all of which vouch for the uncut/Sartoris relationship.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Colonel John Sartoris (dead): the shaper. The pipe, the derringer, the railroad, the two carpetbaggers he killed, and the phrase "Redlaw'll kill me tomorrow, for I shall be unarmed" define the Sartoris ethos his descendants spend the book re-enacting or escaping. He is "evoked like a genie or a deity by an illiterate old man's tedious reminiscing."
  • Old Bayard Sartoris: the deaf, bank-president grandfather who carries town authority and family guilt. He keeps the hitching post against industrial progress, siphons bourbon into a decanter under his dogs' gaze, meditates on the family bible's dissolving column of names, lets old man Falls treat his wen (which a Memphis specialist plucks off whole), and dies in the car beside his grandson on the frosty road.
  • Miss Jenny Du Pre (Virginia Du Pre, "Aunt Jenny"): the Carolina-born widow transplanted with the colored glass windows, keeper of the family legend (she tells the Jeb Stuart/anchovies story, "I danced a valse with him in Baltimore in '58"). She governs the Sartoris household by insult and discipline — carrot-and-stick mothering of Simon, Isom, and both Bayards — and survives nearly everyone.
  • Young Bayard Sartoris: the returned RAF pilot, twin's survivor. His war (John shot down, Ploeckner) drives him into speed: the gray car, the water-tank dive, the stallion-ride, the serenade, the rib-crushing crash, the marriage to Narcissa, and finally the fatal test-flight at Dayton. He inherits the phrase old Bayard applies to himself — "keeping me for a reliable witness to the extinction of it."
  • John Sartoris, Jr. ("Johnny"): the warm, merry dead twin and Bayard's obsession. Officer killed July 5, 1918, flying a Sopwith Camel ("Pupil of Richthofen's" Ploeckner got him); he haunts every scene as the gauge by which Bayard judges himself.
  • Narcissa Benbow (later Sartoris): Horace's sister and Bayard's wife. Serene, grave, 26, she is the "sanctuary" figure — her calm reads as provincial dullness to Caroline, as something to violate to Byron Snopes, and as love and dread to Bayard. She reads to him through his convalescence and ends by renaming their child "Benbow Sartoris" against the family curse.
  • Horace Benbow: the lawyer-aesthete, glass-blower, "Thou still unravished bride of quietude" reciter. He returns to a hush (waiting for Belle, stringing everything through words), conducts the Belle affair, marries her, and moves to a crude new lumber town where he lives with Belle and Little Belle beyond hope of the "meaning of peace."
  • Belle Mitchell: Harry's wife, "cannily stupid," the hot-house hostess whose tea ritual, music-room kisses, and "I want to have your child" pull Horace away from Narcissa; she goes to Reno and remarries him.
  • Harry Mitchell: the cotton speculator, "ugly as sin and kindhearted," too decent to keep Belle; reduced to "movie subtitles" and a final portrait of a stricken ox.
  • Simon (Deacon Strother): the Sartoris carriage-driver, butler, and church deacon, the family's satiric chorus. He "puts out" the Second Baptist building fund, is bailed out by old Bayard's check, and is finally found with his head crushed in Meloney Harris's cabin — Miss Jenny's "last one."
  • Isom and Elnora: Simon's grandson and daughter; Isom in borrowed khaki, Elnora's singing and kitchen labor keep the household's Black domestic economy visible.
  • Caspey: Simon's war-returned son, insolent about "cullud soldiers" and demanding rights, knocked through the screen door by old Bayard with a stick of stove wood — the novel's sharpest point on race and the postwar labor order.
  • Dr Lucius Quintus Peabody: 87-year-old, 310-pound, Yocona County institution and John Sartoris's regimental surgeon; he saves old Bayard from the knife and lectures him about the automobile.
  • Dr Alford: the cold, ambitious newcomer who diagnoses cancer and wants to cut; his fame-wen disappears only when the Memphis specialist touches it.
  • old man Falls: the poor-farm veteran who returns the colonel's pipe and treats the wen with Choctaw salve; his war memories and "walk up and spit in deestruction's face" speeches are the book's oldest surviving memory-bearer.
  • Byron Snopes: the bank's bookkeeper, the anonymous letter-writer who dictates the secret letters through the boy Virgil Beard, who rises at the Benbows' garage roof, steals Narcissa's packet of letters, flees with stolen banknotes, and slinks to Frenchman's Bend — the first Snopes as villain.
  • The MacCallums (Virginius the father, Rafe, Henry, Lee, Jackson, Buddy): the hill-country hunting family that shelters Bayard; Jackson's fox-hound experiment and Buddy's war "charm" provide the novel's most whole alternative to Sartoris doom.
  • Aunt Sally Wyatt, Mrs Marders, Frankie, little Belle, Joan (Belle's sister), V.K. Suratt: supporting registers of gossip, courtship, and male folly.

Themes and concepts to track

  • The living dead: how a family legend, a pipe, a derringer, and a cussed sentence ("Redlaw'll kill me tomorrow") run the household better than any living man.
  • War memory and failed reintegration: Bayard's return outruns itself; Horace's return is verbal; Post-1918 Jefferson cannot hold either.
  • Ceremony as ideology: the carriage, top hat, wedding, church deputation, Thanksgiving, and inheritance are all ways of performing rank under economic change.
  • Race and labor after emancipation: Simon, Caspey, Elnora, Isom, and the "putting-out" of church money show dependency, insolence, and patronage inside one household.
  • Words as evasion: Horace's letters, Byron Snopes's anonymous letters, and Miss Jenny's telegrams all shape reality to the speaker.
  • Doom as path dependence: each generation replays "the fatal semblance of his dream" until the name is changed — "He isn't John. He's Benbow Sartoris."

Core concepts

  • Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation: Colonel Sartoris's pipe and family legend let the heroic dead govern the living household; old man Falls's recollections, the cedar chest, and the family bible's fading column of names are the archive the story works from.
  • Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy: carriage rituals, military costumes, domestic manners, and public deference keep the Sartoris rank visible in postwar Jefferson; the new lumber town's $60,000 courthouse is its brittle fake.
  • Informal Institutions: the veranda dispute over Second Baptist church money reveals obligation moving through household authority, church claims, and improvised accounting; the bank, the Beard hotel, and the deputation are informal power centers.
  • Coercive Labor: Simon's threatened absence exposes how Sartoris dignity depends on Black domestic labor that the household treats as both subordinate and indispensable; Caspey's wartime "freedom talk" is the counter-order.
  • Language and Ideology: Horace edits letters and aesthetic references into a version of himself that can evade the facts of his divided loyalties; Byron Snopes composes anonymous love letters by rote and guilt.
  • War, Crisis, and Collapse: the novel is the classic postwar-collapse text — veterans, displaced households, inherited injury, and the family's own civil war carried forward by sons.
  • Path Dependence and Institutional Drift: the Sartoris doom is institutionally inherited — each Bayard re-enacts the founder's recklessness ("I am the first of my name to see sixty years"); the hitching-post versus the automobile is drift in miniature.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: the whole final pages turn on naming the heir — "We'll name him John" versus "He isn't John. He's Benbow Sartoris" — the fight over who (and what) succeeds the dead line.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter One

Summary: The chapter opens with old man Falls walking three miles in from the county Poor Farm carrying Colonel John Sartoris's pipe, returning it because "a po' house aint no fitten place for anything of his'n." The dead colonel, "far more palpable than either of the two old men," dominates the bank office; the pipe carries his teeth marks. After Falls goes, the green-eye-shaded bookkeeper announces Simon's arrival, and old Bayard drives out past the salvia bed where a Yankee patrol once halted, through Simon's news that young Bayard jumped off the wrong side of the two o'clock train and "lit out th'ough de woods," not wearing soldier clothes. At home, Elnora cannot tell the truth about anything; old Bayard retires. The chapter then turns to Aunt Jenny (Virginia Du Pre), who came from Carolina in '69 with the colored glass windows and tells the Christmas-story of her brother, the Carolina Bayard, Jeb Stuart's A.D.C., who died before second Manassas riding back "after those anchovies" and was shot in the back by a cook with a derringer — "Mister Stuart... said he was a good officer and a fine cavalryman, but that he was too reckless." old man Falls tells his own war yarns: the colonel in sock feet on the rail, the Yankee patrol, the flight by the hog-lot to the horse hidden in the willows, and the two carpetbaggers killed with "that same dang der'nger," leading to the prophecy "Redlaw'll kill me tomorrow... I'm tired of killing men," and John Sartoris's death. Old Bayard rides out with a ticked setter; Elnora sings. In Part Two, the scene shifts to Belle's card party, where Simon holds court in the kitchen over ice cream, and Miss Jenny and Narcissa Benbow discuss Horace (a wire "over fifty words long" about something he is bringing home). Simon tells Miss Jenny of Bayard's return and is cursed for it. Miss Jenny finds old Bayard siphoning bourbon in his office; the night brings the nine-thirty train, and young Bayard comes out of the lilac bushes to the veranda, telling of the war, of John: "I tried to keep him from going up there on that goddam little popgun," of Ploeckner — "Pupil of Richthofen's" — who "hemmed him up like a damn calf in a pen," and of John taking fire and jumping. Miss Jenny feeds him milk instead of whisky; asleep, he wakes groaning, and she sits by his bed.
Analysis: The pipe, the colonel, and falls make Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation tactile — the father's teeth marks turn legend into an heirloom relic, and old man Falls's compulsion to tell and re-tell ("Do I have to tell you that every time you tell me this damn story?") keeps the dead alive. The war-return of Bayard carries War, Crisis, and Collapse into the family — John's death-before-return is the whole engine of the doom. Aunt Jenny's Jeb Stuart tale and the "banners in the dust" phrase ("her voice was proud and still as banners in the dust") set the Sartoris legend beside Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy: the slogan of rank is a story told at the right fireplace. "Redlaw'll kill me tomorrow" plus the derringer directly primes The Unvanquished, which retells exactly this Colonel and his killing from the inheriting Bayard's side, and the "fatal semblance of his dream" casting of the dynasty is the same family-curse machinery Absalom, Absalom! blows up to epic scale. Simon's theatre with the matched geldings and the carriage-rouge prefigures the book's whole ceremonial apparatus of class.

Chapter Two

Summary: Opening on the morning veranda and garden, Miss Jenny quarrels with Simon over the salvia bed while Miss Benbow calls; Isom appears in Caspey's borrowed khaki — divisional emblem, tarnished service stripe, putties, overseas cap. Miss Jenny's rants cover war-suffering, her judgment of men, and the story of young Bayard's marriage to the "thin as a rail" Caroline and the Memphis wedding and delicatessen dinner; then the apparition of the girl "beyond the veil." In the parlour of shrouded furniture she and Narcissa use the piano; in the kitchen Caspey tells his tall war tales (the steamboat cellar, the German swimmers, the Porto Rican medal) and advances his post-war claim: "War unloosed de black man's mouf... I dont take nothin' fum no white folks no mo'." Miss Jenny notes Caspey's insolence and invokes Senator Vardaman. Narcissa shows Miss Jenny an anonymous letter — "You did not answere mine of 25th... Hal Wagner"— which she insists on tearing up. At the Benbow house Aunt Sally Wyatt comments on the Sartoris boys (water tank, balloon ascension — John's, she will not believe it) and warns Narcissa away from Bayard: "He'll be killing you same as he did that poor little wife of his." Narcissa's Red Cross/S.O.S. charity, John's death, and Caroline's death in childbirth in October are recalled; Bayard comes into the parlor and says "Everybody ought to get married once, like everybody ought to go to one war." Part Two: young Bayard brings home the low gray car — four-cylinder, sixteen valves, eight spark-plugs, guaranteed to do eighty — and no sooner drives Miss Jenny and Isom to town than old man Falls reports the Poor House hill at "forty miles an hour," which Miss Jenny corrects to fifty-four via the speedometer; the wen on old Bayard's cheek is inspected ("Hard's a rock. Hit'll git bigger... I'll take hit off"). Old Bayard knocks Caspey through the screen door for refusing to saddle the mare, and Simon scolds his son's "nigger freedom talk." Part Three: Miss Jenny refuses to babysit the driving, old Bayard sits up nights reading Dumas and worrying; the car bursts past on a curve with Isom at the wheel; the harness-strap whipping follows. Miss Jenny predicts "He'll buy an aeroplane." old Bayard climbs to the cedar-chest attic, opens the chest (locked since 1901, when his son John died of yellow fever) and handles the Toledo rapier, the sabre, the dueling pistols, the "dang der'nger," the silver oil-can engraved "Virginia, August 9, 1873," and the brass-bound bible whose fading column of genealogies he extends with a fountain pen: "John Sartoris. July 5, 1918." and "Caroline White Sartoris and son. October 27, 1918." He reads John Sartoris's sentence on genealogy — "In the nineteenth century, genealogy is poppycock." Part Four: in the bank, Snopes the bookkeeper watches Narcissa, sweats, draws out a sheet at noon and writes, folds, burns; then he goes to the Beard hotel, and with the boy Virgil Beard paper-writing in a copybook hand, dictates the anonymous letter composed in the bank — "Code number forty eight. Mister Joe Butler, Saint Louis, Missouri... 'I thought once I would try to forget you...' ... 'Yours truly Hal Wagner.'" — paying the boy and promising an air-gun; "The next afternoon Virgil Beard killed a mockingbird." Part Five: Simon laments the carriage ("Gent'mun equipage"), talks to John Sartoris's ghost in the salvia bed, refuses to cut the switch on the car, then lets Bayard take him and is terrified, kneeling, holding the switch off — "Dat's de way you stops it, Lawd!" — and walks home with his rabbit-foot charm; Bayard runs the car past a wagon and reaches town, where the blind beggar, MacCallum, and the toddy-drinking at the restaurant (Henry's whisky) lead to a near-fight with "Gratton"; then the stallion — a fifteen-hundred-dollar killer in the livery stable — is mounted by Bayard, bursts through the splintered gate, nearly tramples a child, and crashes into concrete; "Then for Bayard, a red shock, then blackness." Part Six: the gathering-up — Dr Peabody bandages his head, V. K. Suratt (with his sewing-machine car) and Hub take him to the spring to drink whisky; the serenade that night with Mitch and three negroes with a bull fiddle, guitar, and clarinet — old tunes and "Goodnight, Ladies" under girls' windows, including the Benbows' where Narcissa watches from the curtain, another caller already hiding on the garage roof; then Bayard circles the square, is taken by Marshal Buck, and sleeps in the marshal's quarters at the jail, thinking "Three score and ten... and he was only twenty-six."
Analysis: This is the book's comic core and its most social chapter. The borrowed khaki and Caspey's "freedom talk" make Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy theatrical — masculine rank is costume, be it soldier's or bank's — while Caspey, Simon, Isom, Elnora, and the harness-strap whipping expose Coercive Labor as the household's raw nerve: dignity is underpinned by exactly the Black labor its speech demeans. The anonymous letters link directly to Language and Ideology — Byron Snopes composes passion to a rehearsed formula, paid by air-gun; the marriage-épisode and the parlor scenes show housekeeping-as-ceremony. The car, the water-tank dive, the stallion, and the "Everybody ought to get married once" line advance the war-driven recklessness that is the novel's doom engine; the garage-roof watcher at the Benbows' (Byron) anticipates the predatory intrusion Sanctuary will specialize in. The stallion sequence and the tomb/lineage talk of the cedar chest deepen Path Dependence and Institutional Drift — a family line that "cannot leave the race to a heirloom" but keeps re-issuing itself into speed, war, and death.

K. Suratt; serenade; Marshal Buck.

Chapter Three

Summary: Horace Benbow arrives on the two-thirty train in ill-fitting khaki, overloaded with parcels and a fragile box of glass, calls his sister "Dear old Narcy," supervises porters, and brings a vase blown on the ship (his cabin caught fire on the voyage from Brest); a Second Division marine scorns his noncombatant triangle. The drive through Jefferson shows the courthouse, the Confederate monument, and the Benbow brick "doll's house" of cedars and an English architect's design; the "meaning of peace" settles on him. At supper Aunt Sally Wyatt asks "Did you bring your Snopes back with you?" — the Snopeses of Frenchman's Bend, among them Flem (soon vice-president of the Sartoris bank) and Montgomery Ward, who went to the Y.M.C.A. with Horace. Part Two gives Horacian history: Sewanee and Oxford, the plan to be an Episcopal minister, Will Benbow's death, and the law office of wills — his practice "the verities and humanities." Part Three moves to Belle's tennis: tea ritual, Mrs Marders, Frankie (the first bobbed head) playing against him, Harry Mitchell's whisky in the bathroom, and Belle's music-room kisses — "I want to have your child, Horace" — broken by little Belle at the door. Part Four covers little Belle's piano recital, Belle's coldness and hostility, the walk home with Narcissa, the note in his pocket, and Narcissa's "You've got the smell of her all over you. Oh, Horry, she's dirty!"; Miss Jenny and Narcissa discuss it in June; Horace's five named glass vases. Part Five returns to young Bayard, temporarily "snared by a rhythm of activities": tractor, grist mill, Caspey taught to drive, then in July the car in the creek with the son-father negro pair (John Henry), broken ribs, the whiskey-dream and the burning of John's coat, bible, trophy, and photograph behind the smokehouse ("Johnny," he whispered, "Johnny"), and the anaesthetic and the fury and "You beast, you beast" from a distraught Narcissa reading to him afterward. Part Six: old man Falls's June visit — salve again for the wen, peppermint candy, the calomel, the horse-race story (Cunnel on Jupiter vs. Zeb Fothergill's sorrel) crowding the spring with a Yankee cavalry dinner and "Surround 'em, boys!"; the ballot-box/carpetbagger story at Miz Winterbottom's; Simon's "financial 'rangements" — the church building money "put out" — and "collateral." Part Seven: the quarrels over the salve, Simon in charge again, the trip with Dr Alford and the young specialist (Dr Brandt) who twitches the wen off in his fingers — "a round spot of skin rosy and fair as any baby's" — the fifty-dollar bill, and Miss Jenny's acid remark. Part Eight: Narcissa's visits to Bayard in his cast, reading to him, his nightmare ("a loop of 'em around my chest"), his re-telling of John's death (the zigzag, the sideslip, "he thumbed his nose at me... and kicked his machine out of the way and jumped"), and gripping her wrists until she sobs. Part Nine: another anonymous letter, Horace telling Narcissa he and Belle will marry ("Belle has a backstairs nature"), the cast off, the promise about the car, and the bridge scene — "You promised," but he jerks the throttle down and drives straight over. Part Ten: the bookkeeper's sickness — the sleepless night, Virgil Beard again, the five dollars, the whipping, moving to I.O. Snopes's restaurant-house, and then the night in the bank: the final letter, the vault opened, the banknotes taken, the drive through Frenchman's Bend at two o'clock, the log house with Turpin and Minnie Sue, his failure ("a name not hers" on his lips — he babbles Narcissa's), and her "You come back tomorrer, when you git over this." He drives away.
Analysis: The chapter is Horace's — Language and Ideology at full stretch, from "the meaning of peace" to "Belle sends love, O Serene," with glass-blowing as the literal shaping of a self. His affair with Belle and its dénouement (Reno, the rented house, her mailbox marooned "backstairs") is the same triangle Sanctuary will follow into its darker key. Against him young Bayard's convalescence and the burning of John's relics keeps War, Crisis, and Collapse burning — the crash you must live through before you strike. The church money and Simon's finance plus old man Falls's Civil War stories (Jupiter, the ballot box) keep Informal Institutions alive — obligation as memory, as patronage, as improvised accounting. The wen/cancer episode frames mortality as household and medical politics and completes the "testament" of a man "keeping me as a reliable witness to the extinction of it." The Snopes material (Flem, I.O., Montgomery Ward, Byron) plants the parasitic counter-class and its secret writing squarely in the middle of Sartoris ceremony, tying into Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy. The Byron strand also prefigures Sanctuary's stalking violence, while the "I tried to keep him from going up there" refrain returns Bayard's guilt to The Unvanquished's question of what a Sartoris inherits and whether he must re-enact or refuse the past.

Snopes; Turpin; Minnie Sue.

Chapter Four

Summary: Part One — a sunny October Sunday: the deputation of six negroes comes round the house for the Second Baptist church money, led by Uncle Bird in his prince-albert coat, with Brother Moore producing his shoe horn, tobacco plug, knife, twine, strap, and "greasy, dogeared notebook" before reading "Sixty sevum dollars en fawty cents"; old Bayard pays by check, and there is a forty-cent coda. Miss Jenny and Narcissa cut flowers in the garden — the last rose that "just dont know how to give up gracefully" — and "We'll name him John." Indian summer brings the sorghum-mill idyll at the negro patriarch's spring: the mule plodding its circle, the "Homer of the cotton fields" paragraph on the mule who "won the prone South from beneath the iron heel of Reconstruction," and a 'possum hunt with Caspey and Isom by lantern where Narcissa, shrinking, sees the creature killed with an axe and turns away; at the gate that night "she tasted fatality and doom," and afterward "she would not go with him again" save to lie with him in the dark. Part Two — Thanksgiving dinner with Dr Peabody at the Sartoris table: the yard-long fish ("Dat's a fish in dis worl', mon"), the catalogue of turkey, ham, quail, squirrel, baked 'possum, and sweet-potato pies, Peabody's mock proposal to Miss Jenny and her "You old gray-headed liar," and the pond with a nigger to bait every hook. Then December rains, Bayard hunting wet at dawn, Miss Jenny's warning ("It's in the blood. Savages, everyone of 'em"), the "Would you do it over again?" "Yes" exchange, and Narcissa's day with Horace in the rain — chocolate pie and the missing anonymous letters. Part Three — Horace sees Joan, Belle's sister: the tiger memory from his first circus at five, her swearing, "You're Horace Benbow, aren't you?", "I'm Belle's sister. I want to see what you look like," her cold carnivorousness, her history (married at eighteen, Honolulu, Australia, Calcutta, the oil-company husband), her prowl into Narcissa's room ("The one that married that Sartoris... I'd like to have known that man"), and her departure on the evening train, leaving his cold room full of her. Part Four — Bayard and old Bayard drive out on the frosty December road; the ford skids, the car hangs over the ravine and crashes down, climbs the bank and stops — and "his grandfather sat motionless beside him... his head bent a little and his hand on the door." He is dead. Part Five — the MacCallums: Bayard rides out to their hills, finds the fox Ethel with the puppies, the old man watchful by his fireplace, and passes Christmas with them — the hybrid foxhound litter of Jackson's experiment that "cant smell, cant bark," General's dignified horror, Buddy's war "charm" ("we'uns git up at fo' oclock"), the old man's "Buyin' turkeys!" grumble, and the memory of Johnny head-singing at Samson's bridge. He leaves after Christmas with the jug of corn whisky from Henry for his grandfather, turning aside at the road's fork rather than face Jefferson. Part Six — the night ride: he turns away from town, drinks the jug, rides to the cedar cold, halts at a negro cabin, asks lodging ("Bayard Sartoris, from Jefferson"), sleeps in the barn loft under a filthy quilt through Christmas Eve, wakes to Christmas Day breakfast among the pickaninnies' "Sandy Claus," hires the negro to drive him eight miles to a railroad town, and in the dusk boards the train with the jug between his legs — past the square's finished fireworks, past where John Sartoris's effigy looks down on his railroad.
Analysis: Chapter Four is the book's hinge of community and doom. The deputation is Informal Institutions at its funniest and most concrete — Brother Moore's notebook, the reinstated "deaconic capacity," and old Bayard's check are improvised accounting for a parish the money never reached. The mule passage, the sorghum mill, and the 'possum hunt fold Coercive Labor into a natural economy — the mule as the South's enduring laborer "who finds bread not only for a race, but for an entire form of behavior." The Thanksgiving table and the MacCallum Christmas against "Buyin' turkeys!" test Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy: hospitality and the barter of food are the older order's currency. Old Bayard's death in the car is the family's secular sacrifice — War, Crisis, and Collapse arriving as automotive accident — and it re-triggers Absalom, Absalom!'s family-doom pattern: the survivor inherits the curse and feels "You caused it all: you killed Johnny." The MacCallum interlude is the novel's cleanest contrast to Sartoris doom (a functional, whole, unhurried household), and its Johnny-memories and the fox/Ethel motif keep the sibling theme of The Reivers' late comic breed in view. Joan's chapter, meanwhile, is Horace's last, most brutal temptation, and her tiger-figuration points to Sanctuary's predatory register.

Chapter Five

Summary: Part One — Horace, in a new crude lumber town, writes his spring letter: "since the essence of spring is loneliness and a little sadness," remembering apple trees and green lanes, "thou still unravished bride of quietude," aware he is "washing one woman's linen in the house of another," crosses out "I believe that Harry," writes "Belle sends love, O Serene," and races to catch the four o'clock train; the chapter meditates on the town's brigand prosperity (the hardwood lumber mill, the $60,000 courthouse, "There was money there... whose, at any one given time, God Himself could not have said"), the shrimp crate he carries home every Tuesday for Belle, and his guilt toward Harry — Harry's picture of "a stricken ox" — ending "She had ghosts in her bed." Part Two — after January: his aunt receives a postcard from Bayard mailed at Tampico, then Mexico City (a wire for money), then Rio; old Bayard is dead "practically from the 'inside out,'" Miss Jenny rummages out the miniature of John, recalls the boys' childhood fights, the curls, the barbering, and the New York police letters; she names the expected child John in her mouth. In June a request for money from San Francisco (robbed), a wire: "Come home you fool and see your family or I will have you arrested." But Bayard is in a Chicago sea of saxophones and painted ladies, with a girl afraid of him, an aviator named Monaghan, and a shabby inventor of a spring-braced wing that needs testing — Bayard agrees; at Dayton, in the inventor's "flying coffin," the wings buckle, come off, and the plane crashes. Part Three — "That day Narcissa's child was born": Simon rejoices ("De little marster done arrive'"), Miss Jenny wires, and the telegram office brings Dr Peabody with the news — "MISSISSIPPI AVIATOR" — and she reads from the paper, then says "I dont have to read it... Thank God that's the last one." A week later Simon is found in Meloney Harris's cabin with his head crushed in: "So that's where that church money went that he 'put out.'" Miss Jenny takes to her bed for three weeks, rises to refuse the christening, and drives with Isom to the cemetery, where she clears the graves and reads the inscriptions: Bayard Sartoris, March 16, 1893–June 11, 1920; the cenotaph of Lieut. John Sartoris, R.A.F., "I bare him on eagles' wings and brought him unto Me"; and Colonel John Sartoris's effigy — "Soldier, Statesman, Citizen of the World / For man's enlightenment he lived / By man's ingratitude he died / Pause here, son of sorrow; remember death" — with the chiselled addition "Fell at the hand of——Redlaw, Sept. 4, 1876." Dr Peabody and his son young Loosh join her; they speak of the new child — "He's got Sartoris blood in him, too." That evening, at the piano in the dusk, Narcissa tells Miss Jenny: "He isn't John. He's Benbow Sartoris," and when Miss Jenny asks "Do you think... because his name is Benbow, he'll be any less a Sartoris and a scoundrel and a fool?" Narcissa smiles and plays on: "for there is death in the sound of it, and a glamorous fatality, like silver pennons downrushing at sunset, or a dying fall of horns along the road to Roncevaux."
Analysis: Chapter Five closes the two arcs. Horace's letter is Language and Ideology distilled — the "unravished bride" citation, the crossed-out Harry, "Belle sends love, O Serene" — words managing cowardice and grief; its rented-house-with-Belle-and-little-Belle is the exact household Sanctuary inherits. Bayard's end — wandering, robbed, the inventor's spring-wing plane — completes War, Crisis, and Collapse as personal and generational suicide, while the "MISSISSIPPI AVIATOR" telegram and the cemetery inscriptions make the family story an archive of Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation fixed in stone — and doctored, as old Bayard had Redlaw's name chiselled in. The naming climax — "We'll name him John" versus "He isn't John. He's Benbow Sartoris" — is the book's purest Legitimacy and Succession: a woman trying to end the doom by renaming the heir, against Miss Jenny's fatalism that a Sartoris "played too late and to an old dead pattern" cannot be changed by a name. The closing "Player and the game... there is death in the sound of it" is the book's explicit merging with Absalom, Absalom!'s doom-aesthetics and with the whole Yoknapatawpha genealogical curse; the child itself reaches toward The Reivers' comic last-acts of the line, and Simon's murder plus Miss Jenny's "last one" resolve the household's own Black story that The Unvanquished will re-narrate from within the war.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • Title origin is inside the extract: Miss Jenny's voice ends a war story "proud and still as banners in the dust" (Chapter One). The book's own apparatus confirms Flags is the uncut version of a novel published in 1929 as Sartoris, cut to ~110,000 words.
  • The wen: Dr Alford diagnoses cancer; Dr Peabody refuses; old man Falls treats it with Choctaw salve from his snuff-tin; in Memphis, Dr Brandt lifts it off in his fingers — "a round spot of skin rosy and fair as any baby's" — and bills fifty dollars.
  • The family bible entries written in by old Bayard: "John Sartoris. July 5, 1918." / "Caroline White Sartoris and son. October 27, 1918." — the oldest archive of the novel's dates.
  • The car: "low and gray," four cylinders, sixteen valves, eight spark-plugs, "guaranteed eighty miles an hour," windshield strip against speed; Miss Jenny's correction "fifty four... speedometer."
  • Center of the novel's comedy: the Second Baptist deputation and Brother Moore's pocket notebook, which takes "Sixty sevum dollars en fawty cents" out of the church funds that Simon "put out" to Meloney Harris — the same money behind his murder.
  • Signature characters introduced here that recur in later Faulkner: V. K. Suratt (the sewing-machine agent with vinegar wit), Flem and I.O. Snopes and Montgomery Ward, and the entire Sartoris lineage; the fox Ethel, Jackson's hybrid hound-fox "breed," and General the old hound at the MacCallums'.
  • The "Homer of the cotton fields" mule paragraph — the mule who "won the prone South from beneath the iron heel of Reconstruction" — is one of the extract's set pieces.
  • "Thou still unravished bride of quietude" (Keats) is Horace's running motto, and "She had ghosts in her bed" closes his chapter.
  • Cemetery inscriptions worth quoting in any note: John's cenotaph "Lieut. John Sartoris, R.A.F. / Killed in action, July 5, 1918 / 'I bare him on eagles' wings and brought him unto Me'"; the Colonel's pedestal "Soldier, Statesman, Citizen of the World / For man's enlightenment he lived / By man's ingratitude he died / Pause here, son of sorrow; remember death," plus the crude added line "Fell at the hand of——Redlaw, Sept. 4, 1876."
