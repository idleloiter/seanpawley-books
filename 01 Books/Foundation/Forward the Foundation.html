Forward the Foundation

Position in the vault

This note sits inside Foundation Sequence as the final Seldon prequel and the direct continuation of Prelude to Foundation: it connects the Demerzel/Daneel material of that book to the institutional setup behind the original Foundation. Chronologically it ends at 12,069 G.E. (1 F.E.), with Seldon's death on Trantor and Gaal Dornick installing the Crisis holograms at Terminus. Its central concern is the machinery of managed decline — the Cleon assassination, the militarized succession crisis, the loss of Dors Venabili, and the discovery of Wanda's mentalic talent — which makes it the anchor note for the public Foundation Project and the hidden Second Foundation. It sits beside Prelude to Foundation as its chronological companion and alongside Foundation as its sequel, and it feeds the vault's doctrine clusters on State Capacity, Empire and Periphery, and Legitimacy and Succession.

Detailed overview

Forward the Foundation is structured as the last biography of Hari Seldon before the Foundation era begins. Its Encyclopedia Galactica openings turn each part into a retrospective historical problem: Eto Demerzel's benevolent despotism, Cleon I's declining reign, Dors Venabili's obscure origin and disappearance, Wanda Seldon's hidden work, and Seldon's own final testimony. The novel is less about discovering psychohistory than about protecting enough time, personnel, political cover, and secrecy for psychohistory to survive the Empire's collapse.

The first part brings Laskin "Jo-Jo" Joranum against Eto Demerzel and forces Seldon to understand why a robot First Minister can be powerful and vulnerable at once. Yugo Amaryl sees that Demerzel's fall would endanger Seldon's grants and the unfinished mathematics; Dors explains that the Zeroth Law and First Law prevent Demerzel from simply altering Joranum if the side effects might harm individuals or humanity. Seldon, Raych, Namarti, Streeling University, and Trantor's crowds become the human tools around a constraint Demerzel cannot cross directly. Seldon defeats Joranum not with psychohistory but with deduction — the Mycogenian origin concealed behind Nishaya, the coached laugh that disproves robothood, and a banishment framed as mercy — and the reward is the First Ministership itself.

The Cleon section converts imperial friendship into tragedy. Mandell Gruber, an Anacreonian gardener who loves open air, trees, geometry, and the Palace grounds planned by Tapper Savand, becomes the accidental hinge of imperial history when Cleon's attempt to reward him by promoting him out of the gardens destroys the one life he wants. The Joranumite aftershocks, the palace bureaucracy, and Seldon's First Minister role show how psychohistory depends on a decaying court whose good intentions can still produce catastrophe. Namarti's infrastructure sabotage and Andorin's gardener-planted assassination plot are simultaneously a collapse of state capacity and a family feud, resolved only when Manella Dubanqua, revealed as a security officer, shoots Andorin — just before Gruber, to escape his office, shoots Cleon.

The Dors section is the emotional center of the book. Wanda's childhood fear of Seldon's sixtieth birthday introduces mortality inside the family, while Dors's identity as protector, historian, wife, and robot-linked guardian becomes increasingly fragile. The book uses Dors's speed, strength, knowledge of robots, connection to Demerzel, and death to make Seldon pay personally for the project that supposedly serves quadrillions. Yugo Amaryl, Raych, Manella, and Wanda all become part of the shrinking circle around him, and Tamwile Elar's Electro-Clarifier — an information technology designed to betray its users — exposes Dors as the robot she has always been.

The final Wanda and epilogue sections prepare the two Foundations. Seldon hears political talk about Anacreon in the Galactic Library, watches the Empire mishandle the periphery, and discovers Wanda's mentalic talent. Stettin Palver, Bor Alurin, Star's End, Gaal Dornick, Terminus, Linge Chen, Agis, the Commission of Public Safety, and the Seldon Vault all appear as pieces of the post-Seldon design. The old man in the wheelchair records Crisis holograms while wondering whether his grand historical work cost Yugo, Raych, Dors, and others too much.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Hari Seldon: psychohistorian, former First Minister, and builder of the Foundation. His motivation shifts from pure mathematics to institutional survival: every choice he makes — letting Raych lie to Joranum, planting the poll-tax trap for General Tennar, sending Wanda to Star's End — is a small intervention he hopes will buy the science time.
  • Eto Demerzel / R. Daneel Olivaw: the robot First Minister who protected Seldon during his flight across Trantor and now labors under the Zeroth Law for the good of humanity. He resigns so that Seldon can take office, then vanishes from the Imperial record while continuing his long-term humanist program.
  • Dors Venabili: Seldon's consort and protector, a robot whose love for Hari has, by her own last words, made her "human." Her arc moves from hidden guardian to exposed casualty of Tamwile Elar's Electro-Clarifier, and her death is the book's emotional hinge.
  • Laskin "Jo-Jo" Joranum: the populist demagogue from (supposedly) Nishaya, secretly a Mycogenian Breakaway. He wants the First Ministership and psychohistory itself as instruments of rule; Seldon destroys him with the truth about Mycogen and the embarrassment of exile.
  • Gambol Deen Namarti: Joranum's right-hand man who rebuilds the movement after Joranum's fall. Motivated by hatred of Seldon and a decade of resentment, he engineers infrastructure sabotage and the gardener-assassin plot that ends in Cleon's death.
  • Cleon I: the Entun-dynasty Emperor, forty when the book opens, eighteen years on the throne (the extract's "quarter-century reign" refers to his total reign across the series). Kindly, vain, and meddling, he rewards Gruber into misery and is assassinated by the very gardener he promoted.
  • Mandell Gruber: Anacreonian Gardener First-Class who lives for the open air and the geometry of the Palace grounds. His one desire — to be left alone — is destroyed by promotion, and his despair produces the Emperor's death.
  • Yugo Amaryl: the Dahlite heatsinker turned mathematician who is psychohistory's truest devotee. He warns Seldon about Joranum, works himself to death on the Prime Radiant, and is the one who first proposes the two Foundations.
  • Raych Seldon: Seldon's foster son, a Dahlite with a talent for inspiring trust. He lures Joranum, infiltrates the Wye conspiracy, marries Manella, writes the book on Dahl, and dies defending an Imperial University on Santanni.
  • Manella Dubanqua: the security officer who plants Raych into the gardener plot and shoots Andorin; she marries Raych and dies, with their daughter Bellis, aboard the vanished Arcadia VII.
  • Wanda Seldon: Raych and Manella's daughter, granddaughter of Hari. Her "mind-touching" ability — revealed when she reads Yugo's mind through the Prime Radiant — becomes the seed of the mentalic Second Foundation.
  • Tamwile Elar: the brilliant, ambitious mathematician whose achaotic equations and Electro-Clarifier advance the Project while his real plan is to kill Dors and take over.
  • General Tennar and Colonel Hender Linn: the junta's strongman and his counselor; they want psychohistory without Seldon and are undone by Seldon's planted tax policy.
  • Emperor Agis XIV: the "Citizen Emperor," powerless before the Legislature and the Commission of Public Safety; his alliance with Seldon is the faint last echo of Imperial legitimacy.
  • Stettin Palver: Seldon's Twister bodyguard and, unknown to almost everyone, a fellow mentalic who becomes Wanda's partner at Star's End.

Major linkages

  • Foundation Sequence: the series hub; this volume is the final chronologically placed Seldon story.
  • Foundation Project: the public, encyclopedia-justified Foundation at Terminus, planned here under the guise of the Encyclopedia Galactica.
  • Second Foundation: the hidden mentalic Foundation at Star's End, designed by Seldon from Wanda's discovery.
  • Empire and Periphery: Anacreon, Santanni, and the border provinces crumbling away from a Trantor that can no longer afford to hold them.
  • State Capacity: the junta, the failing security establishment, the sabotage of infrastructure, and the Empire's inability to tax or police its own capital.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: from Joranum's populism to the military junta, Agis's hollow throne, and the puppet child emperor.
  • Artificial Intelligence: the Laws of Robotics as a constraint on Demerzel, and Dors's programmed love.
  • Knowledge Preservation: the Galactic Library, the Encyclopedia Galactica, the Crisis holograms, and the Seldon Vault.

Themes and concepts to track

  • The fate of a science that needs political protection to survive.
  • Minimalism: the requirement that intervention into a chaotic system be as small as possible to stay predictable.
  • The unintended consequences of benevolent promotion and kindness.
  • The difference between foreseeing collapse and preventing it.
  • Institutions that must die for their own survival plan to work.
  • The hidden double design: a visible Foundation and a secret corrective one.
  • How a ruler's wish for prophecy becomes a tool of those who supply it.
  • The cost of the grand project measured in individual lives.

Core concepts

  • Artificial Intelligence: Demerzel's robotic laws make a powerful First Minister vulnerable when action requires balancing harm to individuals against harm to humanity.
  • Speculative Governance: Seldon designs a visible Foundation at Terminus and a hidden mentalic Foundation at Star's End to guide a collapsing Empire through future crises.
  • Empire and Periphery: Anacreon's distance from Trantor makes imperial force expensive and foreshadows the outer provinces' political breakaway.
  • Information and Coordination: psychohistory needs mathematics, political shelter, personnel, mentalics, secrecy, and timed holograms to turn prediction into coordinated action.
  • Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation: Galactica entries, Crisis holograms, and the Seldon Vault shape how later generations reconstruct people and act on inherited plans.
  • State Capacity: Trantor's failing infrastructure, the degenerate security establishment, and the junta's inability to tax without provoking revolution show the Empire losing grip on its own center.
  • Legitimacy and Succession: every transfer of power in the book — Joranum's bid, the junta, Agis's enthronement, the puppet child — is engineered around legitimacy deficits.
  • Intelligence: Wanda's mentalics, Raych's infiltration, and Dors's surveillance all turn information advantage into a form of governance.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Part 1, Chapter 1

Summary: Yugo Amaryl warns Hari Seldon that Eto Demerzel, the First Minister, is "in deep trouble" because of the demagogue Laskin "Jo-Jo" Joranum, whose slogan is "Government is the people." Amaryl argues that Joranum is using populism to remove Demerzel, manipulate Cleon, and take the throne, and that a Demerzel fall would hasten the Empire's decline before psychohistory is ready. Seldon dismisses the urgency but is unsettled by the argument.
Analysis: Yugo Amaryl gives psychohistory its first realistic political frame: the mathematics can only be finished if the government that funds it survives, so defending Demerzel becomes an act of Information and Coordination rather than friendship. "Government is the people" exposes how Language and Ideology lets a claim about sovereignty double as a claim to office, and Joranum's movement tests the Empire's weakened Legitimacy and Succession from below.
Source anchors: Yugo Amaryl; Eto Demerzel; Laskin Joranum; psychohistory; Streeling University.

Part 1, Chapter 2

Summary: Over Seldon's fortieth birthday he hears the hypnotic "Jo ... Jo ... Jo ..." chant on the campus. He finds Gambol Deen Namarti holding an unauthorized rally at the University Field with "goons" in support. Seldon vaults onto the platform, demands a permit, throws one attacker with a Twisting move, and threatens to ruin Namarti's larynx unless the rally disperses — the crowd switches sides and the meeting collapses.
Analysis: Seldon's aging body and his Twister reflexes make the rally a test of his own declining capacity, but the episode is also a lesson in crowd psychology: an audience follows momentum, and one visible act of defiance flips its allegiance. His insistence on the University permit shows how Institutions still police speech within a decaying order, while the chant "Jo" demonstrates the State Capacity of mass politics over individual reason.
Source anchors: fortieth birthday; University Field; Gambol Deen Namarti; goons; Twisting.

Part 1, Chapter 3

Summary: Dors Venabili is waiting for Seldon and is displeased; she notes that she sent Raych out to find him. He explains the illegal rally and his intervention. He tells her that Demerzel is "untouchable" because he can alter emotions, and she replies flatly that Demerzel "has shortcomings" and is not untouchable — a remark that unsettles him because she understands robots.
Analysis: The chapter establishes Dors as both wife and keeper, a partner whose protective role Seldon accepts even as he chafes at it. Her quiet correction that Demerzel "has shortcomings" marks the first open suggestion that the Laws of Robotics limit the First Minister, turning Artificial Intelligence into a constraint his human protector understands better than he does. The dinner preparation and the domestic setting humanize a relationship that the book will later reveal as programmed.
Source anchors: Dors Venabili; Raych; Eto Demerzel; robot knowledge.

Part 1, Chapter 4

Summary: Dors explains what Seldon knows about robots but does not understand: Demerzel is a robot governed by the Zeroth Law, which takes precedence over the First Law and forbids harming humanity as a whole. Because altering Joranum's mind would create unpredictable side effects, Demerzel cannot simply change him — any change must be minimal. Seldon concludes that if Demerzel can do nothing, then he himself must act.
Analysis: Dors domesticates the most important theoretical move of the book: the necessity of minimalism, the principle that any intervention into a chaotic system must be as small as possible to remain predictable. Applied to Artificial Intelligence, the same logic means a powerful robot First Minister is paralyzed by his own laws — a beautiful parallel to psychohistory's own helplessness. The chapter frames Seldon's ethics: he is not bound by the Laws of Robotics, so he can act where his protector cannot.
Source anchors: Dors Venabili; Eto Demerzel; Zeroth Law; First Law; minimalism.

Part 1, Chapter 5

Summary: Seldon struggles to arrange a private meeting with Demerzel, who is busy running the Empire. Before the appointment comes, he receives an archaic note in ornamental script: "I CRAVE AN AUDIENCE WITH PROFESSOR HARI SELDON," signed Laskin Joranum. Seldon is amused and intrigued, accepts, and decides to bring Raych as his companion.
Analysis: Joranum's deliberately archaic letter is a piece of performance politics: the phrasing and script manufacture curiosity and flatter Seldon into an audience. It shows how Speculative Governance works through spectacle as much as force, and Seldon's decision to take Raych signals that even a scholar now understands the need for protection in a world where Empire and Periphery violence can reach a university office.
Source anchors: appointment; Laskin Joranum; Raych; archaic message.

Part 1, Chapter 6

Summary: Joranum visits Seldon's office with Namarti, who formally apologizes for the Field incident. Joranum probes Seldon's past, his psychohistory paper, and his connection to Demerzel, then offers himself as Seldon's future protector if Demerzel falls. He wants psychohistory as a tool to rule the Empire more efficiently, and he ends with a quasi-religious appeal to the quadrillions of the Galaxy.
Analysis: The interview lays bare the political market for prediction: Joranum, like Cleon and Demerzel before him, wants Seldon as a legitimating instrument, treating psychohistory as a weapon of rule. His appeal to the "quadrillions" is an exercise in Legitimacy and Succession, while Seldon's careful denials show how valuable a science becomes by being publicly dismissed. Joranum's charisma is analyzed as a technology: the half-smile, the orator's voice, the staged humility.
Source anchors: Laskin Joranum; Gambol Deen Namarti; psychohistory; protector offer.

Part 1, Chapter 7

Summary: From Namarti's point of view, Joranum explains why they must not pressure Seldon: psychohistory, he suspects, is real but frozen, and Demerzel may be holding it in reserve. He tells the folktale of the Ax of Venn — an instrument only its owner can lift — to show that forced loyalty would be worthless. He proposes instead to work through Raych, whom he saw watching him with "his heart in his eyes."
Analysis: The Ax of Venn parable is Joranum's theory of Knowledge Preservation: some instruments are so specialized that they cannot be stolen or transferred, which is exactly why control of Seldon must come as voluntary allegiance. Namarti's distrust of the "fortune-telling" frame reveals the empirical boundary of the movement, while Joranum's decision to recruit Raych converts Seldon's own family into an intelligence channel. The scene reads as a study in how Intelligence operations recruit through emotional sympathy.
Source anchors: Namarti; Joranum; Ax of Venn; Raych.

Part 1, Chapter 8

Summary: Raych, growing a Dahlite mustache, tells Seldon he should not have seen Joranum and admits he is half-impressed by Joranum's call for sector equality and popular representation. Seldon explains that Joranum has no responsibilities and that equality is easy to preach and hard to govern; Raych insists his loyalty to his father exceeds his concern for the Empire.
Analysis: Raych's divided mind is the human cost of the political game: he is drawn, as a Dahlite, to Joranum's promise of equality even while he distrusts the man. Seldon's reply — that the Emperor must manage twenty-five million planets while the demagogue answers to no one — is a compressed theory of State Capacity and the difference between promise and governance. The scene also exposes how Elite Formation normally excludes men like Raych, which is precisely why Joranum's message lands.
Source anchors: Raych; Dahlite mustache; sector equality; loyalty.

Part 1, Chapter 9

Summary: Seldon investigates Nishaya, Joranum's supposed home world, and discovers that none of Joranum's much-repeated folktales are known there and that its inhabitants speak with a thick accent Joranum does not have. He reasons that Joranum invented the Nishayan origin to avoid scrutiny, and he realizes from the perfection of Joranum's hair that the demagogue is actually from Mycogen, the hairless, past-worshipping sector of Trantor.
Analysis: Seldon's deduction is a model of forensic Legibility: a detail as small as an accent and a hairline exposes an entire fabricated identity. The Mycogenian truth matters because Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy carries political poison — being laughed at is fatal for someone seeking the throne. The chapter shows psychohistory's method working before it has any mathematics, as Seldon reads a society through its smallest traces.
Source anchors: Nishaya; Mycogen; accent; artificial hair.

The scene shows how Institutions and prejudice are linked, since Mycogen's comic reputation is the very mechanism that protects the sector and ruins its deserters.

Part 1, Chapter 11

Summary: Seldon meets Demerzel privately at the Dome's Edge Hotel, and Demerzel invites him to use his real name: R. Daneel Olivaw. Over Mycogenian cooking Daneel discusses the Decennial Convention, confesses that he does not "eat," and confirms that Joranum is genuinely dangerous. Analysis: The private dinner reveals governing under a robotic conscience: Daneel can read every risk but cannot act, because the Zeroth Law binds Artificial Intelligence to balance harm against humanity's interest. "We argue in a useless circle" names the coordination problem at the heart of the book — prediction cannot rescue a system that must survive to fund the prediction. The scene also prepares Seldon's later discovery that his own protector is similarly nonhuman.

The gamble is Information and Coordination without a network behind it, one man's judgment carrying the risk of everyone the science depends on.

The aging Emperor and the unchanging Demerzel also make the book's mortality theme explicit at the top of the political hierarchy.

Part 1, Chapter 18

Joranum's reasoning — Demerzel cannot publicly disprove robothood without destroying himself — is a precise piece of Language and Ideology politics, and the slogan turns a children's legend into a mass weapon. Namarti's skepticism about robots keeps the reader aware that belief, not truth, is what moves crowds, which is the same assumption Seldon is betting on.

Cleon's misreading — that he has a working prophecy — is the book's comedy of belief, and it fatally depersonalizes the Emperor's reliance on Seldon. The scene underlines an Information and Coordination failure: the ruler hears what he wants, so the scientist is assumed to command a future he has not yet calculated.

Sunmaster Fourteen is a second, targeted lever, one that bypasses mass bigotry by feeding the Mycogenian hierarchy its own reasons to punish a Breakaway. Part 1, Chapter 22

Summary: Seldon coaches Demerzel to laugh — rehearsing smiles, twinkles, and bodily humor — until the First Minister can reproduce the sound on cue. On subetheric holovision Demerzel delivers a grave, matter-of-fact talk about Empire-wide crises and then is asked directly if he is a robot. He smiles, laughs, and answers only "Must I really answer that?", leaving the audience laughing with him.
Analysis: The coached laugh is a masterstroke of Speculative Governance: the one thing no one can imagine a robot doing becomes the disproof of robothood. It also reveals the performative core of rule — legitimacy is manufactured in the audience's laughter, not in documents. Dors's indignant "no one makes their eyes twinkle" keeps the scene ludicrous and human, and it is precisely this unrehearsable humanity that saves the robot First Minister.

Dors's skepticism keeps the reader aware that the whole plan rests on unproven judgment.

Part 2, Chapter 3

Summary: In his laboratory, Seldon and Amaryl work with the Prime Radiant — a black cube producing equations in the air. Amaryl's methods now yield a genuine psychohistorical finding: the Empire faces two possibilities, trouble at the center (civil war on Trantor) or trouble at the periphery (breakaway Outer Worlds), and the two are mutually exclusive. Seldon chooses to keep Trantor stable and let the Periphery go, declaring that will mark "the beginning of the Fall of the Galactic Empire."
Analysis: This is psychohistory's first real prediction, and Seldon's choice of periphery-over-center is an explicit policy of managed decline: sacrifice the edges to buy the core time to finish the science. Amaryl's mention of Sisyphus, the myth Seldon has to explain, supplies the book's image of history as an endless rolling uphill. The tradeoff is the clearest statement in the series of Empire and Periphery as a governance choice rather than an accident.

Dors's warning about Joranumite remnants, dismissed as shadow-chasing, becomes the book's most consequential piece of foresight.

The name "Star's End" — revealed through mind-touch rather than speech — seals Second Foundation as a place known only to those who can read it.

Epilogue

Summary: Seldon, eighty-one, dictates his own testimony: former First Minister, Professor Emeritus, Director of the Psychohistory Research Project, Executive Editor of the Encyclopedia Galactica, creator of the Foundation. He has just finished recording the Crisis holograms, now taken by Gaal Dornick to Terminus for the Seldon Vault; he recalls Linge Chen's banishment of the Project two years earlier, the loss of Emperor Agis and the puppet child emperor who now sits on the throne, and the work of Wanda and Stettin at Star's End. Alone with his Prime Radiant, watching the equations that will be his legacy, he sees the Terminus equation glowing, the Trantor figures skewed, and Star's End steady — then dies slumped over his desk, clutching his activated Prime Radiant.
Analysis: The epilogue is Seldon's first-person audit of the whole project — "Was I so concerned with the grand sweep of psychohistory that the people and events that intersected my life sometimes seemed inconsequential by comparison?" — the moral reckoning that converts the science into Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation. Gaal Dornick, Terminus, and the Seldon Vault turn the plan into timed inheritance: holograms that will speak at the Crises, enough knowledge to guide without revealing the whole. Linge Chen's banishment, revealed as the manipulation that placed the Project at Terminus, closes the loop on the Foundation's founding lie, and Star's End confirms the double design of a public encyclopedia colony and a hidden mentalic guardian.
Source anchors: eighty-one; Crisis holograms; Gaal Dornick; Terminus; Seldon Vault; Linge Chen; Agis; Star's End; Prime Radiant.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • Timeline: set in 12,061–12,069 G.E. (with the Epilogue closing in 12,069, 1 F.E.); Seldon is forty at the start of Part 1, forty when he becomes First Minister and fifty when he relinquishes the post, sixty at the Dors section, seventy in Part 4, and eighty-one in the Epilogue.
  • The Zeroth Law: "A robot may not injure humanity, or, through inaction, allow humanity to come to harm" — the law that binds both Demerzel and, through protection duty, Dors.
  • Minimalism: the rule that any intervention into a chaotic social system must be as small as possible to stay predictable; Seldon applies it to politics as well as mathematics.
  • The Joranum slogan: "Government is the people" — a single slogan that simultaneously legitimizes popular rule and rationalizes a demagogue's seizure of it.
  • The Ax of Venn: Nishayan folktale of a magic ax only its owner can lift, used by Joranum to explain why Seldon must be won voluntarily.
  • Desperance: the outlawed chemical modification of a tranquilizer that induces hopelessness, used by Andorin to make Raych his reluctant assassin.
  • The Tiger Woman: the public legend of Dors Venabili, built on the sergeant incident and the Palace grounds invasion; the reputation does the protecting.
  • Conservation of personal problems: Amaryl's finding that friction in a closed group can be shifted but never reduced, a social analogue of physical conservation laws.
  • The poll-tax experiment: Seldon's first rehearsed psychohistoric intervention, deliberately steering General Tennar toward a tax that will ignite riots and unseat the junta.
  • "Lemonade death" / "Elar-Monay death": Wanda's eight-year-old mishearing of the Electro-Clarifier's personal name, which becomes the clue to the plot against Dors.
  • Section 33A2D17: the Prime Radiant address of the Star's End equation, revealed to Wanda through direct mind-contact at the end of Part 4.
  • Terminus: an archaic word meaning "the end of the line"; the uninhabited far-periphery world, outside the Anacreon province, chosen as the Foundation's home under the cover of the Encyclopedia Galactica.
  • Star's End: the location of the secret Second Foundation, given to the mentalics through Seldon's mind and never spoken in the text.
  • The Arcadia VII: the hypership on which Manella and Bellis vanish, setting up the disappearance that will fascinate later Foundation historians.
  • Raven Seldon: the mocking public name for the predictor of doom; Agis and others use it to mark how unpopular accurate foresight becomes.
  • The ghost rule: the junta-era custom of keeping the Palace running as though an Emperor existed, an example of institutions surviving their institution.
  • Encyclopedia Galactica entry on Seldon's death: the closing record in the extract, noting the discovery of the activated Prime Radiant, the instrument's shipment to Gaal Dornick on Terminus, the jettisoning of Seldon's body into space per his instructions, and Demerzel's silent appearance at the funeral — the last trace of robot intervention.
