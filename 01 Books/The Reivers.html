Detailed overview

Faulkner frames The Reivers as Grandfather Lucius Priest telling a later generation about the spring of 1905, when eleven-year-old Lucius Priest leaves Jefferson, Mississippi, in Boss Priest's new Winton Flyer with Boon Hogganbeck and Ned William McCaslin. The title's theft is comic and literal: they take an automobile that belongs to Lucius's grandfather while the family is away for a funeral in Bay St. Louis. But the narrative is also about older forms of theft, barter, borrowing, racial hierarchy, male pride, sexual commerce, gambling, and the ambiguous education that adults call experience after children have already paid for it.

The novel begins in Jefferson's livery-stable world, not on the road. Maury Priest counts freight-bill money, John Powell hides a cherished .41 revolver in his jumper, Ludus borrows a team for private errands, and Boon tries to shoot Ludus but only wounds a girl outside Cousin Ike McCaslin's hardware store. Faulkner uses this opening to establish the comedy's operating laws: adults conceal what everyone knows, Black and white workers negotiate by indirection, family authority is both real and porous, and Boon is dangerous because he is loyal, impulsive, and almost childlike.

The automobile brings modernity into that order. Boss Priest buys the Winton Flyer because Colonel Sartoris's ordinance and Mr Buffaloe's homemade car have turned bank rivalry into mechanical rivalry. The car is equipped with kerosene lamps, dusters, goggles, a smell-tight gasoline can, a shovel, an axe, barbed wire, block and tackle, and a ritual of Saturday rides through Jefferson to accustom horses to the machine. Boon's passion for it, Grandmother Sarah's terror of it, Ned's refusal to wash it, and Boss Priest's prediction that roads and bonds will follow automobiles all make the Winton both a toy and a social forecast.

The trip to Memphis is built from evasions. Lucius learns to drive at Mr Buffaloe's old motordrome, lies at Cousin Zack Edmonds's McCaslin farm, locks the carriage-house door behind an empty shed, survives Hurricane Creek mud, discovers Ned hidden under the tarpaulin, and reaches Miss Ballenbaugh's by night. Once in Memphis, the stolen-car adventure mutates: Boon takes Lucius to Miss Reba's brothel on Catalpa Street; Miss Corrie, whose real name is Everbe Corinthia, becomes Lucius's moral center; Mr Binford, Otis, Minnie, Sam Caldwell, and a stolen racehorse named Lightning draw the party into a second theft that is supposed to redeem the first.

Ned is the book's great tactician. He swaps Boss Priest's automobile for Lightning, calculates that the horse can win back the car at Parsham, moves the horse through the Memphis depot in a boxcar with Sam Caldwell's help, trains Lucius as jockey, manipulates McWillie and Acheron, hides Minnie's stolen gold tooth in a tobacco sack, and eventually reveals that Lightning runs for sardines. Faulkner makes Ned both a comic mastermind and a man forced to work under racial constraint: he can outthink almost everyone, but he must often do it through masks, deference, secrecy, and the help of Black networks around Uncle Parsham Hood, Lycurgus, and the McCaslin family.

Lucius's education is not a simple loss of innocence. He sees prostitution, police coercion, racial insult, gambling, theft, violence against Everbe, and his own ability to lie. Yet he also sees Miss Reba defending her house's women, Sam using railroad authority to help strangers, Mr Poleymus distinguishing "Law" from people, Uncle Parsham treating him with grave courtesy, and Ned carrying the burden of practical judgment while exhausted. The novel repeatedly asks Lucius to choose before he has language for what he is choosing, and then to live with consequences that a beating cannot settle.

The ending returns Lucius to Jefferson but refuses to erase the trip. Boss Priest retrieves the automobile, Boon decides to marry Everbe, Minnie recovers her gold tooth, Ned gives gambling money to Uncle Parsham's church rather than to Boss, and Father Maury is stopped from reducing the whole episode to a whipping with a razor strop. Boss teaches Lucius that a gentleman bears responsibility for his actions, including actions he merely consented to by failing to say no. Faulkner closes years later with Everbe and Boon's child, Lucius Priest Hogganbeck, turning the adventure's shame, loyalty, and affection into a name.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter 1

Summary: The opening chapter introduces eleven-year-old Lucius Priest in Maury Priest's Jefferson livery-stable office, where he collects freight-bill money for ten cents a week while Father counts the canvas sack. Boon Hogganbeck bursts in to seize the stable pistol and shoot Ludus, then steals John Powell's hidden .41 snub-nosed revolver from the harness-room jumper. The chapter pauses over John Powell's private pistol, Mr Ballott, Luster, Gabe the blacksmith, Dan Grinnup or Grenier, Frenchman's Bend, and the unwritten agreement by which everyone knows what must not officially exist. Boon fires five shots in the Square, misses Ludus, shatters Cousin Ike McCaslin's hardware-store window, grazes a Black girl's buttock, and winds up before Sheriff Hampton, Doctor Peabody, and Judge Stevens over the difference between being called narrow-asted and narrow-headed. Source anchors: Maury Priest, Boon Hogganbeck, Ludus, John Powell pistol, Cousin Ike, Sheriff Hampton, narrow-headed.

Analysis: Maury Priest's office, John Powell's pistol, and Ludus's mule errand give the novel its first lesson in Informal Institutions: Jefferson runs on rules that are known, denied, and selectively enforced. Boon's assault on Ludus shows why the Priests protect him while fearing him, because his loyalty and outrage produce public damage before anyone can translate them into order. Cousin Ike, Sheriff Hampton, Doctor Peabody, and Judge Stevens place the comedy inside a kin-and-office network where punishment is negotiated, and Lucius learns that adult truth often arrives through reconstruction rather than confession.

Chapter 2

Summary: The second chapter explains Boon's status as a shared responsibility of the Priests, McCaslins, Edmondses, De Spains, and Compsons, then traces him back to Major de Spain's hunting camp and the care of General Compson. Faulkner names old Issetibbeha, Chickasaw ancestry, Sam Fathers, Walter Ewell, Tennie's Jim, Carothers McCaslin's place, Thomas Sutpen's old river-bottom wilderness, Manfred de Spain, and the retreat of hunting country before lumber companies and roads. Boon is brave, faithful, enormous, and unreliable; he can nurse General Compson through the woods but still cannot shoot a deer at ten paces. The chapter then turns to Boss Priest's Winton Flyer, Colonel Sartoris, Mr Buffaloe's homemade automobile, the Jefferson ordinance against mechanical vehicles, Boon's love for the machine, Grandmother Sarah's alarm, Ned's skepticism, and the Saturday automobile rides in dusters and goggles. Source anchors: Major de Spain, General Compson, Sam Fathers, Winton Flyer, Colonel Sartoris, Mr Buffaloe, Grandmother Sarah.

Analysis: Major de Spain's camp and General Compson give Boon an older wilderness function before the automobile gives him a modern one. Sam Fathers, Walter Ewell, and the retreating river-bottom country connect this comic road novel to Faulkner's larger Yoknapatawpha memory of hunting, timber, and lost woods. The Winton Flyer, Colonel Sartoris, Mr Buffaloe, and Grandmother Sarah make Technological Change domestic and social: the car rearranges labor, authority, status rivalry, and desire in Jefferson before the trip ever begins.

Chapter 3

Summary: Grandfather Lessep dies in Bay St. Louis, and the funeral removes Boss Priest, Grandmother Sarah, Father Maury, and Mother Alison from Jefferson by Number Twenty-three. Boon understands before Lucius does that the Winton Flyer will be free for several days, so he teaches Lucius to drive at Mr Buffaloe's field while Aunt Callie prepares to take the children, including Alexander, to Cousin Zachary Edmonds's McCaslin farm seventeen miles away. Lucius lies to Cousin Zack about staying with Cousin Ike to fish, returns with Boon, hides luggage and the gasoline can under the tarpaulin, locks the carriage-house door behind the absent automobile, and chooses the Memphis road instead of the McCaslin fork. At Hurricane Creek, Boon lifts the car from the mud with a fence rail, and near Mr Wyott's place he discovers Ned hidden under the tarpaulin with Lucius McCaslin's old grip, a Bible, and a little whiskey. Source anchors: Bay St. Louis, Number Twenty-three, Mr Buffaloe field, Aunt Callie, Cousin Zack, Hurricane Creek, tarpaulin, Ned.

Analysis: The Bay St. Louis funeral supplies opportunity, but Lucius's lesson at Mr Buffaloe's field supplies complicity: he is no longer merely carried along by Boon. Cousin Zack, Cousin Ike, Aunt Callie, and the McCaslin fork make each lie local and personal, so the adventure begins as a betrayal of people who know exactly where Lucius belongs. Hurricane Creek proves the stolen automobile can travel only by bodily labor and improvisation, while Ned under the tarpaulin turns Boon's private escapade into a three-man expedition whose real manager has just revealed himself.

Chapter 4

Summary: Ned insists he has as much right to the trip as Boon or Lucius, and the party reaches Miss Ballenbaugh's near the old Iron Bridge and Wyott's Crossing by night. Grandfather Lucius's narration gives the history of Ballenbaugh's from Chickasaw crossings, Vicksburg river traffic, cotton wagons, Colonel Sartoris's railroad, horse thieves, moonshine, vanished revenuer footprints, and the fist-conversions of Baptist Hiram Hightower to the present Miss Ballenbaugh's clean loft and excellent table. Boon gives Miss Ballenbaugh, Alice, and Ephum a ride in the automobile, while Ned jokes that Boon is showing off. During the night Boon offers to turn back, but by morning they cross the Iron Bridge, enter another county, and confront Hell Creek bottom, where mud, poles, brush, block and tackle, a local man with mules, and six dollars are needed before the Winton reaches the road to Memphis. Source anchors: Miss Ballenbaugh, Iron Bridge, Wyott's Crossing, Hiram Hightower, Alice, Ephum, Hell Creek, six dollars.

Analysis: Miss Ballenbaugh's house links the road trip to a layered local past of Chickasaw crossings, ferry commerce, criminal refuge, revival violence, and domestic order. The Iron Bridge and Hell Creek test the automobile against terrain that still belongs to mules, ramps, mud, and men who charge six dollars for rescue. Boon's offer to return and Lucius's refusal matter because the boy's wrongdoing becomes deliberate; he cannot later claim that Boon, Ned, or the Winton carried him past choice.

Chapter 5

Summary: After Hell Creek, the travelers wash tools and clothes in the creek, eat Miss Ballenbaugh's lunch of ham, fried chicken, biscuits, pear preserves, cake, and buttermilk, and enter a more prosperous, automobile-marked road toward Memphis. Lucius drives part of the way before Boon retakes the wheel near streetcars, boulevards, Main Street, the Gaston, the Peabody, and the Gayoso, then turns toward Miss Reba's brothel rather than a respectable hotel. Ned leaves to pursue his own Memphis business, while Boon gives Lucius the switch key and wallet before Miss Reba, Minnie with the gold tooth, and Miss Corrie receive them. Boon makes Lucius bathe, prepares him to keep quiet about the house, and introduces him to Mr Binford, whose Sunday rules, gambling weakness, lost forty dollars, quarrel with Miss Reba, and exposure by Otis end when Ned appears in the back yard with a horse. Source anchors: Hell Creek, Memphis, Gayoso, Miss Reba, Minnie gold tooth, Miss Corrie, Mr Binford, Otis.

Analysis: The Memphis arrival shifts the book from road comedy to sexual and urban education, with Gayoso respectability deliberately bypassed for Miss Reba's controlled disorder. Minnie gold tooth, Miss Corrie, Mr Binford, and Otis give Lucius different forms of appetite and discipline to decode, none of them available in Jefferson's family language. Ned's arrival with the horse at the end converts the brothel visit from Boon's object of desire into Ned's horse-racing plot, and the lost automobile begins to require a new theft for its recovery.

Chapter 6

Summary: Ned explains that he traded Boss Priest's automobile for a chestnut gelding because Boss prefers a real horse to the Winton, and because a horse at Parsham can win back the car in a three-heat race. He recalls his legendary mule, bought from McCaslin Edmonds at ten cents a week and unbeaten across forty or fifty miles, then argues that the new horse has a similar kind of sense even if it is not a mule. The horse proves to be a nervous, valuable-looking three-year-old, and Boon suspects it is stolen; Miss Reba tells them to get it out of Memphis, while Sam Caldwell and Miss Corrie help plan its rail movement. The chapter also brings Otis's corruption into the attic, where he tells Lucius about Aunt Fittie, Bee or Everbe Corinthia, peepholes, pugnuckling, and nickel spectators; Lucius attacks him, cuts his hand on Otis's pocketknife, and receives Everbe's promise that her Arkansas past will not be her fault anymore. Source anchors: Boss's automobile, chestnut gelding, Ned's mule, Parsham, Miss Reba, Sam Caldwell, Otis, Everbe Corinthia.

Analysis: Ned's mule story gives him a theory of animal intelligence that will drive the race plot, and it also establishes why his judgment outranks Boon's panic. The chestnut gelding, Parsham, and Sam Caldwell show Ned building a route from theft to restitution through racing, railroads, and Black practical knowledge. Otis, Aunt Fittie, and Everbe Corinthia give Lucius a harsher education than the car theft did: he fights not from boyish adventure but because he recognises Everbe as someone injured before she could defend herself.

Chapter 7

Summary: Sam Caldwell arrives in railroad uniform with a horse blanket and becomes the key to getting Lightning to Parsham by train. Boon resents Sam's authority and attention to Miss Corrie, but Miss Reba forces him toward apology, and the group leads the horse through closed Sunday-night saloons, blue laws, a Memphis policeman, Second and Third streets, and the Union depot. Otis talks about policemen, apples, peanuts, "jack," Minnie’s gold tooth, Parchman, Arkansas, and his eighty-five cents, while Ned and Lucius keep him moving. At the depot, Sam, Charley, Boon, Miss Corrie, Ned, and Lucius move a boxcar with crowbars, improvise a ramp from a chicken walk, build a stall with planks, hay, water, and grain, secure a baggage check, and leave Ned with a sandwich, whiskey, and Lightning until the four o'clock train. Source anchors: Sam Caldwell, railroad uniform, blue law, Union depot, Charley, boxcar, chicken walk, baggage check.

Analysis: Sam Caldwell's railroad uniform gives the thieves temporary legitimacy, which is why Ned trusts him and Boon resents him. The blue law, Union depot, policeman, and Charley turn Memphis into a night machine of permissions that can be bent when the right worker knows the right switch, platform, and baggage procedure. The boxcar, chicken walk, and baggage check make State Capacity comic but real: Ned's outlaw plan succeeds by converting a stolen horse into official freight rather than by avoiding institutional routine.

Chapter 8

Summary: At the Memphis depot, Ned has already named the horse Forked Lightning, arranged for Sam to switch the boxcar onto the train, and asks for Otis as a lighter jockey because Lucius's cut hand may be risky. Everbe agrees to bring Otis on a later train, while Boon sulks over Sam and Lucius defends her enough to force Boon to relent. At Parsham, Ned, Lucius, and Lightning meet Lycurgus, Uncle Parsham Hood, and a clean dog-trot house with locust trees, chinaberries, hounds, a cow, mules, ham, and a McClellan saddle. Ned tests Lightning in the creek pasture, learns that the horse runs back toward him rather than forward into empty track, slips Lucius's bandaged hand into a wool sock, uses a switch and the smell of sardines or sour dean, and watches Lightning surge when Ned says he has the reward; then Butch Lovemaiden arrives, handles Everbe, challenges Boon, insults Ned, and refuses Otis's inflated riding price. Source anchors: Forked Lightning, Parsham, Lycurgus, Uncle Parsham Hood, McClellan saddle, wool sock, Butch Lovemaiden, sour dean.

Analysis: Parsham gives Ned the home ground he needs, even though it is not Jefferson, because Lycurgus and Uncle Parsham Hood provide shelter, tack, food, and racial trust outside white supervision. The McClellan saddle, wool sock, and sour dean show the race becoming a technical problem: Lightning's body is capable, but his attention must be aimed. Butch Lovemaiden enters as a corrupt badge attached to sexual aggression, and his handling of Everbe exposes how racial trust networks and local household authority have to protect what formal white law may endanger.

Chapter 9

Summary: Everbe takes Lucius to a local doctor for the cut hand while Butch drinks with Boon and jokes about ether, whiskey, and Lovemaiden authority. The doctor recognises Everbe's profession with a sigh about not having been to Memphis in thirty-five years, asks for a keepsake instead of payment, and receives a garter after confirming the hand is already treated. Otis disappears from the surrey, and the party reaches the Parsham hotel without him; Everbe then tells Boon she has quit Miss Reba's world and intends to find work washing dishes, ironing, cooking, hoeing, or picking cotton. Miss Reba and Minnie arrive on Sam's train, reveal that Otis has stolen Minnie's gold tooth, and Ned reports that Uncle Parsham's hounds treed Otis in a gum sapling behind the henhouse; Miss Reba confronts Butch at the hotel and uses local constable knowledge to get him away from Everbe for the night. Source anchors: local doctor, Everbe's garter, Butch Lovemaiden, Parsham hotel, Sam's train, Minnie’s tooth, gum sapling, constable.

Analysis: The doctor, Everbe's garter, and the bandaged hand show Lucius passing through adult economies of sex, debt, and care without fully understanding them. Otis's theft of Minnie's tooth turns his earlier talk about money into an actual violation, and the gum sapling makes him comic without making him harmless. Miss Reba's handling of Butch through the threat of the constable proves that she understands small-town law better than Boon does, while Everbe's decision to quit makes the rest of the plot morally larger than recovering Boss Priest's automobile.

Chapter 10

Summary: On race morning, Lucius thanks Everbe for washing his clothes, learns from her that Sam can find her a job in Chattanooga or elsewhere, and tells her he knows and accepts the name Everbe Corinthia. Ned brings Lucius through the hotel kitchen, warns that the two o'clock first heat at Colonel Linscomb's half-mile track must be lost on purpose, and takes him to a private spring-branch hideout with Lightning, Lycurgus, Uncle Parsham, corn bread, sidemeat, and buttermilk. Ned plants confusion in McWillie by saying Coppermine or Lightning likes to follow another horse until he sees the finish line, while Bobo Beauchamp briefly appears and raises the possibility that the automobile's location is known. At the track, the first false starts add extra distance to Acheron, the first real heat lets McWillie win by a neck, Boon fights in the crowd, Butch approaches with another man, and Ned passes Lucius a tobacco sack containing Minnie's gold tooth before being arrested. Source anchors: Everbe Corinthia, Chattanooga, Colonel Linscomb track, spring branch, McWillie, Bobo Beauchamp, Acheron, tobacco sack.

Analysis: Everbe Corinthia and Chattanooga show Lucius becoming the keeper of someone else's hoped-for new life, which is why her name matters so much. Ned's spring-branch preparations, McWillie talk, and planned first loss prove that he is running a psychological race against Acheron's rider as much as a physical race against Acheron. The tobacco sack with Minnie's tooth ties the comic subplot to the arrest, because Ned has to protect restitution, Lightning, and Lucius all at once while Butch tries to turn the law into private revenge.

Chapter 11

Summary: Butch and the Beat Four constable, Mr Poleymus, take Lightning and arrest Ned under a legal paper about the horse's ownership, while the crowd argues over whether the race and bets still stand. Sam demands to know whether he, Boon, and Ned are under arrest, then leaves for Memphis to find better law, while Lucius refuses to go with him and chooses Uncle Parsham Hood. Ned tells Mr Poleymus that there is a place where Law stops and people start, and Poleymus accepts Lucius's wish to stay with Uncle Parsham rather than forcing him into a white household. Uncle Parsham teaches Lucius how to drive a mule respectfully, feeds him supper with Mary, says a dignified grace, takes him fishing at Mary's hole, hears from Lycurgus that Boon is in the woodshed, the women are guarded at the hotel, Lightning is in the hotel stable, Sam has made bond for Ned, and a 9:40 train to Jefferson is available if Lucius wants to go home. Source anchors: Mr Poleymus, Beat Four, legal paper, Sam Caldwell, Uncle Parsham Hood, mule lesson, Mary's hole, 9:40 train.

Analysis: Mr Poleymus first appears as law that can be misused, but his exchange with Ned over where Law stops separates him from Butch's badge-and-pistol bullying. Uncle Parsham Hood, the mule lesson, Mary's hole, and Mary's supper show Moral Economy at its quietest: Lucius receives care, dignity, and instruction in a Black household that the constable initially doubts a white boy should enter. The 9:40 train sharpens Lucius's choice: he can return to Jefferson, but he cannot yet abandon Boon, Ned, Lightning, and the unfinished consequences of the theft.

Chapter 12

Summary: The next morning Ned tells Lucius the hard news: Boon beat Butch, struck Everbe after believing she freed Lightning by submitting to Butch, and is now being taken to Hardwick jail with Miss Reba, Everbe, and the others under vagrancy or related charges. Uncle Parsham forces Ned to stop shielding Lucius, and Lucius rides with Ned toward town, confronts Boon in the Parsham hotel ladies' parlor, tries to hit his face while crying, and is steadied by Mr Poleymus. Poleymus gives Ned two dollars to bet, Ned buys peppermint drops and bananas, and Lucius returns to the track with Lightning, McWillie, Acheron, Lycurgus, and a plan that begins with the phrase "My name is Ned William McCaslin." In the first heat, Lucius holds Lightning back, confuses McWillie, scrapes Acheron, sends Acheron outside the rail, and triggers a dispute resolved by Colonel Linscomb, Mr van Tosch, and the judges by placing the heat in escrow; in the final heat, Ned uses sour dean, positions himself beyond the wire, and Lightning wins before Boss Priest appears with his gold-headed cane. Source anchors: Hardwick jail, Everbe, Mr Poleymus, two dollars, My name is Ned, Acheron outside rail, escrow, Boss Priest.

Analysis: Hardwick jail and Everbe's beating make Lucius's anger at Boon unavoidable, because loyalty to Boon now conflicts with the promise he accepted from Everbe. Mr Poleymus's two dollars, Ned's instruction, and "My name is Ned William McCaslin" show the boy acting inside Ned's improvisational intelligence rather than inside Boon's rage. The escrow ruling, Acheron outside the rail, sour dean, and Boss Priest's arrival bring every plot line together at the finish wire: legal ambiguity, gambling, animal training, family authority, and Lucius's first public success all arrive in the same moment.

Chapter 13

Summary: The final chapter sorts out the aftermath at Colonel Linscomb's place, where Miss Reba, Everbe, Minnie, Sam, and McWillie's father arrive in Boss Priest's automobile after a trip to Hardwick. Miss Reba reports that Boon attacked Butch again in jail after Butch called the women Memphis whores, Minnie displays her restored gold tooth, Sam gives Lucius Ned's winnings and Miss Reba's smaller roll, and Everbe explains that she thought she had no other way to free Lightning. Ned tells Lucius that Lycurgus recovered the tooth by putting Otis on a mule without saddle or bridle until he dropped it in his cap, then the office conference with Boss, Colonel Linscomb, and Mr van Tosch reveals that Lightning, properly Coppermine, runs for sardines; a private rematch proves he cannot beat Acheron without the trick, and Boss loses $496 rather than take the horse. Back in Jefferson, Father Maury prepares to whip Lucius, Boss stops him, teaches Lucius to live with what he has done, Ned sends gambling money to Uncle Parsham's church, and Everbe later names her baby Lucius Priest Hogganbeck. Source anchors: Colonel Linscomb, restored gold tooth, Lycurgus mule, Coppermine, sardines, $496, razor strop, Lucius Priest Hogganbeck.

Analysis: Colonel Linscomb's house lets Faulkner close the public plot with money, ownership, and explanation: Coppermine is no longer magical once the sardines are named. The restored gold tooth, Lycurgus's mule, and Ned's church donation show restitution occurring through comic but exact forms rather than through official punishment. Boss's refusal to let Maury reduce the trip to the razor strop completes the chapter's Moral Economy: Lucius must keep responsibility instead of buying expiation through pain, and Everbe's son Lucius Priest Hogganbeck turns that memory into kinship rather than merely disgrace.
