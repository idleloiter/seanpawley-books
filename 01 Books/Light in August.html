Detailed overview

William Faulkner builds Light in August by sending Lena Grove's patient road story into the already damaged social world of Jefferson, Mississippi. Lena leaves Doane's Mill in Alabama while pregnant, carrying a palm-leaf fan, a bandanna bundle, thirty-five cents, and a faith that Lucas Burch will be found at the planing mill. Her arrival is deliberately slow: Armstid, Martha Armstid, Varner's store, anonymous wagons, sardines, cheese, crackers, and the sight of smoke over Jefferson make her movement feel older than the particular scandal she carries. That movement becomes the novel's outer ring, a rural comic-pastoral rhythm that keeps returning after the violent plot of Joe Christmas seems to have swallowed the book.

The darker center is Joe Christmas, a man who cannot secure a socially recognized origin and is therefore made available for every local fantasy about race, sex, religion, and punishment. Faulkner does not present Christmas as a case history in a straight line. He lets Jefferson first see him as a rootless worker in city clothes, then as Joe Brown's bootlegging partner in a cabin on Joanna Burden's property, then as the suspected killer of Joanna, and only later as the orphanage child shaped by Doc Hines, the dietitian, McEachern, and Bobbie Allen. The fragmented order matters because the town's certainties about Christmas are already hardening before the reader has access to the childhood wounds behind his violence and self-division.

Byron Bunch is the book's hinge between Lena's road and Jefferson's catastrophe. He works six days a week at the planing mill, directs the rural church choir on Sundays, and tries to live invisibly until Lena's question about Lucas Burch pulls him into action. His love for Lena makes him both more generous and more foolish: he protects her from the immediate truth about Brown, finds her shelter near Reverend Gail Hightower, brings Mrs. Hines to assist with the birth, and eventually challenges Brown with a physical courage that does not fit his earlier life. Byron's plainness gives the book a moral scale different from Hightower's grand Confederate memories, McEachern's Calvinist violence, Percy Grimm's militarized certainty, or the town's appetite for spectacle.

Joanna Burden's house, body, and family history connect Jefferson to abolition, Reconstruction, and the unresolved violence that white townspeople prefer to treat as ancient contamination. Joanna is descended from New Englanders murdered over Black political rights, lives alone on the edge of town, sends money to Black schools and lawyers, and becomes sexually involved with Christmas before trying to force him into prayer, confession, and a Black college future. Her murder and the burning house become Jefferson's public event, but Faulkner has already shown that the town's response is less an investigation than a ritual of naming: Christmas becomes "Negro" when that word is needed to make the crime usable.

Reverend Hightower's isolation gives the novel another structure of time. He has lived for years under the memory of his grandfather's Civil War death, his disgraced wife's death in Memphis, and the congregation that expelled him. Byron keeps bringing the present to Hightower's door: Lena needs shelter, Christmas needs an alibi, Mrs. Hines needs help at the birth, and the town finally needs a place where Grimm can finish the manhunt. Hightower's late inward reckoning does not redeem Jefferson, but it gives Faulkner a way to stage the cost of refusing ordinary obligation in favor of inherited stories.

The last movement returns the novel to motion after Joanna's murder, Christmas's castration and death, and Hightower's collapse. Brown flees Lena again; Byron is beaten and then rides on with her; the furniture dealer's comic report places Lena, Byron, and the baby on another road toward Tennessee. That ending does not erase what happened in Jefferson. It sets Lena's durable, unembarrassed forwardness beside a town that has turned Christmas into a corpse, Joanna into a scandal, and Hightower into a failed witness. The book's design depends on that double exposure: a woman who keeps traveling in search of a family, and a community that keeps manufacturing victims to defend its stories about blood, race, sex, and God.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter 1

Summary: Lena Grove sits beside the road after nearly four weeks of walking from Doane's Mill, Alabama toward Jefferson, Mississippi in search of Lucas Burch. Faulkner gives her history with McKinley, the sawmill village, her brother's house, the leanto window, pregnancy, thirty-five cents, a palm-leaf fan, and the heavy shoes she carries more often than wears. Armstid and Winterbottom notice her pregnancy, Armstid gives her a ride, Martha Armstid breaks her rooster bank to send money, and Varner's store men tell Lena that the planing-mill worker may be named Bunch rather than Burch. The chapter ends with Lena riding the last twelve miles toward Jefferson, eating cheese, crackers, and sardines, feeling the child move, and seeing two smokes: the planing mill's coal stack and a burning house. Source anchors: Lena Grove; Doane's Mill; Lucas Burch; Armstid; Martha Armstid; Varner's store; sardines; burning house.

Analysis: Lena Grove, Doane's Mill, and Lucas Burch establish a search that is practical, bodily, and stubbornly hopeful before Jefferson's violence enters. Armstid, Martha Armstid, and Varner's store enact a Moral Economy of roadside obligation: strangers may judge Lena's pregnancy, but they still owe a traveler food, transport, money, and usable information. The sardines and burning house put that quiet reciprocity beside a town already organizing itself around Joanna Burden's fire.

Chapter 2

Summary: Byron Bunch remembers Joe Christmas arriving at the planing mill three years earlier in dusty but decent city clothes, with a straw hat, a tie, and a contemptuous look that angers the workers. Christmas takes work on the sawdust pile, remains apart, and later moves into a cabin on Joanna Burden's place with the talkative Joe Brown, who is really Lucas Burch. Byron's own habits are defined: six days at the mill, Sundays leading choir at a country church, and evenings with Reverend Hightower, the disgraced minister whose wife died after a Memphis scandal. When Lena reaches the mill asking for Lucas Burch, Byron recognizes the name problem, withholds the truth about Brown, and learns that the smoke is from Joanna Burden's burning house. Source anchors: Byron Bunch; Joe Christmas; planing mill; Joe Brown; Lucas Burch; Joanna Burden; Reverend Hightower; Memphis.

Analysis: Byron Bunch, Joe Christmas, and Joe Brown give the novel its first contest over Legibility: the mill reads Christmas as an unreadable outsider, while Brown's false name lets Lena search beside the man she cannot recognize. The planing mill and Joanna Burden place public labor beside hidden domestic scandal, while Reverend Hightower and Memphis introduce another identity fixed by town memory. Byron's silence about Lucas Burch protects Lena by manipulating what she can know.

Chapter 3

Summary: Chapter 3 turns to Reverend Gail Hightower's long disgrace in Jefferson and the way Byron has made him a listener despite the town's rejection. Hightower came to the Presbyterian church obsessed with his Confederate grandfather, who was killed in a cavalry raid near Jefferson, and he preached as if that inherited Civil War image mattered more than his living congregation. His wife, lonely and unstable, made repeated trips to Memphis, was finally found dead after a fall from a hotel window, and left Hightower exposed to public humiliation. The church forced him out, but he refused to leave town, living alone while Byron visits and now brings Lena's problem, Joe Brown's identity, and the Burden murder toward his door. Source anchors: Gail Hightower; Confederate grandfather; Presbyterian church; cavalry raid; Memphis hotel; Byron; Lena; Burden murder.

Analysis: Gail Hightower and the Confederate grandfather show Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation becoming pathological when an inherited cavalry story displaces present duties. The Memphis hotel and Presbyterian church fix his wife's death into a public disgrace that the town preserves through expulsion. Byron and Lena challenge Hightower's chosen deadness by forcing the keeper of a heroic family memory to answer ordinary living claims at his door.

Chapter 4

Summary: Faulkner returns to Joe Christmas and Joe Brown in the cabin on Joanna Burden's property, showing their bootlegging partnership and Brown's noisy dependence on Christmas's colder competence. Christmas moves around Jefferson without belonging to the mill workers, Brown, Joanna, or the Black neighborhoods he enters at night. Brown enjoys talk, alcohol, gambling, and display, while Christmas guards his money, his body, and his past with violent privacy. The chapter also tightens the public timeline around Joanna's murder: the burning house, the discovery of her body, Brown's eagerness for the thousand-dollar reward, and the first circulation of Christmas's possible Black ancestry. Source anchors: Joe Brown; bootlegging; cabin; Joanna Burden; thousand-dollar reward; Christmas; Black ancestry; Jefferson.

Analysis: Joe Brown, bootlegging, and the cabin show Christmas living in an illicit economy that depends on secrecy and distrust between partners. Joanna Burden and the thousand-dollar reward let Brown convert cowardice into civic performance. His claim of Black ancestry activates Language and Ideology because the racial label matters less as verified fact than as the phrase that translates an intimate murder into a public hunt Jefferson already knows how to conduct.

Chapter 5

Summary: Chapter 5 enters Joe Christmas's relation with Joanna Burden after he first comes to her house for food and later breaks in as if burglary, trespass, and courtship were indistinguishable to him. Joanna lives alone outside Jefferson, marked by her family's Northern abolitionist past and by local memory that treats the Burdens as alien even after generations in Mississippi. The sexual relation between Christmas and Joanna passes through secrecy, violence, dependence, and a strange domestic routine, while Christmas keeps testing whether she sees him as white, Black, lover, servant, or instrument. Joanna's family history includes murdered male kin and continuing commitments to Black schools and legal work, making her house a private archive of Reconstruction conflict. Source anchors: Joanna Burden; Burden house; abolitionist past; Black schools; Christmas; burglary; Reconstruction; Jefferson.

Analysis: Joanna Burden, the Burden house, and the abolitionist past make Christmas's affair part of Jefferson's racial memory rather than a private liaison. Black schools and Reconstruction explain why Joanna is already isolated before Christmas arrives; her politics have made her body and property symbolic to the town. Christmas's burglary-like approach to intimacy shows his inability to accept ordinary recognition, especially from a woman whose gaze he suspects may fix his race.

Chapter 6

Summary: The sixth chapter follows Christmas and Joanna deeper into the phases of their relationship, from secrecy and sexual compulsion into Joanna's religious and racial designs for him. Joanna begins to speak of prayer, repentance, Black destiny, and education, imagining Christmas as someone who should enter a Black college or take up work tied to her reform inheritance. Christmas resists the language she puts around him, fearing that her need to classify him repeats the orphanage, McEachern, and the town's logic in another form. Their final encounters include Joanna's menopause-haunted urgency, the pistol, prayer, and the movement toward the murder that Jefferson will later treat as proof of what Christmas was all along. Source anchors: Joanna; prayer; Black college; Christmas; menopause; pistol; repentance; murder.

Analysis: Joanna, prayer, and Black college show that benevolence can become coercive when it requires Christmas to accept a racial identity chosen by someone else. Menopause and the pistol make Joanna's demand bodily and immediate rather than abstract philanthropy. Christmas's violence grows from his refusal of the names pressed on him, but the murder also gives Jefferson exactly the story it wants about Joanna and a man it can call Black.

Chapter 7

Summary: Chapter 7 begins Christmas's childhood account at the orphanage, where he is called Joe and then marked by the possibility that he has Black blood. The dietitian's sexual encounter with a young doctor is accidentally witnessed by the child, and her fear of exposure leads her to project guilt and danger onto him. Doc Hines, working as the janitor, watches Christmas with fanatical attention, calls him an abomination, and helps set in motion the removal that will send him to the McEacherns. The chapter's key materials are toothpaste, the closet, the dietitian's panic, orphanage gossip, the word "nigger," and the bureaucratic ease with which adults move a child out of sight. Source anchors: orphanage; dietitian; Doc Hines; toothpaste; closet; McEachern; Black blood; janitor.

Analysis: The orphanage, dietitian, and closet show Christmas's identity being formed through adult fear before he can understand the charges around him. Doc Hines's claim of Black blood exposes Legibility as coercive classification: adults make an uncertain child administratively and morally readable by assigning him a race, then interpret every act through it. McEachern's arrival shows the institution solving its discomfort by transferring the child to a household where violence will be recorded as discipline.

Chapter 8

Summary: Christmas's life with Simon McEachern and Mrs. McEachern is defined through Calvinist punishment, farm labor, food, and secret resistance. McEachern beats scripture and obedience into the boy, demands memorization of the catechism, and treats the child as a will to be broken rather than a person to be known. Mrs. McEachern tries to offer food and tenderness, but Christmas rejects her gestures because accepting pity would place him in another form of dependence. The chapter follows small acts that become permanent habits: hiding food, enduring beatings, refusing prayer, learning secrecy, and discovering that the McEachern house cannot give him a name he can inhabit. Source anchors: Simon McEachern; Mrs. McEachern; catechism; farm labor; beatings; food; prayer; secrecy.

Analysis: Simon McEachern, catechism, and beatings make Religion and Legitimacy a domestic technology of domination: Calvinist authority lets the adoptive father rename violence as discipline and obedience as salvation. Mrs. McEachern's food complicates the house because kindness is present but unusable to a child trained to read every offer as control. Secrecy becomes Christmas's defense against a household whose sacred vocabulary legitimizes punishment.

Chapter 9

Summary: As an adolescent, Christmas discovers sex, money, and public shame through Bobbie Allen, the waitress and prostitute connected to a nearby dance hall. He lies to McEachern, steals or hoards coins, buys clothes, and enters a nighttime world of cars, drinking, music, and men who treat Bobbie as available in ways Christmas both desires and resents. McEachern follows him to the dance, denounces the scene, and Christmas kills or nearly kills him with a chair in the violent break from his foster father. Christmas then runs to Bobbie, expecting escape, but Bobbie, Max, and Mame reject and beat him, leaving him robbed of the romantic outlaw story he had imagined. Source anchors: Bobbie Allen; dance hall; McEachern; coins; chair; Max; Mame; escape.

Analysis: Bobbie Allen, the dance hall, and coins give Christmas an initiation into adult exchange where affection, sex, and money cannot be separated. McEachern and the chair turn filial rebellion into irreversible violence, making Christmas's escape from discipline also an entry into fugitive life. Max and Mame matter because they destroy his fantasy that Bobbie's world offers freedom; it only repeats exploitation in a different accent.

Chapter 10

Summary: Chapter 10 compresses Christmas's wandering after the McEachern break, following him through years of rootless work, sex, racial passing, and self-testing. He moves through towns and jobs, sometimes telling women that he is Black in order to provoke disgust, fascination, or punishment, and sometimes violently rejecting the consequences of the identity he has announced. His body becomes the place where he tests every social boundary he cannot settle by knowledge of ancestry. The chapter connects the orphanage suspicion, McEachern discipline, Bobbie's rejection, and later Jefferson life by showing Christmas repeatedly choosing situations where recognition turns into danger. Source anchors: wandering; racial passing; women; Black identity; McEachern; Bobbie; rootless work; danger.

Analysis: Wandering, racial passing, and women show Christmas trying to force the world to answer the question the orphanage and Doc Hines planted. McEachern and Bobbie remain active in these episodes because punishment and betrayal have become the forms of contact he knows best. Rootless work leads him toward Jefferson already trained to treat belonging as a trap and identity as something that can only be tested through injury.

Chapter 11

Summary: The narrative returns to the murder aftermath, with Christmas fleeing Jefferson while Brown tries to profit from information and the sheriff, townspeople, and posses translate rumor into pursuit. Christmas moves through the countryside, steals or begs food, and seems both desperate to escape and drawn toward capture. The town's language increasingly fixes him as Black, murderer, ravisher, and threat, even though much of what it knows comes from Brown's self-serving account. Faulkner uses roads, kitchens, fields, dogs, and armed men to show how quickly a private crime becomes a communal chase once race and reward money enter the story. Source anchors: Christmas fleeing; Brown; sheriff; posses; reward money; dogs; roads; race rumor.

Analysis: Christmas fleeing and the posses make the manhunt a problem of Information and Coordination rather than a neutral search for evidence. Brown and reward money corrupt the claim at its source, but racial rumor gives uncertain information enough emotional force to mobilize armed men. Roads, dogs, telephones, and sightings turn the landscape that carried Lena through reciprocal aid into a tracking grid that coordinates punishment faster than verification.

Chapter 12

Summary: Chapter 12 brings Doc Hines and Mrs. Hines into Jefferson after they hear of Christmas's arrest and recognize in him the lost child of their daughter Milly. Hines's backstory emerges: he hated Milly's lover because he believed the man had Black blood, killed or allowed the man to die, withheld help during Milly's childbirth, and abandoned the infant at the orphanage where he later worked as janitor. Mrs. Hines has lived for decades with grief, submission, and the hope of seeing the child, while Hines converts family crime into religious mission. Their arrival reframes Christmas not as a stranger without kin but as the product of a white family's racial fanaticism, secrecy, and murder. Source anchors: Doc Hines; Mrs. Hines; Milly; orphanage; janitor; Black blood; childbirth; Jefferson.

Analysis: Doc Hines, Milly, and Black blood reveal that Christmas's uncertainty was manufactured by family violence before it became town rumor. Mrs. Hines and childbirth add a buried maternal history that contrasts sharply with McEachern's and Hines's punitive religion. Jefferson now holds not just a suspect but the return of an abandoned infant whose life exposes the kinship between private patriarchy and public racial terror.

Chapter 13

Summary: Byron tries to manage the collision between Lena's childbirth, Brown's exposure, and the Hineses' demand to see Christmas. He arranges for Lena to stay in the cabin near Hightower, keeps Brown's identity from her as long as possible, and brings Mrs. Hines into the birth scene because she longs to assist the grandchild she lost through Milly's death. Hightower is drawn further into events as Byron asks him to help with Lena and later to provide a false alibi for Christmas. The chapter holds together birth, concealment, old grief, and impending execution: Lena's baby is arriving while Christmas's life is being narrowed by the jail, the sheriff, and the town's certainty. Source anchors: Byron; Lena's childbirth; Brown; Mrs. Hines; Hightower; false alibi; Milly; jail.

Analysis: Byron, Lena's childbirth, and Mrs. Hines make care a practical Moral Economy in a book crowded with failed fathers and punitive believers. Shelter, attendance, transport, and help during labor create obligations that neither marriage nor blood has formally assigned. Brown and the false alibi push Byron from kindness into risky intervention, while Hightower must perform ordinary service at the birth before anyone asks him for public courage.

Chapter 14

Summary: This chapter gives Joanna Burden's ancestry and Jefferson's memory of the Burden family in fuller historical depth. Her grandfather Calvin Burden and brother are tied to New England abolitionism, Black political rights, and Reconstruction conflict in Mississippi, and their deaths leave Joanna inheriting both property and a mission. The town continues to see her as foreign, unfeminine, and racially disloyal, even while accepting her physical presence as a long-standing local fact. Joanna's relation with Christmas is thereby framed by a family past of murdered reformers, Black schools, lawyers, letters, and an inherited obligation she tries to press onto a man who cannot bear any imposed role. Source anchors: Calvin Burden; New England; Reconstruction; Black political rights; Joanna; Black schools; lawyers; Jefferson memory.

Analysis: Calvin Burden, Reconstruction, and Black political rights make Joanna's isolation historically specific rather than eccentric. Black schools and lawyers explain why Jefferson treats the Burden house as a standing insult even before the murder. Joanna's attempt to assign Christmas a place inside that inherited mission repeats the Burden family's moral certainty, but Christmas receives it as another prison made from names.

Chapter 15

Summary: Christmas is captured in Mottstown after days of flight, and the town briefly treats him as a spectacle before Jefferson claims him. He appears strangely passive, eating and moving as if the pursuit has ended by some inward decision as much as by police work. In Mottstown, the Hineses identify him, and Doc Hines erupts with the old language of damnation while Mrs. Hines clings to recognition and loss. The sheriff returns Christmas to Jefferson through crowds already prepared to see him as the Black murderer of Joanna Burden, while Brown's role as informer remains shabby, frightened, and opportunistic. Source anchors: Mottstown; capture; Doc Hines; Mrs. Hines; sheriff; Jefferson crowds; Joanna Burden; Brown.

Analysis: Mottstown and capture show Christmas's flight ending in public display, not legal clarity. Doc Hines and Mrs. Hines split recognition into curse and mourning, giving Christmas a family history at the same moment the crowd erases him into a racial category. Brown and Jefferson crowds keep the justice process contaminated by cowardice and appetite for punishment.

Chapter 16

Summary: Byron brings Hightower the plan to save Christmas by claiming that Christmas was with him at the time Joanna was killed. Hightower resists, trapped between fear, habit, resentment, and his long withdrawal from Jefferson's public life. Byron's request forces Hightower to think again about his wife, his ministry, his grandfather's cavalry death, and the cost of having chosen memory over action. The chapter is built around persuasion and refusal rather than physical action, with Byron's plain urgency pressing against Hightower's self-protective eloquence. Source anchors: Byron; Hightower; false alibi; Joanna's death; wife; grandfather; ministry; refusal.

Analysis: Byron, Hightower, and the false alibi give the novel its clearest test of speech: whether a disgraced minister will tell a saving lie for a condemned man. Joanna's death and the wife are linked because Hightower's old scandal has trained him to avoid public entanglement. The grandfather and ministry show how his imagination has been occupied by dead riders while living people ask for help at his door.

Chapter 17

Summary: Lena gives birth in the cabin with Byron, Hightower, Mrs. Hines, and the absent-present Brown all bound to the event in different ways. Brown, exposed as Lucas Burch, comes near enough to see the baby and Lena but is unable to become the husband and father she has imagined. Byron tries to force responsibility onto him, while Lena remains oddly calm, interpreting events through the same patient confidence that carried her from Alabama. The chapter sets the baby's arrival against Christmas's imprisonment, making one cabin hold new life, evasion, old grandmotherly grief, and Byron's growing willingness to act beyond his timid habits. Source anchors: Lena; baby; cabin; Lucas Burch; Byron; Mrs. Hines; Alabama; Christmas imprisoned.

Analysis: Lena, baby, and cabin restore bodily continuity to a novel otherwise consumed by the Burden murder and the manhunt. Lucas Burch and Byron create rival models of male response: flight versus chosen obligation. Mrs. Hines and Christmas imprisoned bind the birth to the lost child plot, so Lena's delivery becomes both immediate relief and a painful echo of Milly's abandoned baby.

Chapter 18

Summary: Percy Grimm enters as the embodiment of young, organized, extralegal violence in Jefferson. He is associated with military discipline, patriotic clubs, uniforms, and the belief that public order requires decisive men who do not wait for ordinary law. As Christmas is moved through the legal system, Grimm attaches himself to the case with a self-authorized certainty that the older officials tolerate or fail to stop. His presence changes the manhunt from communal pursuit into a focused machinery of execution, bringing guns, cars, and ideological confidence to the town's racial panic. Source anchors: Percy Grimm; military discipline; patriotic clubs; uniforms; Christmas; Jefferson; guns; extralegal violence.

Analysis: Percy Grimm, uniforms, and patriotic clubs give Jefferson's old racial violence a modern administrative style. Christmas and guns make Grimm's certainty lethal because he treats due process as weakness after the accused has been racially named. The chapter exposes Order and Governance absorbing extralegal violence: officials do not formally sentence Christmas, but they equip, tolerate, and fail to restrain a man whose vigilantism speaks their language of patriotic order.

Chapter 19

Summary: Christmas escapes custody and runs to Hightower's house, where the possible false alibi and the minister's long avoidance converge too late. Hightower tries to claim that Christmas was with him, but Percy Grimm follows, enters the house, shoots Christmas, and castrates him in an act the narration presents as both murder and ritualized racial-sexual punishment. The town receives the death through report, horror, fascination, and relief, while Hightower is left with the collapse of his last chance to intervene effectively. Christmas's body becomes the final object onto which Jefferson writes the story it had prepared from Brown's accusation, Joanna's murder, and the rumor of Black blood. Source anchors: Christmas escape; Hightower's house; Percy Grimm; false alibi; shooting; castration; Black blood; Joanna.

Analysis: Christmas escape and Hightower's house bring the book's two withdrawn men into the same room at the decisive instant. Percy Grimm, shooting, and castration show that Jefferson's punishment is aimed not only at alleged murder but at the sexual and racial fear attached to Black blood. Joanna remains present because her death supplies the warrant through which the town accepts the mutilation of Christmas as closure.

Chapter 20

Summary: After Christmas's death, Hightower undergoes an inward reckoning in which memory, fantasy, guilt, and bodily decline crowd his room. He thinks of his grandfather's ride, his wife's Memphis death, the congregation, Byron, Lena's child, and the failed alibi, with the past no longer available as a heroic refuge. The chapter loosens chronological narration into a mental and spiritual accounting, forcing Hightower to see that his life in Jefferson has been organized around evasion as much as suffering. His collapse does not undo Grimm's act or save Christmas, but it breaks the protective story by which he had made himself the passive victim of other people's misunderstanding. Source anchors: Hightower; grandfather's ride; Memphis death; congregation; Byron; Lena's child; failed alibi; collapse.

Analysis: Hightower, the grandfather's ride, and the Memphis death expose the private myth that has governed his isolation. Byron and Lena's child are living claims he half answers and half fails. The failed alibi turns Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation into an ethical judgment: Hightower has preserved the cavalry story so completely that it consumed the attention needed for his wife, congregation, Christmas, and the people who brought present danger to his door.

Chapter 21

Summary: The final chapter returns to Lena, Byron, Brown, and the road through the comic report of a furniture dealer who has given Lena and Byron a ride. Brown flees yet again after Byron confronts him, and Byron is beaten but remains attached to Lena and the baby. Lena continues traveling, now with her child and Byron nearby, moving toward Tennessee with the same serene practicality that carried her from Alabama to Jefferson. The furniture dealer and his wife interpret the pair with amused curiosity, noting Byron's awkward devotion and Lena's unhurried certainty, while the novel ends not with the courthouse or the Burden house but with motion, talk, and another wagon road. Source anchors: furniture dealer; Lena; Byron; Brown flees; baby; Tennessee; Alabama; wagon road.

Analysis: The furniture dealer, Lena, and Byron shift the book out of Jefferson's murder story without pretending that story did not happen. Brown flees and the baby show Lena's family still incomplete by conventional standards, yet more viable than the households built by Hines, McEachern, Joanna, or Hightower. Tennessee, Alabama, and the wagon road make the ending a return to Lena's durable movement, the counterweight to the town's appetite for capture and punishment.

Editorial Apparatus

Summary: The extracted text includes the Vintage International and Library of America apparatus around the novel. The Publisher's Note says the edition follows the 1985 corrected text prepared under Noel Polk, using Faulkner's ribbon setting copy and comparison with holograph manuscript and carbon typescript, while Joseph Blotner prepared line and page notes. The back matter includes an Editors' Note explaining correction principles, a biographical sketch of William Faulkner from New Albany and Oxford through the RAF, New Orleans, Yoknapatawpha, Hollywood, the Nobel Prize, and his 1962 death, plus lists of works such as The Sound and the Fury, As I Lay Dying, Sanctuary, Absalom, Absalom!, and Go Down, Moses. The publisher material also advertises Vintage International and academic resources for educators. Source anchors: Noel Polk; ribbon setting copy; holograph manuscript; Joseph Blotner; New Albany; Oxford; Nobel Prize; Vintage International.

Analysis: Noel Polk, ribbon setting copy, and holograph manuscript matter because this edition foregrounds textual correction before the novel begins. Joseph Blotner, New Albany, and Oxford locate the apparatus in Faulkner scholarship rather than in the fictional Jefferson story itself. The Nobel Prize and Vintage International material should not be mistaken for novel chapters, but keeping them in this final note records why the extract's first manifest undercounted the real twenty-one-chapter structure.
