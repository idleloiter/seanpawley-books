Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep?

Position in the vault

This note is a standalone (non-series) novel note in the vault's science-fiction layer, one of two Philip K. Dick books present, the sibling being The Man In The High Castle. It belongs to the Science Fiction and Speculative Orders map and sits beside the vault's other meditations on artificial cognition, post-collapse society, and manufactured belief: Snow Crash, Accelerando, and Children of Time. Conceptually it is the vault's clearest statement of empathy-as-measurement, connecting the Legibility and Moral Economy clusters to the Ecological Constraint work that spans the nonfiction half of the vault. The extract (22 chapters) is fully present in 91 Extracted Text/Standalone/.

Detailed overview

Philip K. Dick builds Do Androids Dream of Electric Sheep? around Rick Deckard's single day of bounty hunting in post-World War Terminus San Francisco, but the chase plot is inseparable from a society that has externalized emotion into devices, status into animals, and religion into shared electronic suffering. The opening mood organ scene with Rick and Iran Deckard establishes a world where affect can be dialed, scheduled, and argued over. The empty apartment building where John R. Isidore lives establishes the other half of the world: dust, abandonment, Buster Friendly's television, and people classified as "specials" after radioactive damage.

The novel's central test is the Voigt-Kampff scale, which claims to distinguish humans from androids through involuntary empathy responses to animals and suffering. Deckard's work for Inspector Harry Bryant after Dave Holden is wounded sends him after Nexus-6 androids including Max Polokov, Luba Luft, Inspector Garland, Roy Baty, Irmgard Baty, and Pris Stratton. The test is bureaucratic and physiological, but Dick keeps making its moral authority unstable: Luba Luft's singing, Rachael Rosen's seduction, Phil Resch's cold competence, and Deckard's own exhaustion all make empathy look less like a species property than a fragile practice.

Animals are the book's public religion and private economy. Deckard wants a real animal because his electric sheep humiliates him; pet shops, price tags, Sidney's catalog, the ostrich from Cleveland, the goat bought after bounty payments, and the final toad all convert ecological extinction into social obligation. Owning and caring for an animal is a way to prove participation in Mercerist empathy, but the market turns that proof into another status hierarchy.

Mercerism and Buster Friendly provide rival mass realities. Wilbur Mercer offers shared suffering through the empathy box, a climb up a hill under stones, and a communal ritual that connects isolated humans. Buster Friendly offers continuous media chatter and eventually exposes Mercerism as staged with actors and sets. The exposure does not destroy Mercer's power for Deckard because Dick's question is not whether a mediated reality is factually pure; it is whether it can still sustain mercy, attention, and restraint in a world where extinction and imitation have made authenticity hard to locate.

The ending refuses a clean moral victory. Deckard retires the remaining androids, becomes the most successful bounty hunter of his day, loses the goat when Rachael kills it, wanders into the wasteland, experiences Mercer, finds a toad that turns out to be electric, and returns to Iran. The artificial toad does not cancel the need to care for it. That is the novel's harshest and most humane turn: in a world of fakes, programmed moods, synthetic animals, and android people, the capacity to care for an imitation may be the last surviving form of the thing everyone claims to measure.

Primary characters and motivations

  • Rick Deckard: bounty hunter for the San Francisco Police Department, assigned Dave Holden's Nexus-6 list after Holden is lasered. He wants a real animal (first a sheep, then an ostrich, then a goat) as outward proof of empathy and status. Across twenty-four hours he retires six Nexus-6 androids, is bested by the Rosen Association's corporate maneuvering and by Rachael's seduction, begins to empathize with androids, loses the goat, fuses permanently with Wilbur Mercer on a lone hillside, and ends the book able to care for an electric toad. His arc is from confident hunter to self-diagnosed "scourge" who can no longer kill.
  • Iran Deckard: Rick's wife, who schedules a six-hour self-accusatory depression twice a month and calls him "a murderer hired by the cops." She wants security and a real animal, mistrusts the emotional safety of the mood organ, and is the book's counterweight to Rick's hunting. She ends by revealing the toad is electric and, unasked, ordering artificial flies to care for it.
  • Rachael Rosen: a Nexus-6 android and "niece" of Eldon Rosen, used by the Rosen Association as a sales device and as a weapon that discredits the Voigt-Kampff test (by failing it) and neutralizes bounty hunters (by seducing them). She speaks for android self-awareness — the two-year lifespan, "we are machines stamped out like bottle caps" — and her arc is the novel's trap: she initiates the affair, then kills Rick's goat in retaliation, then offers him the oyster-shaped catalepsy device to survive Roy Baty.
  • Eldon Rosen: head of the Rosen Association, the system's largest manufacturer of androids for the colonization program. He engineers the Seattle setup where Rachael (programmed with false memories) is tested first, voiding the Voigt-Kampff's authority, then offers Deckard an owl and threats. His motivation is regulatory survival for the Nexus-6 line.
  • John R. Isidore: a "special" (chickenhead) living alone in a kippleized, depopulated apartment building in the peninsula suburbs south of San Francisco, who drives a truck for the false-animal repair firm Van Ness Pet Hospital under Hannibal Sloat. A devout Mercerite, he takes in Pris Stratton and then Roy and Irmgard Baty because being needed makes him feel alive; his arc runs from loneliness to betrayal (the spider mutilation, Buster's expose of Mercer) to grief and leaving the building.
  • Wilbur Mercer (and Mercerism): the archetypal robed figure of the empathy box who climbs a hillside under thrown stones; the box fuses everyone who holds the handles into a shared mental climb. Buster Friendly's expose reveals him as retired bit player Al Jarry on a sound stage (leaked by android friends), yet Mercer keeps appearing — to Isidore after the spider, and to Deckard — because his function is shared suffering, not factual existence.
  • Phil Resch: bounty hunter of the android-run Mission Street "police" station, coldly efficient and visibly enjoying the kills. He is the novel's control case: he passes the Voigt-Kampff, owns a real squirrel (Buffy), and yet kills with pleasure — proof that empathy can fail in a human and that the test measures a thin slice of humanity.
  • The androids (Max Polokov, Luba Luft, Inspector Garland, Roy Baty, Irmgard Baty, Pris Stratton): escaped Nexus-6s from Mars fleeing synthetic servitude. Polokov poses as a Soviet officer; Luba Luft is an opera singer in The Magic Flute; Garland runs a counterfeit police institution; Roy Baty is the "natural leader" who tried to build an android version of Mercerism and engineered the escape; Irmgard urges trust in Isidore; Pris Stratton, a duplicate of Rachael's type, loves pre-colonial fiction and maims the spider. Together they make the book's case that intelligence and artistry can coexist with the "coldness" the test is calibrated to catch.

Themes and concepts to track

  • Empathy measured versus empathy practiced: the Voigt-Kampff test claims to detect it physiologically while the book shows it failing on Rachael, nearly failing to explain Phil Resch, and surviving only as unmeasured care (the toad).
  • Authenticity and imitation: real versus electric animals, organic androids versus false memory, caring for an ersatz sheep as a moral act.
  • The economic valuation of life and affect: Sidney's catalogue prices, the ostrich at $30,000, bounty at $1,000 a head, mood settings as commodities, Rachel as a product the company "stamps out."
  • Faith and fraud in a media age: Mercerism versus Buster Friendly's expose, belief surviving the revelation of manufacture.
  • Ecological collapse and social sorting: World War Terminus dust, specials and "emigrate or degenerate," kipple as entropy made social.
  • Identity, memory, and legibility: phone calls routed nowhere, false identities, cephalic patterns, android doubling (Rachael/Pris), the parallel Mission Street police.
  • The android as mirror: Dick never lets the reader finally separate the cold human from the warm android.

Core concepts

  • Legibility: the Voigt-Kampff scale turns empathy into a bureaucratic classification tool whose authority the novel repeatedly destabilizes.
  • Ecological Constraint: radioactive dust and animal extinction reorganize status, migration, labor, and moral obligation.
  • Technological Change: mood organs, empathy boxes, androids, and artificial animals make emotion, identity, and care technologically mediated.
  • Moral Economy: animal ownership and the electric toad test whether care depends on authenticity, status, or sustained obligation.
  • Artificial Intelligence: the Nexus-6 is the vault's benchmark android — organic, intelligent beyond many humans, but built without empathy — and the book asks whether such a mind can be art, love, or prey.
  • Religion and Legitimacy: Mercerism is a mediated religion whose factual fraud (Al Jarry, the sound stage) does not cancel its legitimating power over conscience.
  • Commercial Society: the Rosen Association, Sidney's catalogue, and the bounty contract turn people, androids, and animals into fungible commodities.
  • Intelligence: Dick contrasts the Nexus-6, which "evolved beyond" many humans, with the intelligence tests that stopped catching androids a decade earlier.
  • Collapse and Resilience: World War Terminus and kipple are the entropic ground against which every gesture of care is measured.
  • Language and Ideology: Buster Friendly's chatter, emigration propaganda, and the expose show media manufacturing consensus and doubt.
  • Information Theory: tests, false memories, trapped vidphone lines, and dossiers turn identity into a signal-detection problem the book keeps failing.

Chapter-by-chapter notes

Chapter 1

Summary: Rick Deckard wakes in multicolored pajamas from a Penfield mood organ setting while Iran resists being programmed into cheerfulness. Their argument covers scheduled moods, depression, Buster Friendly and His Friendly Friends, Mercerism, the emptiness of Earth, Rick's electric sheep, the neighbor Barbour's real horse, and Rick's desire for an authentic animal as proof of status and empathy.
Analysis: Rick Deckard, Iran, and Penfield introduce Technological Change through manufactured emotion: mood becomes a setting spouses can schedule and contest. Buster Friendly and Mercerism establish competing systems for filling spiritual and social emptiness, already framing the novel's Religion and Legitimacy problem. The electric sheep matters because Rick's shame over an artificial animal ties private marriage, public status, and the novel's title question together from the first chapter — the animal economy of Sidney's prices is Commercial Society from page one.
Source anchors: Rick Deckard; Iran; Penfield; Buster Friendly; Mercerism; electric sheep.

Chapter 2

Summary: Chapter 2 moves to John R. Isidore's decaying apartment building in the depopulated suburbs of San Francisco after World War Terminus. A television advertises emigration, Mars, synthetic servants, New America, and Interspace Horizons to an almost empty room, while Isidore, a "special" damaged by radioactive dust, listens to Buster Friendly, thinks about Wilbur Mercer, and remains on Earth among kipple, silence, and abandoned apartments.
Analysis: John Isidore and World War Terminus make Ecological Constraint visible in an emptied building rather than a battlefield, and kipple is the book's plainest figure for Collapse and Resilience as entropy made domestic. Mars and Interspace Horizons show the official escape route sold by Language and Ideology, but special status traps Isidore on radioactive Earth. The empathy-box fusion with Wilbur Mercer gives Isidore his one counter-current to the kipple — an early sketch of Religion and Legitimacy rooted in shared ascetic suffering.
Source anchors: John Isidore; World War Terminus; San Francisco; Mars; Interspace Horizons; special.

Chapter 3

Summary: Rick stops at animal row to look at an ostrich from Cleveland, the only ostrich on the West Coast, before reporting late to the Hall of Justice on Lombard Street. Inspector Harry Bryant tells him that Dave Holden has been hospitalized at Mount Zion after encountering Nexus-6 androids, gives him notes on escaped models from the Rosen Association, and sends him toward Seattle to verify the Voigt-Kampff test against the new brain unit.
Analysis: Animal row and the ostrich show how extinction has made animals luxury objects negotiated on time-payment contracts — Commercial Society before the detective plot begins. Harry Bryant and Dave Holden introduce institutional danger: the androids can defeat experienced bounty hunters. Nexus-6 matters because the improved brain unit makes the testing tool the only legal and moral anchor left, and Dick's digression on why empathy marks "herd animal" humans rather than solitary "predator" androids already undercuts the clean categories of Artificial Intelligence.
Source anchors: animal row; ostrich; Harry Bryant; Dave Holden; Mount Zion; Nexus-6.

Chapter 4

Summary: Bryant briefs Rick on the remaining six Nexus-6 androids in Northern California after Holden retired two and was lasered by Max Polokov. The chapter focuses on the Voigt-Kampff Altered Scale, the Seattle trip to the Rosen Association, doubts about whether the test still works, a Soviet W.P.O. memo on Leningrad psychiatrists who fear schizoid humans would fail it, and the list of targets including Polokov, Luba Luft, Garland, and the Batys.
Analysis: Bryant and Max Polokov turn Rick's promotion into a mortality test rather than a career opportunity. Voigt-Kampff and Rosen Association make Legibility a legal hinge: if the classification tool fails, bounty hunting becomes murder. The Leningrad study on flattened affect, and the note that Nexus-6 types "surpassed several classes of human specials" in Intelligence, plant the book's doubt that the human/android line is where the test's red needles actually fall.
Source anchors: Bryant; Max Polokov; Voigt-Kampff; Seattle; Rosen Association; Northern California.

Chapter 5

Summary: Rick administers the Voigt-Kampff test to Rachael Rosen while a white light, wire-mesh disc, gauges, and questions about calfskin wallets, raw oysters, and animal cruelty measure her involuntary reactions. Rachael and the Rosen Association try to discredit the test by presenting her as human, but Rick concludes she is an android, exposing corporate manipulation around Nexus-6 production, Seattle wealth, and synthetic empathy.
Analysis: Rachael Rosen and Voigt-Kampff make Legibility measurable, adversarial, and commercially contested. The calfskin wallet question reveals why animals are central to the test: empathy toward nonhuman suffering becomes the marker of humanity. Rosen Association and Nexus-6 matter because the corporation's interest is not truth but regulatory survival — Rachael is the product, the test subjects include humans, and the owl is offered as a bribe, all of it Commercial Society and Artificial Intelligence colliding at once.
Source anchors: Rachael Rosen; Voigt-Kampff; calfskin wallet; Rosen Association; Nexus-6; gauges.

Chapter 6

Summary: Isidore hears Buster Friendly's weather chatter about fallout, the Eastern seaboard, Mongoose satellite reports, and a coming expose, then knocks on the door of a frightened new neighbor. Pris Stratton cuts off the TV, hides in the abandoned building, and cautiously meets Isidore, who offers help despite his awkwardness, loneliness, and reverence for both Buster Friendly and Wilbur Mercer.
Analysis: Buster Friendly and the Mongoose satellite show Language and Ideology normalizing radioactive danger as entertainment, turning fallout into chatter between jokes and teasers. Isidore and Pris Stratton begin the novel's second refuge plot, where android fear depends on a human "special" for shelter. Wilbur Mercer matters because Isidore's kindness is shaped by a religion the androids will later mock and test, and because the First Law of Kipple already frames fusion as the one force that holds out against Collapse and Resilience; his isolation makes him more receptive to any visitor who seems to need him.
Source anchors: Buster Friendly; Mongoose satellite; Isidore; Pris Stratton; fallout; Wilbur Mercer.

Chapter 7

Summary: Isidore thinks about cooking for Pris, pre-war vegetables, margarine, his job at Van Ness Pet Hospital, Mr. Hannibal Sloat, Milt Borogrove, artificial-animal repair, Buster Friendly, Wilbur Mercer, and his desire to be useful to someone. The chapter explains his work world of fake animal maintenance: an electric cat dies on the truck and turns out — horrifically — to have been real, and Isidore is forced to tell Mrs Pilsen her cat Horace is dead while Pris's ignorance of ordinary media and customs marks her as both vulnerable and suspicious.
Analysis: Pris and pre-war vegetables show Isidore translating care into food and domestic fantasy. Van Ness Pet Hospital, Hannibal Sloat, and artificial animals reveal Commodity Systems beneath the animal-status economy: extinction creates a market for synthetic substitutes and the repair work that sustains them, including an insurance payout and a proposed exact electric duplicate of Horace. Sloat's claim that "Buster is immortal, like Mercer" anticipates the chapter's darker argument — that the counterfeits and the faith are run by the same commercial machinery, a thread for Religion and Legitimacy and Commercial Society alike.
Source anchors: Pris; pre-war vegetables; Van Ness Pet Hospital; Hannibal Sloat; Milt Borogrove; artificial animals.

Chapter 8

Summary: Rick returns to the San Francisco Hall of Justice, where Bryant sends him after Max Polokov before he can consult Holden. Polokov has taken a city trash-collector job, a Soviet officer named Sandor Kadalyi appears through Aeroflot and opera contacts, and Rick navigates false identities, the Nexus-6 list, Seattle test results, and the immediate danger of an android who already wounded Dave.
Analysis: Hall of Justice and Bryant restore the bureaucratic pace after the Rosen test, but the pace is deceptive because Rick is being sent at once against the android who wounded Holden. Max Polokov and the trash-collector cover show the androids hiding inside civic labor rather than science-fiction spectacle, turning the scavenger business itself into a symptom of Collapse and Resilience. Sandor Kadalyi and Aeroflot matter because identity in this chapter is purely tactical — names, accents, jobs, and diplomatic covers can all be masks read only through Legibility, and Rick's survival depends on reading the mask before it shoots.
Source anchors: Hall of Justice; Bryant; Max Polokov; trash collector; Sandor Kadalyi; Aeroflot.

Chapter 9

Summary: Rick finds Luba Luft at an opera-house rehearsal of Mozart's The Magic Flute, where Papageno, Pamina, German lyrics, the chorus, bird-feather costume, magic bells, and the beauty of music complicate his readiness to retire her. Luba asks about art, the Rosen Association, Nexus-6 status, and why he is questioning her, while Rick reflects that no magic bells exist to make enemies vanish without effort and feels the opera's human beauty pressing against his job. A hard-nosed "harness bull" called in by Luba ends the test.
Analysis: Luba Luft and The Magic Flute create the novel's strongest challenge to Rick's category of android as mere target. Mozart, Papageno, and Pamina give Luba a relation to beauty that the Voigt-Kampff framework cannot easily process, and Rick's meditation on Mozart's pauper's grave makes his own work part of "the form-destroying process of entropy." The opera house matters because Deckard's work intrudes into art, forcing him into Moral Economy and Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy as Artificial Intelligence crosses into the vocation of singing.
Source anchors: Luba Luft; The Magic Flute; Mozart; Papageno; Pamina; opera house.

Chapter 10

Summary: Rick is arrested at the unfamiliar Mission Street Hall of Justice by Officer Crams for representing himself as a peace officer. The building, booking process, cephalic identification, George Gleason, Phil Resch, Inspector Garland, Miss Luft's Voigt-Kampff results, and an alternate police bureaucracy suggest that androids have built or infiltrated an entire fake law-enforcement structure in San Francisco.
Analysis: Mission Street and Officer Crams destabilize Rick's trust in institutions as sharply as Rachael destabilized the test. Phil Resch and Inspector Garland mirror bounty hunting through android-controlled bureaucracy, an exact inversion of the reader's — and Rick's — earlier assumptions. Cephalic pattern turns Legibility against Rick because identity verification becomes part of the trap rather than a route back to certainty, and the two parallel police agencies make the chapter the book's strongest argument that the apparatus of classification is itself a manufactured object.
Source anchors: Mission Street; Officer Crams; Phil Resch; Inspector Garland; George Gleason; cephalic pattern.

Chapter 11

Summary: Inspector Garland warns Phil Resch that test results will disturb him, fetches a laser tube, admits knowledge of Polokov, and confronts Rick inside the false department. Resch retrieves Boneli gear for a Bond Reflex-Arc Test, Officer Crams remains part of the apparatus, and Rick realizes Garland is an android while Resch's own status becomes uncertain to both men. Resch kills Garland in the office — an android-against-android kill — and the two bounty hunters flee toward the opera house.
Analysis: Inspector Garland and the laser tube turn the false police station into a direct assassination threat, and Garland's tirade about being "not even considered animals" hands the book's sympathies to the hunted. Phil Resch and Bond Reflex-Arc matter because the hunter may not know whether he is human — Garland describes the synthetic-memory system, making Resch's biography a manufactured Legibility problem rather than a fact. The counterfeit institution, the planted memories, and the dossier that identifies Garland "inaccurately" as an insurance underwriter all hook the Artificial Intelligence theme to Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation.
Source anchors: Inspector Garland; Phil Resch; laser tube; Polokov; Bond Reflex-Arc; Officer Crams.

Chapter 12

Summary: Rick and Phil Resch pursue Luba Luft from the opera house to a museum exhibit of Edvard Munch, where she studies paintings and woodcuts among schoolchildren. Rick buys her a print before Resch kills her, and the episode forces Rick to compare Resch's eagerness, his own attraction to Luba, Rachael Rosen, the Voigt-Kampff test, and the possibility that empathy can fail in humans as well as androids.
Analysis: Luba Luft and Edvard Munch deepen the opera chapter's problem by putting android life beside human art and children — the screaming isolate of The Scream is read by Resch as "how an andy must feel." Phil Resch and Voigt-Kampff expose a Moral Economy failure inside correct classification: Resch may be human, but his pleasure in killing looks less humane than Rick's hesitation, and Rick's own needles spike at "a female android." Rachael Rosen matters because Luba's death prepares Rick for the sexual and moral trap Rachael later sets, and the museum is the site where Culture, Identity, and Legitimacy collides with the hunter's arithmetic.
Source anchors: Luba Luft; Phil Resch; Edvard Munch; museum; Rachael Rosen; Voigt-Kampff.

Chapter 13

Summary: Isidore drives home through the late-afternoon sky with black-market groceries: bean curd, ripe peaches, cheese, and Chablis bought with money borrowed from Mr. Sloat. He returns to the kipple-filled building hoping Pris is still there, thinking about Buster Friendly, the TV, his repaired car, Bank of America, and the possibility that food and company might make him valuable. Pris tells him about Mars, about Roy Baty's drugstore, and about returning to Earth because Mars was "not conceived for habitation."
Analysis: Isidore and black-market groceries make care material and costly in a world of scarcity — a gift economy of one running against the rationing of Moral Economy. Bean curd, peaches, and Chablis show that he is courting connection through rare food rather than ideology, the whole transaction routed through the parallel economy of Illicit Markets. Mr Sloat matters because Isidore's generosity is financed by the workplace that repairs fake animals, tying his emotional need back to the artificial-animal economy.
Source anchors: Isidore; black-market groceries; bean curd; peaches; Chablis; Mr Sloat.

Chapter 14

Summary: Roy Baty and Irmgard Baty join Pris in Isidore's apartment, where the androids eat peaches, discuss Mars, Polokov, Garland, Anders, Gitchel, Luba Luft, Dave Holden, and the danger of bounty hunters. Pris presents Isidore as someone taking care of her, Irmgard gives him warmth, Roy asserts control, and the remaining fugitives decide how much they can trust the "special" who now knows them, installing an alarm and rigging electronic defenses.
Analysis: Roy Baty, Irmgard Baty, and Pris form the android household that mirrors Rick and Iran's damaged domesticity. Isidore and Mars make Coercive Labor central to the fugitives' story: the androids flee synthetic servitude rather than arrive as pure malice, and Pris's talk of smuggling pre-colonial fiction from libraries to Mars shows how badly they want a past and a world that were never theirs. Luba Luft matters because her death becomes evidence in their tactical debate about whether contact with humans is always fatal, and Roy's alarm device — tripped by any extra "mentational entity" — is the technology of the hunted answering the state's technology of hunting.
Source anchors: Roy Baty; Irmgard Baty; Pris; Isidore; Mars; Luba Luft.

Chapter 15

Summary: The androids vote on whether to stay in Isidore's apartment or kill him and flee. Irmgard argues to remain, Roy Baty proposes killing Isidore, and Pris says his value outweighs the danger because Polokov, Garland, Luba, and Anders died after being discovered among humans. The chapter also brings Rick's approaching hunt, Mercerism, and Isidore's desperate hope of belonging into one room, then cuts to Rick spending his bounty money on a black Nubian goat and confessing to Iran that he has begun to empathize with androids.
Analysis: The android vote and Roy Baty show the fugitives making collective survival decisions with cold clarity, while Irmgard and Pris complicate that coldness by disagreeing over Isidore's usefulness — the fugitives acting in Coercive Labor's shadow, fleeing servitude at the cost of any legal place on Earth. The goat purchase inverts the scene: Rick buys a real animal "to get my confidence back" after Phil Resch, and Mercer tells him over the empathy box that he "will be required to do wrong no matter where you go." Together these make the chapter a hinge of Moral Economy and Religion and Legitimacy: value is being assigned both to the goat and to the retirements by a man who no longer trusts the market or the machine that priced them.
Source anchors: android vote; Roy Baty; Irmgard; Pris; Polokov; Mercerism; goat.

Chapter 16

Summary: Rick sits in a sumptuous hotel room reading carbon sheets on Roy and Irmgard Baty, including fuzzy 3-D telescopic snapshots and details of Roy's Mars cover as a pharmacist or laborer with mystical preoccupations. He wonders whether androids dream, compares their escape from barren servitude to Luba Luft's opera singing, calls Rachael Rosen, drinks bourbon, and prepares to hunt Pris, Roy, and Irmgard in San Francisco.
Analysis: Hotel room and bourbon show Rick using money and intoxication to prepare for killing after Luba's death. Roy Baty and Irmgard Baty become dossiers before they become bodies — the state's Legibility machinery compressing lives into typewritten sheets — and the account of Roy's stolen mind-fusing drugs, "hoping to promote in androids a group experience similar to that of Mercerism," makes the android leader's tragedy explicit within Artificial Intelligence. Rachael's declaration that "there goes I" at the thought of Pris, a stamped-out copy of her own type, and her talk of zygote-bath DNS and the coming Nexus-7, turn the chapter into an inside view of the manufacturer — Commercial Society where the goods doubt their own existence.
Source anchors: hotel room; Roy Baty; Irmgard Baty; Mars; Rachael Rosen; bourbon.

Chapter 17

Summary: After sex with Rachael, Rick drinks room-service coffee while she showers and explains that androids cannot control their physical sensual passions. She reveals she has slept with other bounty hunters, says the experience often prevents them from killing Nexus-6 targets, discusses the Batys and Pris, and attacks Rick's fantasy of owning animals by telling him he will not be able to keep his new goat safe from her.
Analysis: Rachael and room-service coffee make the seduction feel banal after its moral shock, and her confession that the association uses her to "reach the bounty hunters" is the book's clearest instance of intimacy weaponized in Moral Economy. Her account strips the encounter down to chemistry — the two-year lifespan, "we're not born... we wear out like ants" — and leaves Rick outside every legal category he uses for her, a problem of Legibility made personal. The goat matters because she locates his emotional vulnerability precisely: his desire for animal care is the part of him she can punish even if she cannot stop every retirement.
Source anchors: Rachael; room-service coffee; bounty hunters; Nexus-6; Batys; goat.

Chapter 18

Summary: Pris orders Isidore to bring up his TV so the androids can watch Buster Friendly's long-awaited announcement, and Irmgard agrees that they need the set. Roy Baty sits heavily in the room while Isidore, thrilled to be useful, retrieves the television, thinks of Buster Friendly and Friendly Friends, and prepares for the expose that will attack Wilbur Mercer and the empathy-box religion he cherishes. On the way he captures a spider, and Pris, bored with the broadcast, systematically cuts off its legs while Buster reveals that Mercer is a fraud.
Analysis: Pris and the TV set show the androids using Isidore's hospitality to undermine his faith, and Buster Friendly's expose — the painted sky, the sound stage, retired bit player Al Jarry, the "rocks" of soft plastic — is Language and Ideology at full power, converting a shared religion into a "swindle." Roy Baty's pleasure confuses the point: the expose is true by its own research methods and yet changes nothing for the believers, which is the chapter's central claim about Religion and Legitimacy. The spider mutilation, set against "the last living spider on Earth," and the ledger of false memory, publicity stills, and planted testimony both belong to Memory, Narrative, and Knowledge Preservation.
Source anchors: Pris; Isidore; TV set; Buster Friendly; Roy Baty; Irmgard; spider.

Chapter 19

Summary: Isidore grips the empathy box as the lights go out and the androids panic over Rick's possible arrival. Irmgard digs her nails into him, Pris tells him not to let the bounty hunter in, Roy Baty waits inside, Mercer remains present through the box, and Isidore is ordered to show identification, demand a warrant, and lie that no one else is in the apartment when Rick Deckard from the San Francisco Police Department's special deceives and kills Pris Stratton outside, then the Batys inside.
Analysis: The empathy box and Isidore put Mercerist vulnerability at the center of the final hunt, and Mercer's appearance at the stairs — warning Rick which android to retire first, then vanishing — is the book's proof that Mercer survives his own debunking as a functioning intelligence of Religion and Legitimacy. Pris arrives as a double of Rachael, so that Rick's killing of her is also a killing of the woman he slept with, collapsing the Artificial Intelligence category into the personal. Irmgard, Pris, and Roy Baty rely on Isidore's legal existence as a shield while holding his emotional dependence hostage, and Rick's impersonation of the stammering Isidore is Legibility as deceit: the masks have moved from the androids to the hunter.
Source anchors: empathy box; Isidore; Irmgard; Pris; Roy Baty; Rick Deckard; Mercer.

Chapter 20

Summary: After retiring Pris, Irmgard, and Roy Baty, Rick reports to Harry Bryant, who offers rest and a patrol car for the three bodies. Rick tells Isidore that Roy mistook him for the special at the door, advises him to leave the apartment, reflects that he is a scourge like famine or plague, thinks of Mercer, Iran, Barbour, Rachael, and the goat, and returns home to learn from Iran that Rachael killed the goat by pushing it off the roof.
Analysis: Harry Bryant and three bodies reduce Rick's climactic work to cleanup procedure, and Roy Baty and Isidore make the victory morally sick, because survival depended on confusing the android about which vulnerable man was at the door. The death of the goat — Rachael's answer, "she had what seemed to her a reason," an "android reason" — turns the animal into the casualty of the affair Rick chose, a final accounting in Moral Economy against Commercial Society's promise that status could be bought. Iran's "curse," Rick's "I'm a scourge like famine or plague," and his verdict that "everything is true. Everything anybody has ever thought" keep the private and the cosmic versions of Religion and Legitimacy fused.
Source anchors: Harry Bryant; three bodies; Roy Baty; Isidore; Mercer; goat; Iran.

Chapter 21

Summary: Rick flies into the early morning wasteland, seeing gray refuse-littered ground, house-sized pebbles, dead former farmland, and the remains of a world where crops and animals once lived. Exhausted after retiring six Nexus-6 androids in twenty-four hours, he thinks of Dave Holden at Mount Zion, Harry Bryant, Dr. Johnson snuff, Mercer, and the possibility that nothing he has done has restored meaning; he climbs alone, is struck by a real stone, cannot reach Holden or Bryant, and tells Miss Marsten he has "permanently fused" with Mercer.
Analysis: Wasteland and six Nexus-6 turn Rick's triumph into emptiness, and the dead landscape is the novel's rawest statement of Ecological Constraint and Collapse and Resilience — "Once crops grew here and animals grazed." Dave Holden and Mount Zion remind him that the job began as replacement for another injured hunter, not as heroic destiny. Mercer matters because Rick's landscape becomes Mercer's hill: the religious image of suffering is no longer mediated by the empathy box but encountered alone, as direct fusion — "I did it alone" — which is the chapter's whole measure of what Religion and Legitimacy costs when it stops being shared.
Source anchors: wasteland; six Nexus-6; Dave Holden; Mount Zion; Harry Bryant; Mercer.

Chapter 22

Summary: Rick finds what appears to be an extinct toad, checks Sidney's catalog under Bufonidae, remembers that toads were precious to Wilbur Mercer, boxes it, and brings it home to Iran. Iran discovers the control panel showing it is electric, but she orders electric flies and prepares to care for it, while Rick rests beside the Penfield mood organ and the artificial animal becomes a final test of whether care depends on authenticity.
Analysis: Toad and Sidney's give Rick one last chance to believe in miraculous recovery of the natural world, and the Bufonidae listing's "E" for extinct, plus the memory that toads were "most precious" to Mercer, connect the animal to Mercerism's most sacred imagery — making the disappointment sharper when it proves artificial. Iran's decision to order "one pound of artificial flies" and her insistence that "electric things have their lives, too" resolve the novel's Moral Economy argument by shifting value from authenticity to care: an electric toad still requires attention. In that gesture the artificial animal is redeemed as an object of Technological Change, and the wasteland that should have held only Ecological Constraint becomes, for a man who can no longer kill, a place where care for the imitation is the only real thing left.
Source anchors: toad; Sidney's; Bufonidae; Wilbur Mercer; Iran; electric flies.

Useful details and retrieval cues

  • Dedication and epigraph: "To Maren Augusta Bergrud / August 10, 1923 - June 14, 1967," followed by the Yeats lines "And still I dream he treads the lawn, walking ghostly in the dew," and the Reuters 1966 item about Tu'imalila, Captain Cook's turtle, kept by the Tongan court and mourned at Nuku-alofa — the book's own epigraph for extinction.
  • Penfield mood organ settings: 3 (desire to do something), 481 (awareness of manifold possibilities, "new hope"), 594 (pleased acknowledgement of husband's superior wisdom), 670 (long deserved peace), 888 (desire to watch TV no matter what's on it); Iranian Iran schedules a six-hour "self-accusatory depression" twice a month.
  • The First Law of Kipple: "Kipple driven out non-kipple... a universal principle," borrowed from Gresham's law; the whole universe trends toward "total, absolute kippleization."
  • Sidney's Animal & Fowl Catalogue: italics mean the species is extinct or the listing is guidance only — "Sidney's never makes a mistake. What else can we depend on?"
  • Voigt-Kampff stimuli: calfskin wallet, raw oysters vs. boiled dog stuffed with rice, a wasp crawling on a wrist, a nude girl on a bearskin rug, an abortion scenario, bullfight posters — all keyed to involuntary reactions to animal suffering.
  • Mercerism vocabulary: the two handles of the empathy box, the ascent, the tomb world, the killers/old antagonists; "You shall kill only the killers"; Mercer's boyhood with foster parents Frank and Cora Mercer and his lost time-reversal nodule bombarded with radioactive cobalt.
  • Buster Friendly's expose: research chief Earl Parameter; former special-effects man Wade Cortot; and retired bit player Al Jarry of Lark Avenue, East Harmony, Indiana; "the 'rocks' did consist of rubber-like plastic" and the "blood" was catsup. Buster later revealed (by Pris) to be an android.
  • The Munch works: the screaming isolate (as in The Scream) and Puberty (1894), the print Rick buys Luba Luft and then burns to ash.
  • Phil Resch: bounty hunter of the Mission Street station; owns a real squirrel named Buffy in a wheel; his verdict on hunting: "Go to bed with her first, and then kill her."
  • Roy Baty: pharmacist cover on Mars, group-escapist, drug experimenter ("hoping to promote in androids a group experience similar to that of Mercerism"); his alarm device registers any extra "mentational entity" and radiates a Penfield panic mood; "the spider... maybe the last spider on Earth."

  • The toad: Sidney's lists Bufonidae "E" (extinct); the electric mock has its control panel in the abdomen; Iran orders "one pound of artificial flies that really fly around and buzz" plus a perpetually renewing puddle, and the clerk recommends a periodic tongue adjustment.
