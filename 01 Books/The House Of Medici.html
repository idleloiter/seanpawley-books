The House Of Medici

Buy on Amazon — The House Of Medici

Detailed overview

Christopher Hibbert writes the Medici as a family history that is also a history of Florence's changing machinery of power. The opening image is not a triumphal portrait but Cosimo de' Medici shut in the Alberghettino, the cramped cell in the bell-tower of the Palazzo della Signoria, looking down over the city that will soon be ruled through his money, his friends, his silences, and his command of offices whose names still sound republican. Hibbert first gives the city its material shape: the Ponte Vecchio lined with butchers' shops, the Orsanmichele granary where bankers set out green cloth-covered tables, the Mercato Vecchio crowded with drapers, fishmongers, candle-makers, prostitutes, town-criers, pigs, geese, and beggars. The book's early chapters make the Medici intelligible only after the reader has understood guild politics, the borse kept at Santa Croce, the Signoria, the Gonfaloniere, the Balìa, and the social exclusion of the ciompi from the guild order.

The first family story is built around Giovanni di Bicci's bank, Cosimo's cautious wealth, and the fight with the Albizzi. Hibbert moves from legendary origins in the Mugello and the palle on the Medici arms to the hard political consequences of the Lucca war, Rinaldo degli Albizzi, Francesco Sforza, Niccolò Piccinino, and the failed Serchio diversion scheme. Cosimo survives imprisonment, exile at Ferrara and Padua, and the Albizzi attempt to strip him of his fortune; when he returns to Florence, his control rests less on office than on the ability to banish enemies, place friends, manage loans, and make foreign ambassadors pass through the Via Larga palace before they deal with the republic's official magistrates.

Hibbert pairs political domination with patronage rather than treating art as a decorative sidebar. The General Council of 1439, Pope Eugenius IV, the Greek delegation, Marsilio Ficino, San Marco, San Lorenzo, Michelozzo, Brunelleschi, Ghiberti, Donatello, Fra Filippo Lippi, and Fra Angelico appear as part of the same system that makes Cosimo Pater Patriae. The book is attentive to the awkward human material behind the masterpieces: Donatello smashing a bronze head after a Genoese merchant haggles over its price, Lippi escaping from a locked room in the Medici Palace by cutting his bedclothes into strips, Cosimo choosing the Adoration of the Magi for his cell at San Marco as a private image of rulers laying down crowns.

The middle of the book turns the Medici inheritance into a test of succession. Piero il Gottoso has method, pain, Lucrezia Tornabuoni, and children, but he also faces Luca Pitti, Agnolo Acciaiuoli, Dietisalvi Neroni, Niccolò Soderini, and the factional split between the Party of the Hill and the Party of the Plain. Lorenzo then becomes the family's most brilliant political performer: ugly, energetic, educated by Gentile Becchi, Cristoforo Landino, and Marsilio Ficino, married to Clarice Orsini, almost killed in the Pazzi conspiracy in Santa Maria del Fiore while Giuliano is stabbed to death, and later forced to travel to Naples to deal directly with King Ferrante after Sixtus IV and Naples press Florence toward ruin.

Lorenzo's chapters show why Hibbert treats magnificence as both cultural achievement and financial danger. Lorenzo supports Poliziano, Pico della Mirandola, Botticelli, Filippino Lippi, Ghirlandaio, Verrocchio, Leonardo, Michelangelo, Giovanni Lascaris, the University of Pisa, and Greek teaching in Florence, but the bank that had sustained Cosimo's politics decays under him. Hibbert lingers over Lorenzo's Tuscan poetry, his country images of flocks, cranes, falcons, olive groves, the Ombrone in flood, and peasants fleeing with cattle, then ends the part at Careggi with lightning striking the Cathedral lantern, Piero Leoni's failed medicine, Pico's visit, and Lorenzo buried beside Giuliano in San Lorenzo.

After 1492 the book becomes a history of rupture. Piero di Lorenzo lacks his father's tact, Savonarola turns San Marco and the Cathedral into a pulpit for visions of the Sword of the Lord, Charles VIII crosses the Alps with banners marked Voluntas Dei, and Florence expels the Medici after Piero surrenders Tuscan fortresses. Hibbert follows the republic through Savonarola's excommunication and execution, the sack of Prato that enables the Medici return, Giovanni de' Medici's transformation into Leo X, Giulio de' Medici's papacy as Clement VII, the Sack of Rome, the siege of Florence, Francesco Ferrucci's death at Gavinana, and the violent installation of Alessandro de' Medici before Lorenzaccio kills him in an Epiphany trap.

The final part replaces the banking family with a dynastic state. Cosimo I, son of Giovanni delle Bande Nere and Maria Salviati, defeats the Fuorusciti at Montemurlo, marries Eleonora of Toledo, removes Spanish garrisons, suppresses old magistracies, takes Siena, pursues the grand-ducal title from Pius V, builds naval power through Elba, Cosmopolis, Leghorn, the Knights of Santo Stefano, La Saetta, La Pisana, and San Giovanni, and makes Florence more secure and less free. Hibbert then follows the hereditary decline through Francesco, Ferdinando, Cosimo II, Ferdinando II, Marguerite Louise d'Orléans, Cosimo III, Grand Prince Ferdinando, Gian Gastone, the Ruspanti, Austrian troops, and finally Anna Maria Luisa, whose will keeps the Medici collections in Florence for the public benefit.

Section-by-section notes

Part One · Il Quattrocento

Summary: Hibbert begins Part One with Cosimo de' Medici being summoned to the Palazzo della Signoria in September 1433 and locked in the Alberghettino, then widens the view to a Florence of more than 50,000 people, four quartieri, the Ponte Vecchio, Orsanmichele, the Mercato Vecchio, Santa Croce, and the guild system that controls public office. The section explains how the Arte della Lana, Arte di Calimala, Arte del Cambio, Priori, Gonfaloniere, borse, Balìa, and ciompi revolt form the civic world in which Giovanni di Bicci and Cosimo rise. It then traces the Medici from the Mugello legends and Salvestro de' Medici through Giovanni di Bicci's papal banking, Cosimo's Roman management of the bank, his slave Maddalena, his son Carlo, and his marriage to Contessina de' Bardi. The struggle with Rinaldo degli Albizzi turns on the Lucca war, Francesco Sforza, Niccolò Piccinino, the failed Serchio scheme, Cosimo's arrest, exile in Ferrara and Padua, and his return in 1434 as a ruler who avoids princely display while packing offices and banishing enemies. The part then links power to the Council of Florence of 1439, Pope Eugenius IV, the Greek and Latin churches, Marsilio Ficino, San Marco, San Lorenzo, Michelozzo's Medici Palace, Careggi, Fiesole, Donatello's David, Fra Filippo Lippi's escape from the palace, Fra Angelico's Crucifixion, and Cosimo's death in 1464 as Pater Patriae. Source anchors: Alberghettino, Ponte Vecchio, Arte del Cambio, ciompi, Rinaldo degli Albizzi, Serchio, San Marco, Pater Patriae.

Analysis: Part One makes Cosimo's rule depend on Florence's own institutions rather than on a simple seizure of office. The borse, Signoria, Gonfaloniere, Balìa, and guild hierarchy make Informal Institutions the mechanism of Medici rule: a family outside formal monarchy can still decide who governs, who is banished, and which wars Florence will fight. Rinaldo degli Albizzi, the Lucca campaign, Francesco Sforza, and the Serchio failure give Cosimo a practical opening: he looks prudent where the Albizzi look costly, and his exile at Ferrara and Padua turns into proof that money and credit can survive a sentence from the Palazzo della Signoria. San Marco, San Lorenzo, Michelozzo's palace, Donatello, Lippi, and Fra Angelico then show the second mechanism of Cosimo's control, because buildings, libraries, friars, artists, and humanists make Medici authority visible in churches and streets without forcing Cosimo to call himself a prince.

Part Two · 1464-1492

Summary: Part Two opens with Piero il Gottoso, a forty-eight-year-old heir weakened by gout, arthritis, and eczema but supported by Lucrezia Tornabuoni, Lorenzo, Giuliano, Maria, Bianca, Nannina, the Tornabuoni connection, and a record of embassies to Milan, Venice, Paris, and the Gonfaloniere's office in 1461. Hibbert follows the challenge from Luca Pitti, Agnolo Acciaiuoli, Dietisalvi Neroni, Niccolò Soderini, the Party of the Hill, the Party of the Plain, Francesco Sforza's death in 1466, Giovanni Bentivoglio's warning, Lorenzo's ride from Careggi, and Bartolommeo Colleoni's Venetian expedition. The section then turns to Lorenzo as a young ruler shaped by Gentile Becchi, Cristoforo Landino, Marsilio Ficino, Clarice Orsini, tournaments, Calendimaggio, St John the Baptist's festival, Volterra's revolt, Sixtus IV, Girolamo Riario, and the alum business at Tolfa. Its central crisis is the Pazzi conspiracy: Francesco de' Pazzi, Jacopo de' Pazzi, Archbishop Francesco Salviati, Cardinal Raffaele Riario, Santa Maria del Fiore, Giuliano's murder, Lorenzo's wound, Poliziano's intervention, and the executions in the Piazza della Signoria. After war with the Pope and Naples, Lorenzo travels to King Ferrante at Naples, steadies Florence, and returns as a ruler whose cultural world includes Poliziano, Botticelli, Filippino Lippi, Ghirlandaio, Verrocchio, Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo in the Medici household, Giovanni Lascaris collecting Greek manuscripts, the University of Pisa, and poetry in Tuscan before his death at Careggi in April 1492. Source anchors: Piero il Gottoso, Party of the Hill, Bartolommeo Colleoni, Tolfa alum, Pazzi conspiracy, King Ferrante, Giovanni Lascaris, Careggi.

Analysis: Part Two tests whether Cosimo's informal system can outlive Cosimo's body. Piero il Gottoso has enough method and family credit to survive Luca Pitti, Niccolò Soderini, and the Party of the Hill, but his debt calls and illness show how fragile Medici power becomes when banking confidence falters. Lorenzo's ride from Careggi, the Pazzi attack in Santa Maria del Fiore, and the journey to King Ferrante turn Legitimacy and Succession into personal performance: the heir must absorb danger for Florence before the city accepts him as more than Cosimo's grandson. Tolfa alum, Sixtus IV, Girolamo Riario, and Naples give the Pazzi conspiracy its institutional teeth, because the murder plot is tied to papal finance, ecclesiastical appointments, and Italian alliances rather than private hatred alone. Giovanni Lascaris, the University of Pisa, Poliziano, Botticelli, Michelangelo, and Lorenzo's Tuscan poems show the costlier side of magnificence: Florence becomes the stage for scholarship and art while the Medici bank loses the disciplined commercial base that had made Giovanni di Bicci and Cosimo formidable.

Part Three · 1492-1537

Summary: Part Three begins with Piero di Lorenzo, twenty-two years old, athletic, arrogant, married to Alfonsina Orsini, hostile to his Pierfrancesco cousins Lorenzo and Giovanni, negligent toward the bank, and dependent on Piero Dovizi da Bibbiena and Giovanni Tornabuoni while Girolamo Savonarola preaches from San Marco and the Cathedral. Savonarola's visions of the Sword of the Lord, the Cross of God's Anger, plague, flood, famine, foreign armies, and a Venetian-style constitution meet Charles VIII's invasion after Lodovico Sforza encourages a French claim to Naples, King Ferrante dies in 1494, the French sack Fivizzano, and Piero surrenders fortresses at Sarzana, Sarzanello, Pietrasanta, Pisa, and Leghorn. Florence expels the Medici, plunders the palace, receives Charles VIII, and then lives through Savonarola's rule, the Bonfire of Vanities, Alexander VI's pressure, excommunication, the failed ordeal by fire, and Savonarola's execution in the Piazza della Signoria with ashes thrown toward the Arno. The Medici return after Cardinal Giovanni, Giulio de' Medici, Spanish troops, and the sack of Prato in 1512, leading to Giovanni's election as Leo X, his entry into Florence, Hanno the elephant, Raphael, Michelangelo, Giuliano Duke of Nemours, Lorenzo Duke of Urbino, and the family's renewed domination under papal cover. The later chapters follow Giulio as Clement VII through Francis I, Charles V, the Sack of Rome, Castel Sant' Angelo, Henry VIII's divorce, Florence's second anti-Medici revolt, Michelangelo's fortifications, the 1529-1530 siege, Francesco Ferrucci at Gavinana, Malatesta Baglioni's secret dealings, Guicciardini's revenge, Alessandro de' Medici's dukedom, Caterina de' Medici's marriage to Henri de Valois at Marseilles, Clement's death, Ippolito's death at Itri, and Lorenzaccio's murder of Alessandro with Scoroncolo on Epiphany night before Cosimo I is called from Il Trebbio. Source anchors: Savonarola, Charles VIII, Fivizzano, Bonfire of Vanities, Prato, Leo X, Sack of Rome, Lorenzaccio.

Analysis: Part Three shows the Medici losing and regaining Florence through foreign armies, prophetic religion, and papal office rather than through the old banking network. Piero di Lorenzo's surrender of Sarzana, Sarzanello, Pietrasanta, Pisa, and Leghorn makes Savonarola's Sword of the Lord appear politically real, and Charles VIII turns a Florentine family crisis into an Italian crisis tied to Naples and Milan. The Bonfire of Vanities, Alexander VI's excommunication, the ordeal by fire, and the execution in the Piazza della Signoria make Religion and Legitimacy volatile: Savonarola can mobilize the city through fear and reform, but he cannot keep control once papal pressure and Florentine factionalism close in. Prato, Leo X, Clement VII, the Sack of Rome, and the siege of Florence mark each Medici recovery as dependent on violence outside the city walls; Guicciardini's punishments after Gavinana and Alessandro's Fortezza da Basso make the restored regime more openly coercive than Cosimo Pater Patriae's rule. Lorenzaccio, Scoroncolo, Cardinal Cibò, Vitelli, and the young Cosimo I turn Alessandro's murder into the hinge between unstable Medici papal clients and a durable ducal state.

Part Four · 1537-1743

Summary: Part Four opens with Cosimo I, born in the Palazzo Salviati to Maria Salviati and Giovanni delle Bande Nere, named by Leo X, moved through Venice, Bologna, Il Trebbio, Genoa, Florence, and Naples, then unexpectedly chosen after Alessandro's murder while Guicciardini, Cardinal Cibò, Alessandro Vitelli, and the Palleschi imagine they can manage him. Cosimo defeats the Fuorusciti at Montemurlo in 1537, punishes Piero Strozzi's supporters, marries Eleonora of Toledo, removes Spanish garrisons, sidelines Guicciardini and Vettori, expels the Dominicans from San Marco, conquers Siena between 1554 and 1557, gains the grand-ducal title from Pius V in 1569, builds a navy through Elba, Cosmopolis, La Saetta, La Pisana, San Giovanni, Leghorn, and the Knights of Santo Stefano, and takes public credit after Lepanto. Hibbert then follows the heirs through Francesco, Bianca Cappello, Ferdinando I, Christina of Lorraine, Galileo's Florence, Maria de' Medici's French marriage, Ferdinando II, Marguerite Louise d'Orléans, Cosimo III, Grand Prince Ferdinando, Anna Maria Francesca of Saxe-Lauenburg, Gian Gastone, Giuliano Dami, and the unhappy succession diplomacy involving Charles VI, the House of Este, Philip V of Spain, and Elizabeth Farnese. The last chapters describe Cosimo III's relics, St Francis Xavier's intestines, the Volto Santo, anti-Jewish and censorious piety, Florence's poverty, beggars, monks, income tax, Gian Gastone's early reforms, the Ruspanti, the Pitti Palace bedroom, Austrian troops, Francis of Lorraine's succession, and Anna Maria Luisa's final years. The Medici line ends with Anna Maria Luisa's death in February 1743 and her bequest of palaces, villas, pictures, statues, jewels, furniture, books, and manuscripts to the new Grand Duke on condition that the collections remain in Florence for the pleasure and benefit of the public. Source anchors: Montemurlo, Eleonora of Toledo, Siena, Knights of Santo Stefano, Lepanto, Marguerite Louise, Ruspanti, Anna Maria Luisa.

Analysis: Part Four changes the family from bankers who dominate republican forms into sovereigns who command fortresses, armies, taxes, titles, and marriage diplomacy. Montemurlo, Eleonora of Toledo, the Spanish garrisons, Siena, Pius V, Elba, the Knights of Santo Stefano, and Lepanto define Cosimo I's rule as State Capacity built by coercion and administration: he removes rival patrons, controls magistracies, adds territory, and makes Tuscany matter at sea. The later heirs expose the weakness of hereditary rule when the ruler's household, marriage, and body become public problems; Marguerite Louise's contempt for Florence, Cosimo III's relic-filled piety, Gian Gastone's dependence on Giuliano Dami, and the Ruspanti show a dynasty that can no longer produce either political energy or credible heirs. Anna Maria Luisa's bequest turns Knowledge Preservation into the book's closing reversal: after Montemurlo, Siena, the Fortezza da Basso, and the Austrian succession have stripped Florentines of old political choice, the last Medici preserves the family's most visible achievement by tying the collections permanently to Florence rather than to the next ruling house.
